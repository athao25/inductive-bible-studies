Lesson 0054 · Unit 8: Chapters 18-21

John 19:17-30: It Is Finished

~30 min · Passage: John 19:17-30 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 19:17-30 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write down at least five observations: the wording of the title and its languages, what happens to the garments and which psalm is quoted, what he says to his mother, the word about thirst and the Scripture behind it, and his last word before he dies.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • He carries his own cross (v. 17). John records no Simon of Cyrene; his emphasis throughout is on Jesus acting rather than being acted upon.
  • Between two others (v. 18). "And with him two others, one on either side, and Jesus between them." Numbered with transgressors (Isa 53:12).
  • The title, in three languages (vv. 19-20). "Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews", in Aramaic, Latin, and Greek: the language of religion, of government, and of culture. Everyone could read it.
  • Pilate refuses to change it (vv. 21-22). "What I have written I have written." The one immovable thing he does all day is proclaim the truth accidentally.
  • The garments divided, the tunic left whole (vv. 23-24). Four soldiers, four parts, and a seamless tunic that would be ruined by tearing, so they cast lots. John quotes Psalm 22:18 exactly.
  • Psalm 22 stands behind the whole scene. Pierced hands and feet, mockery, casting lots, thirst, and a cry that begins in abandonment and ends in worldwide praise.
  • Four women, one disciple (vv. 25-26). The named women at the cross outnumber the men.
  • He provides for his mother (vv. 26-27). "Woman, behold, your son!" and "Behold, your mother!" Dying, he arranges her care, and John takes her home that day.
  • "I thirst" (v. 28). "Knowing that all was now finished, said (to fulfill the Scripture), 'I thirst.'" Real physical suffering, and a deliberate fulfilling of Psalm 69:21.
  • Sour wine on hyssop (v. 29). Hyssop was the branch used to apply the Passover blood to the doorposts (Ex 12:22). John chooses the detail on purpose.
  • "It is finished" (v. 30). One word in Greek, tetelestai, a perfect tense: completed, with the results standing.
  • He gives up his spirit (v. 30). "He bowed his head and gave up his spirit." Not "expired"; the verb is active, matching 10:18.

Interpretation

What was finished?

When Jesus had received the sour wine, he said, "It is finished," and he bowed his head and gave up his spirit. John 19:30, ESV

The single word tetelestai was used in ordinary life for a task completed, a debt settled, and a bill marked paid in full. That is the sense here, and the Gospel has been building toward it: "my food is to do the will of him who sent me and to accomplish his work" (4:34); "I glorified you on earth, having accomplished the work that you gave me to do" (17:4). What is finished? The work the Father gave him, the obedience that Adam and Israel failed to render, the sacrificial system that pointed forward for fifteen centuries, and the payment for the sins of his people. Notice the tense: not "I am finished", the cry of a man at the end of his strength, but "it is finished", the announcement of a completed transaction. That single word is the ground of Christian assurance, and the reason nothing may be added to it. Every attempt to supplement the cross with your performance is an attempt to add to a bill already stamped paid. And notice the order in verse 30: he speaks the word first and then gives up his spirit. He does not die and thereby finish; he finishes and then dies, deliberately, which is exactly the authority he claimed in 10:18.

Why do the small details matter?

John is an eyewitness building a case, and the details are evidence. The seamless tunic is the kind of thing only someone present would notice, and it fulfills Psalm 22:18 in a way no one could have arranged; the soldiers were not consulting a scroll. The hyssop is a deliberate Passover echo, put in the hand of the executioner and reported by a man standing close enough to see the plant. The title in three languages announces the truth to the whole world in the moment it looks most false. And the provision for Mary is the detail that stops the scene from becoming an abstraction: in the middle of bearing the sins of the world, he notices his mother has no one to care for her. Some read the seamless tunic as an echo of the high priest's robe, which is possible and secondary; the primary point is the psalm. Two takeaways. First, prophecy fulfilled in details outside anyone's control is a real argument, and John expects you to weigh it (compare 19:35). Second, the cross is not only cosmic. The same event that satisfies divine justice also arranges housing for a widow, and both are in the same paragraph.

Application

  • About God He finished the work. Christianity is not an offer of assistance toward a goal you must reach; it is an announcement about something already completed.
  • About you If you are trying to add to the cross by penance, performance, or self-punishment, verse 30 says the bill is already stamped. Live from that rather than toward it.
  • Do Read Psalm 22 in one sitting this week alongside John 19. Mark every line that appears in the crucifixion account, and note that the psalm ends in worldwide praise rather than despair.

Primary source
In your MacArthur Study Bible (Olive Tree), read the notes on 19:23-24 (the garments and Psalm 22), 19:28-29 (thirst and hyssop), and 19:30 (it is finished). Five minutes. Ask one question: what does the note say tetelestai meant in ordinary usage?
Church prompt
Ask someone at church what "it is finished" means to them practically. Listen for whether they describe something completed or something still being attempted. That difference is the whole gospel.

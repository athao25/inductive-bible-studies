John 12:1-19: The Anointing and the Entry

~25 min · Passage: John 12:1-19 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 12:1-19 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write down at least five observations: what Mary does and what it was worth, what Judas says and what John tells you about him, how Jesus defends her, why the crowd comes out, what they shout, and which prophecy John quotes.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Six days before Passover (v. 1). The countdown is explicit now, and the location is Bethany, where Lazarus is alive at the table.
  • Three people, three postures (vv. 2-3). Martha served, Lazarus reclined, Mary anointed. John lets each be themselves.
  • The extravagance is quantified (v. 3). "A pound of expensive ointment made from pure nard." Verse 5 prices it at three hundred denarii, roughly a year's wages.
  • Feet, and hair (v. 3). Anointing the head was normal for a guest of honor; feet were the servant's territory, and a woman letting down her hair in public was scandalous. She does not care.
  • The house is filled with the fragrance (v. 3). A detail only an eyewitness records, and a picture of what such love does to a room.
  • Judas objects on ethical grounds (vv. 4-5). "Why was this ointment not sold for three hundred denarii and given to the poor?" The complaint sounds noble.
  • John removes the mask (v. 6). "He said this, not because he cared about the poor, but because he was a thief, and having charge of the moneybag he used to help himself to what was put into it."
  • Jesus defends her and interprets the act (vv. 7-8). "Leave her alone, so that she may keep it for the day of my burial." She is the only person in the Gospels who seems to have grasped that he was going to die.
  • Lazarus is a target too (vv. 9-11). "The chief priests made plans to put Lazarus to death as well, because on account of him many of the Jews were going away and believing." Killing the evidence.
  • Palm branches and a political chant (vv. 12-13). "Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord, even the King of Israel!" Palms were a nationalist symbol; the crowd wants a liberator.
  • He answers the chant with a donkey (vv. 14-15). Zechariah 9:9: "Fear not, daughter of Zion; behold, your king is coming, sitting on a donkey's colt." A deliberate correction of what kind of king he is.
  • Understanding came later (v. 16). "His disciples did not understand these things at first, but when Jesus was glorified, then they remembered." The same pattern as 2:22.
  • The crowd is there because of Lazarus (vv. 17-18). The seventh sign is driving the parade.
  • The Pharisees' despairing line (v. 19). "Look, the world has gone after him." Unintentionally prophetic, and it hands directly into the Greeks arriving in the next paragraph.

Interpretation

What makes Mary's act right and Judas' objection wrong?

Jesus said, "Leave her alone, so that she may keep it for the day of my burial. For the poor you always have with you, but you do not always have me." John 12:7-8, ESV

On paper Judas has the better argument. A year's wages could have fed many people; pouring it on someone's feet is, by any accounting, waste. That is exactly why John tells you what was behind the question: a thief building a case for a fuller moneybag. Two lessons follow. First, the highest-sounding objection can come from the lowest motive, and you cannot always tell from the sentence itself. Second, and more importantly, Jesus does not defend Mary by arguing economics. He says the act was for his burial and that his presence is a passing opportunity. There are moments where extravagance toward Christ is precisely the right response, and a Christianity that can only think in terms of utility has lost something. Verse 8 is not indifference toward the poor either, since it quotes Deuteronomy 15:11, where the very next line commands open-handed generosity. It is a statement about this unrepeatable week. Notice too what Mary understood that the twelve did not. Jesus had said plainly and repeatedly that he would die. The men argued about greatness; the woman who sat at his feet listening (Luke 10:39) came with burial spices while he was still alive. Listening produces perception.

Why the donkey?

The crowd greets him with palm branches, which had been the symbol of Jewish national victory since the Maccabees, and with a chant from Psalm 118 plus the added title "the King of Israel". They want an insurrection. His response is not to refuse the acclamation but to redefine it in the most public way available: he sits on a young donkey, deliberately enacting Zechariah 9:9. Read the rest of that Zechariah verse and the following one and the point sharpens: the king comes "righteous and having salvation, humble", and "he shall speak peace to the nations", cutting off the war horse and the battle bow. So the entry is simultaneously an acceptance of the title king and a rejection of the crowd's definition of it. Within five days most of that same city will want him crucified, which is what happens when a crowd's expectations are disappointed. This is also a good place to notice how John handles the disciples' slowness. Verse 16 says they did not understand until after the glorification, when they remembered and the Scripture snapped into place. Much of what you read today may work that way too. Understanding often arrives later, from the far side of an experience, which is a reason to keep reading rather than to wait for clarity before you do.

Application

  • About God He receives extravagant love and defends the person giving it against reasonable criticism. He also refuses to be crowned on anyone else's terms.
  • About you Check the motive underneath your most principled objections, especially when the principle happens to protect your interest. Judas' question was about ethics and was actually about money.
  • Do Do one costly, non-strategic thing for Christ this week: give beyond what is comfortable, spend an hour in worship that produces nothing, serve where no one will know. Let it be inefficient on purpose.

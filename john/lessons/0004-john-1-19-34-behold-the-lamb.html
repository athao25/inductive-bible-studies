John 1:19-34: Behold the Lamb

~25 min · Passage: John 1:19-34 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 1:19-34 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write down at least five observations: who sends the delegation, what they ask, what the Baptist denies, what he finally claims from Isaiah, and what he says when he sees Jesus. Come back with your list in front of you.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The narrative opens as a deposition (v. 19). "And this is the testimony of John, when the Jews sent priests and Levites from Jerusalem to ask him, 'Who are you?'" The prologue announced a witness (1:7); here is his testimony, under official questioning.
  • Three denials before any claim (vv. 20-21). Not the Christ; not Elijah; not the Prophet (the one promised in Deut 18:15). The text underlines the first: "He confessed, and did not deny, but confessed".
  • His self-description is only a voice (v. 23). From Isaiah 40:3: "I am the voice of one crying out in the wilderness, 'Make straight the way of the Lord.'" Not the Lord; the road crew.
  • He points away from himself twice (vv. 26-27). "Among you stands one you do not know"; "the strap of whose sandal I am not worthy to untie". Untying sandals was slave work, and he claims to rank beneath it.
  • The title appears without warning (v. 29). "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" No explanation is given; the Baptist assumes his hearers know what a lamb is for.
  • Preexistence in one line (v. 30). "After me comes a man who ranks before me, because he was before me." John was born first (Luke 1); yet Jesus "was before" him. The prologue's eternity claim, now on a human witness's lips.
  • "I myself did not know him", twice (vv. 31, 33). The Baptist disclaims private insight; he knew Jesus only by the sign God gave him.
  • The Spirit "remained" (vv. 32-33). Descended "like a dove" and remained: not a passing visitation, as with the judges and prophets, but an abiding anointing. The one so marked "baptizes with the Holy Spirit".
  • The testimony lands on a title (v. 34). "I have seen and have borne witness that this is the Son of God." The paragraph that began "who are you?" ends by answering a different question: who he is.

Interpretation

Why does the witness lead with denials?

Because a witness who might be confused with the light is a failed witness. The prologue already warned us, "he was not the light, but came to bear witness about the light" (1:8), and the Baptist's whole vocation depends on that distinction. First-century expectation was electric; Luke tells us people wondered outright whether John might be the Christ (Luke 3:15). So John clears the ground: not the Christ, not Elijah returned in person, not the Prophet of Deuteronomy 18. He will not even accept a title, only a function: a voice, preparing a road. This is the shape of all true ministry, and John will state it as a maxim in two chapters: "He must increase, but I must decrease" (3:30). Watch how this Gospel keeps the pattern: every witness it calls, from the Baptist to the Samaritan woman to the man born blind, succeeds precisely by getting out of the way.

One honest wrinkle: Jesus later says of the Baptist, "he is Elijah who is to come" (Matt 11:14), while John here says "I am not" Elijah. No contradiction once you see what each is answering. The questioners wanted to know if he was Elijah himself, back from the sky (2 Kings 2:11); he was not. Jesus is saying John fulfilled the role Malachi promised, going "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Mal 4:5, Luke 1:17). Same man, two senses of "Elijah"; the angel had already split the difference before John was born.

What does "Lamb of God" mean?

The next day he saw Jesus coming toward him, and said, "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" John 1:29, ESV

No single Old Testament lamb exhausts the phrase; the Baptist gathers a whole storyline into two words. The Passover lamb, whose blood on the doorposts turned away death (Ex 12:13), and John will note at the cross that not one of his bones was broken, exactly as the Passover law required (19:36, Ex 12:46). The ram God provided in place of Isaac, answering Abraham's "God will provide for himself the lamb" (Gen 22:8). The daily offerings, morning and evening, a lamb each (Ex 29:38-39). And the Servant of Isaiah 53, "like a lamb that is led to the slaughter", on whom "the LORD has laid... the iniquity of us all" (Isa 53:6-7). Notice the direction of the phrase "of God": this is the lamb God supplies, not one we bring. And notice the scope: "the sin of the world", not of Israel only. The prologue said his own received him not while the world was made through him; the Lamb's atonement will reach as wide as the unbelieving world from which believers are drawn, Samaritans and Greeks included, as this Gospel will show in person.

Why does it matter that the Spirit "remained on him"?

In the Old Testament the Spirit came upon judges, kings, and prophets for tasks, and could depart (ask Samson, or Saul). Isaiah promised something different for the Messiah: "the Spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him" (Isa 11:2), and for the Servant, "I have put my Spirit upon him" (Isa 42:1). The descending-and-remaining dove is God's own identification sign, arranged in advance with the Baptist ("he who sent me... said to me", v. 33): the one with the abiding Spirit is the anointed one, the Christ. And his ministry is defined by giving what he bears: "this is he who baptizes with the Holy Spirit". Keep this thread; it surfaces at 7:39 ("the Spirit had not been given, because Jesus was not yet glorified"), fills the upper room (ch. 14-16), and breathes on the disciples at 20:22.

Application

  • About God He provides the Lamb. Every other religion tells you what to bring; the gospel announces what God has brought. "Of God" is the whole difference between religion and grace.
  • About you The Baptist's greatness lay in his subtraction: not the light, only a voice; unworthy of the sandal. Ask where you are tempted to be the light rather than the witness, at work, at church, at home, and what "he must increase" would look like there this week.
  • Do Say aloud, from memory, the two words the Baptist would want you to keep: "Behold, the Lamb." Then read Isaiah 53:4-7 once, slowly, as commentary on John 1:29. Five minutes.

John 12:20-36: The Grain of Wheat

~25 min · Passage: John 12:20-36 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 12:20-36 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write down at least five observations: who asks to see him and what he answers, what the grain of wheat teaches, what he says about his soul being troubled, what the voice says and how the crowd hears it, and what the lifting up accomplishes.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Greeks come looking (vv. 20-22). God-fearing Gentiles at the feast ask Philip, "Sir, we wish to see Jesus." The Pharisees just said "the world has gone after him", and here the world arrives.
  • The hour finally comes (v. 23). "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified." Since 2:4 it has been "not yet"; the arrival of the nations triggers the announcement.
  • Glorified means crucified (v. 24). The next sentence is about a seed dying. In John these are the same event viewed from two sides.
  • The seed principle (v. 24). "Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit." Alone, or fruitful through death.
  • Applied to disciples (vv. 25-26). "Whoever loves his life loses it, and whoever hates his life in this world will keep it for eternal life." Then: serving means following, and the servant ends where the master is.
  • The Father will honor the servant (v. 26). A striking promise, easy to skip past.
  • Real distress (v. 27). "Now is my soul troubled. And what shall I say? 'Father, save me from this hour'? But for this purpose I have come to this hour." John's version of Gethsemane, compressed.
  • His prayer is for the Father's glory (v. 28). "Father, glorify your name."
  • The third heavenly voice in the Gospels (v. 28). "I have glorified it, and I will glorify it again."
  • The crowd cannot agree what it heard (v. 29). Thunder, or an angel. Revelation is not self-interpreting to a resistant ear.
  • The voice was for them (v. 30). "This voice has come for your sake, not mine."
  • Two verdicts announced (v. 31). "Now is the judgment of this world; now will the ruler of this world be cast out." The cross is where Satan is defeated, not where he wins.
  • The drawing promise (v. 32). "And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself." John adds in verse 33 that this indicated the kind of death he was to die.
  • The crowd's objection (v. 34). "We have heard from the Law that the Christ remains forever. How can you say that the Son of Man must be lifted up?"
  • The last public words (vv. 35-36). "The light is among you for a little while longer. Walk while you have the light... While you have the light, believe in the light." Then he departed and hid himself.

Interpretation

What does the grain of wheat teach?

Truly, truly, I say to you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit. John 12:24, ESV

The picture answers the Greeks' request in a way they did not expect. They wanted to see Jesus; he tells them how they will actually get him, which is through his death. A seed kept safe on a shelf stays exactly one seed forever. Its only route to multiplication runs through burial. That is first a statement about Christ: his death is not the ruin of his mission but the mechanism of it, and the "much fruit" is a harvest of people from every nation, which is why the Greeks' arrival triggers the announcement. Then he turns it on his followers in verse 25, using the sharpest possible language: loving your life loses it, and hating your life in this world keeps it. "Hate" here is the Semitic idiom of decisive preference, as in Luke 14:26, not psychological self-loathing. The principle is simply that self-preservation is self-loss. Every attempt to keep your life intact, unrisked, and comfortable is the seed staying on the shelf. It is worth applying this in specifics rather than in general: which comfort, which reputation, which plan is currently unplanted?

Does "draw all people" mean everyone is saved?

And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself. John 12:32, ESV

Three considerations answer this. First, context: the paragraph begins with Gentiles arriving, and the whole point is that the cross breaks the boundary that had kept salvation in one nation. "All people" means all kinds of people, Jew and Greek alike, which is exactly the sense of 11:52, "to gather into one the children of God who are scattered abroad", and of 10:16, "other sheep that are not of this fold". Second, vocabulary: the verb "draw" is the same one used in 6:44, where everyone drawn is raised on the last day. If it meant every individual without exception here, it would mean universal salvation there, which no reader of chapter 6 or chapter 12 can maintain, since verses 37 to 40 of this very chapter describe people who could not believe. Third, the Gospel's own summary: the drawing is effective, and the ones drawn come. So the verse is not a promise that everyone will be saved; it is a promise that the cross will gather a people from everywhere, and that not one of them will fail to arrive. Notice as well what the cross does to the enemy in verse 31. The world's judgment falls there, and "the ruler of this world" is cast out. Calvary looks like defeat and is in fact the decisive eviction.

Application

  • About God He was troubled in soul and did not ask to be spared, because the hour was the purpose. The cross is not something that happened to him; it is what he came for.
  • About you The servant is promised the Father's honor (v. 26). Whatever following him costs you, that promise is the compensation, and it comes from the highest possible source.
  • Do Name one specific thing you have been protecting: an ambition, a relationship on your terms, a level of comfort. Plant it this week by taking one concrete step that risks it for Christ's sake.

John 6:60-71: To Whom Shall We Go

~25 min · Passage: John 6:60-71 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 6:60-71 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write down at least five observations: who complains and what word they use for the teaching, what verse 63 says about flesh and words, what verse 65 repeats from earlier, how many walk away, what Peter answers, and what John says about Judas.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The complainers are disciples (v. 60). "Many of his disciples, when they heard it, said, 'This is a hard saying; who can listen to it?'" Not the hostile crowd, but followers.
  • "Hard" means unacceptable, not unclear (v. 60). They understood him well enough to be offended. Difficulty of comprehension is not the issue.
  • He knew without being told (v. 61). "But Jesus, knowing in himself that his disciples were grumbling." The narrator keeps showing his knowledge (2:24-25; 6:64).
  • The ascension question (v. 62). "Then what if you were to see the Son of Man ascending to where he was before?" If the incarnation offends you, the exaltation will finish you. It also quietly claims preexistence.
  • The interpretive key (v. 63). "It is the Spirit who gives life; the flesh is no help at all. The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life." A crude, literal, physical eating was never the point.
  • He knew from the beginning (v. 64). "For Jesus knew from the beginning who those were who did not believe, and who it was who would betray him."
  • Verse 65 repeats verse 44. "This is why I told you that no one can come to me unless it is granted him by the Father." The chapter's doctrine, stated once more at the moment of desertion.
  • Mass departure (v. 66). "After this many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him." The largest exodus from Jesus recorded in the Gospels.
  • The question to the twelve (v. 67). "Do you want to go away as well?" The Greek expects the answer no, but it is a real question all the same.
  • Peter answers with nowhere else (v. 68). "Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life." He picks up the very phrase from verse 63.
  • And with a confession (v. 69). "And we have believed, and have come to know, that you are the Holy One of God." Believing and knowing together, in that order.
  • Jesus does not accept the "we" (v. 70). "Did I not choose you, the twelve? And yet one of you is a devil."
  • John names him for the reader (v. 71). "He spoke of Judas the son of Simon Iscariot, for he, one of the twelve, was going to betray him." The reader knows; the eleven do not.

Interpretation

What does verse 63 settle?

It is the Spirit who gives life; the flesh is no help at all. The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life. John 6:63, ESV

This is Jesus' own commentary on the hardest paragraph in the chapter, and it does three things at once. It rules out crude literalism: physical flesh, eaten as flesh, "is no help at all". It identifies the life-giving agent: the Spirit, exactly as in chapters 3 and 4. And it locates the life in his words, which means the eating and drinking of verses 53 to 58 happen as a person receives what Christ says about himself and his death. Notice how Peter's confession picks that up two verses later: "you have the words of eternal life". He heard verse 63 and repeated it back. That is what a listening disciple looks like. It also tells you where to expect God to work on you. Not through experiences you engineer, but through the words of Christ received by the Spirit, which is a plain argument for the ordinary means of reading, hearing preaching, and sitting under Scripture with other believers.

Why record the desertion at all?

After this many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him. John 6:66, ESV

John could have left this out. He includes it because it teaches what "disciple" does and does not guarantee, and because it completes the chapter's argument. Look at the sequence: five thousand fed, a crown offered, a doctrine preached, a crowd gone, twelve left, and one of those twelve a traitor. The numbers run steadily downward while the truth stays constant. That order should be stamped on anyone who measures ministry by attendance. Two further lessons come off this paragraph. First, on perseverance: the ones who left had been called disciples, and 1 John 2:19 later gives the apostolic verdict on such departures, "they went out from us, but they were not of us". Falling away permanently does not disprove the security of 6:39; it reveals what was there all along. Second, on assurance: Peter's answer is the model, and its logic is not "I feel certain" but "there is nowhere else to go". Faith that survives hard sayings usually survives on that sentence. And then the shadow: Jesus chose twelve, one of whom is a devil, which tells you that proximity to Christ, apostolic privilege, three years of teaching, even miracle-working, can coexist with a heart that never believed. That is not written to make you anxious. It is written to make you honest, and the following chapters will keep Judas in view as the counterexample to Peter's confession.

Application

  • About God He never trades truth for numbers. The same Christ who fed thousands preached the sermon that emptied the room, and he was not panicked by the result.
  • About you When Scripture says something you dislike, you are at a fork this passage has already mapped: the hard saying either drives you off or drives you deeper. Peter's answer is available to you.
  • Do Write Peter's sentence somewhere you will see it: "Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life." Say it the next time doubt arrives, and then read one chapter of John rather than closing the book.

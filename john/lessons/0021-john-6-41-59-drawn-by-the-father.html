Lesson 0021 · Unit 3: Chapters 5-6

John 6:41-59: Drawn by the Father

~30 min · Passage: John 6:41-59 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 6:41-59 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write down at least five observations: what the grumbling is about, exactly what verse 44 says and what it promises, what Isaiah is quoted for, how the imagery escalates from bread to flesh and blood, and how the paragraph ends.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Grumbling is exodus vocabulary (v. 41). Israel grumbled in the wilderness over bread (Ex 16:2). The crowd fed in the wilderness now grumbles about the bread from heaven, which makes them look exactly like their fathers.
  • Their objection is his ordinariness (v. 42). "Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know?" Familiarity as an argument.
  • He does not soften it (v. 43). "Do not grumble among yourselves." No adjustment of the claim.
  • Verse 44 has two halves. "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him. And I will raise him up on the last day." An inability, then a guarantee for everyone the first half describes.
  • Isaiah 54:13 quoted (v. 45). "And they will all be taught by God." Everyone who has heard and learned from the Father comes: the teaching is effective, not merely offered.
  • Seeing the Father is qualified (v. 46). "Not that anyone has seen the Father except he who is from God." The prologue's point again (1:18).
  • The claim is restated three ways (vv. 47-51). "Whoever believes has eternal life"; "I am the bread of life"; "I am the living bread that came down from heaven."
  • Your fathers died (vv. 49-50). The manna kept people alive for a day; this bread means "one may eat of it and not die."
  • The escalation (v. 51). "And the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh." Flesh, and given: this is cross language.
  • They take it literally and recoil (v. 52). "How can this man give us his flesh to eat?" The misunderstanding pattern, now at maximum offense.
  • He intensifies instead of explaining (vv. 53-56). "Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you." For Jews, drinking blood was expressly forbidden (Lev 17:10-14), so the phrase is shocking on purpose.
  • Abiding introduced (v. 56). "Whoever feeds on my flesh and drinks my blood abides in me, and I in him." The vocabulary of chapter 15 appears here first.
  • Life derived from the Father (v. 57). "As the living Father sent me, and I live because of the Father, so whoever feeds on me, he also will live because of me."
  • Location noted (v. 59). "Jesus said these things in the synagogue, as he taught at Capernaum." A public setting, not a private riddle.

Interpretation

What does "draws" mean in verse 44?

No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him. And I will raise him up on the last day. John 6:44, ESV

Three observations settle the meaning without any outside help. First, the sentence begins with an inability, "no one can", which matches 3:3 and 6:65 and rules out reading it as mere influence on people who were already able. Second, the Greek word behind "draws" (helko) is used elsewhere for dragging a net full of fish ashore (21:6, 11) and for hauling Paul and Silas into the marketplace (Acts 16:19). It is not a gentle suggestion. Third, and decisively, look at what the second half of the verse promises: everyone drawn is raised up on the last day. If drawing could fail, the promise would fail with it. Put verse 44 beside verse 37 and 6:65 and the chapter's logic is complete: the Father gives, the Father draws, all who are drawn come, and none are lost. This is what the tradition calls effectual calling. And notice how verse 45 keeps it from sounding mechanical: the drawing happens through teaching. "They will all be taught by God"; the one who "has heard and learned from the Father comes to me". God draws people by opening their ears to truth, which is why preaching and Bible study are not in tension with this doctrine but are the very instruments of it.

Doctrine checkpoint 2: is 6:53-58 about the Lord's Supper?
The language of eating flesh and drinking blood has been read three ways.
  • Directly eucharistic. Taken as teaching that one receives Christ's life by eating the consecrated elements. The problems are decisive: the Supper had not yet been instituted, the audience is a hostile synagogue crowd rather than the disciples, and verse 53 would then make participation in a rite the condition of having life at all, which no one in this crowd could have understood or done.
  • Purely metaphorical, with no sacramental reference. Eating and drinking simply mean believing. This is close to right, and verse 47 supports it: "whoever believes has eternal life" sits in the same paragraph as "whoever feeds on me" and makes the same promise.
  • Believing in the crucified Christ, later pictured at the Table (the reading this course takes). The verbs mean appropriating for yourself a Christ who gives his flesh and sheds his blood, that is, receiving him precisely as the crucified one. That is why the language is so violent: it is cross language, and a Messiah who dies was the offense the crowd could not swallow. Because the Lord's Supper pictures the same reality, the Table is a natural place to remember these words, but the passage is about faith, not about the rite. MacArthur, White, and Durbin all read it this way, and verse 63 will nail it down tomorrow: "the words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life."
Test it yourself: read verses 40, 47, and 54 in a row and notice that all three end with the identical promise of eternal life and resurrection at the last day. Whatever "feeds on me" means, it does exactly what "believes" does.

Why make it so offensive?

Jesus could have defused the confusion in one sentence. Instead he doubles the shock, adding the drinking of blood, which was ritually unthinkable. Why? Because the sifting is the point. Chapter 6 begins with thousands wanting to crown him and ends with almost everyone gone, and Jesus does the sifting himself, deliberately, by preaching a doctrine that a crowd wanting free bread and a national king cannot digest. The offense is not incidental packaging around the message; it is the message. He came to die, and to receive him is to take the crucified one as your life. Anyone who wants Jesus without a cross has to leave, and the next lesson watches them go. There is a hard mercy in this. A ministry that never says anything unpalatable produces crowds that have not decided anything. Jesus preferred twelve who stayed for the right reason over five thousand who stayed for lunch.

Application

  • About God He draws effectively and teaches personally. The reason anyone believes is not superior insight but a Father who opens ears.
  • About you "Feeding" is a daily verb, not a decision made once. Abiding in Christ (v. 56) means returning to the crucified Christ for life as regularly as you return to food.
  • Do Ask honestly: is there a doctrine in Scripture you have kept at arm's length because you find it distasteful? Name it, read the passage that teaches it, and ask God to make you able to hear rather than to grumble.

John 6:1-21: Bread and the Sea

~25 min · Passage: John 6:1-21 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?

Write down at least five observations: what Jesus asks Philip and why the narrator explains it, what Andrew finds and how he rates it, how much is left over, what the crowd tries to do, and what Jesus says to the disciples in the storm.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The only miracle in all four Gospels (vv. 1-13). The feeding of the five thousand is recorded by Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John, which tells you how central the early church considered it.
  • Passover again (v. 4). "Now the Passover, the feast of the Jews, was at hand." John's second Passover, and the timing loads the chapter: bread, exodus, manna, and a lamb are all about to be in play.
  • The test is deliberate (vv. 5-6). "He said this to test him, for he himself knew what he would do." The problem is posed to expose what Philip thinks is possible.
  • Philip does arithmetic (v. 7). "Two hundred denarii worth of bread would not be enough." Eight months of wages, and his answer is still about shortage.
  • Andrew brings a person (vv. 8-9). "There is a boy here who has five barley loaves and two fish, but what are they for so many?" Barley is the poor man's grain. He brings the inadequate thing anyway, which is more than Philip did.
  • Order and abundance (vv. 10-11). They sit down, he gives thanks, and distribution is "as much as they wanted." No rationing.
  • The leftovers are commanded (vv. 12-13). "Gather up the leftover fragments, that nothing may be lost." Twelve baskets: one apiece for the disciples who said it could not be done, and a lesson in extravagance without waste.
  • The wrong conclusion (vv. 14-15). "This is indeed the Prophet"; then they intend "to take him by force to make him king." Right title, wrong kingdom, wrong method, wrong hour.
  • He withdraws alone (v. 15). The crowd's coronation is a temptation to be escaped, not an opportunity to be managed.
  • Dark, rowing, wind (vv. 16-18). "It was now dark, and Jesus had not yet come to them. The sea became rough because a strong wind was blowing."
  • Three or four miles out (v. 19). Roughly the middle of the lake, which is where the walking begins.
  • The words are more than reassurance (v. 20). "It is I; do not be afraid." Literally, "I am; do not be afraid."
  • Immediate arrival (v. 21). "They were glad to take him into the boat, and immediately the boat was at the land to which they were going."

Interpretation

What does the feeding sign say?

Set the pieces in their Passover frame and the meaning assembles itself. Israel came out of Egypt at Passover, crossed a sea, and was fed with bread from heaven in the wilderness through a prophet named Moses. Here, at Passover, a prophet feeds a wilderness crowd with bread, then crosses a sea. The crowd sees the pattern and reaches the right verse, Deuteronomy 18:15, "the Prophet". What they get wrong is what such a prophet came to do. They want a king who guarantees lunch, which is the oldest political promise in the world. Jesus refuses the crown offered on those terms and spends the rest of the chapter explaining that the bread was a sign pointing to himself as the true manna. Two details reward a closer look. First, the test in verse 6: he already knew what he would do, so the question was for Philip's sake. God's questions are never information gathering. Second, the fragments in verse 12. The one who can create bread from nothing still says "let nothing be lost", which is a rebuke to both waste and worry, and John uses that same phrase later for people: "of all that he has given me I should lose nothing" (6:39).

Why walk on the water, and what does "It is I" mean?

But he said to them, "It is I; do not be afraid." John 6:20, ESV

The Greek is ego eimi, "I am". In many contexts that is simply "it is me", and the disciples surely heard reassurance first. But John is writing a Gospel with seven "I am" sayings in it, and he sets this one in a scene loaded with Old Testament resonance. Who treads on the waves of the sea? "He alone stretched out the heavens and trampled the waves of the sea" (Job 9:8). Whose way is through the sea? "Your way was through the sea, your path through the great waters" (Ps 77:19). Who says "Fear not, for I have redeemed you... when you pass through the waters I will be with you" (Isa 43:1-2), and who says "I am he" repeatedly in Isaiah 40 to 55? The sign is doing what the feeding did: showing the disciples, in a category their Scriptures had already prepared, that they are dealing with the God of Israel in a man. And notice the placement. Between the crowd's attempt to crown him and his discourse about being the bread of life, John puts a private miracle for twelve frightened men in the dark. Public acclaim gets refused; the disciples in trouble get a visitation. That ordering is pastoral, and it is worth remembering the next time you are rowing against the wind and he seems late.

Application

  • About God He tests to teach, gives beyond need, wastes nothing, refuses false crowns, and comes to his own across impossible water.
  • About you Philip's arithmetic is the default posture of most Christian problem solving: measure the need, measure the resources, conclude. Andrew's move is better. Bring the inadequate thing and let him decide what it is worth.
  • Do Name the inadequate thing you have been withholding because it is obviously too small: a skill, an hour, a sum of money, an ability to explain the faith. Offer it to Christ this week in one concrete act rather than in a general prayer.

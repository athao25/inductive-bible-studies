The man and the moment

The author never writes his own name. He calls himself "the disciple whom Jesus loved" (13:23, 21:20), and the book's final page vouches for him: "this is the disciple who is bearing witness about these things, and who has written these things" (21:24). The early church unanimously identified him as John the son of Zebedee, the fisherman, one of the inner three with Peter and James, and the writer of three letters and Revelation. He writes as an eyewitness: "we have seen his glory" (1:14), and at the cross, "he who saw it has borne witness" (19:35). He remembers what an eyewitness remembers: that it was about the tenth hour (1:39), that there were six jars (2:6), that the charcoal fire smelled the same on both nights (18:18, 21:9).

He also writes last. Most date this Gospel around AD 80-90, from Ephesus, decades after Matthew, Mark, and Luke were in circulation. That explains its shape: John does not repeat what the church already had. About ninety percent of his material is unique. No genealogy, no birth story, no parables of the Synoptic kind, no bread and cup at the Supper; instead, seven signs, long conversations with individuals, the upper room, and the great "I am" claims.

New word: the Synoptics, and the one who stands apart
Matthew, Mark, and Luke are the Synoptic Gospels, "seeing together": shared material, shared order. John is the fourth voice, a supplement and an interpretation. Where Matthew argued from fulfilled prophecy to a Jewish audience, John writes for everyone, and tells you so.

The stated purpose

You observed it yesterday. No book of the Bible states its aim more plainly:

...but these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name. John 20:31, ESV

Track two words through the whole book. Believe: the verb occurs about 98 times (and the noun "faith", remarkably, never; for John, believing is something you do). Life: not merely existence after death but the life of the age to come possessed now: "whoever believes in the Son has eternal life" (3:36), present tense.

One book, two halves

After the prologue (1:1-18) tells you who Jesus is before he speaks a word, the book divides at 12:23, where "my hour has not yet come" finally becomes "the hour has come".

Inside the first half, John counts seven signs (water to wine, the official's son, the invalid, the feeding, the sea, the blind man, Lazarus), and across the book Jesus makes seven "I am" claims (bread, light, door, shepherd, resurrection, way-truth-life, vine), with the unadorned "before Abraham was, I am" (8:58) behind them all. Both tables are in the book map; every future lesson will orient itself there.

The book's center of gravity

Everything in John argues one claim: Jesus is God the Son, and life is found in him. The first verse says the Word was God (1:1); the last confession in the book is Thomas's "My Lord and my God!" (20:28); and between them every sign, every "I am", and every controversy presses the same question. Note the paradox John will not let you escape: the moment of Jesus' deepest humiliation is called his glorification (12:23, 17:1). In this Gospel the cross is not the King's defeat on the way to a crown; it is the throne room itself, glory revealed in self-giving love.

Your teachers in John: mostly one voice
Good news after the Matthew course: the eschatology forks of Matthew 24-25 barely surface here. On John's great doctrines, the full deity of Christ and the Father's sovereign drawing of sinners (6:37-44), MacArthur, White, and Durbin speak with one voice, and lessons will say so. The live checkpoints are smaller: "born of water and the Spirit" (3:5), whether John 6 speaks of the Lord's Supper (6:52-58), the manuscript question at 7:53-8:11, the fruitless branches (15:2, 6), and "receive the Holy Spirit" (20:22-23). James White is this course's specialist witness: his defenses of John 1:1 and John 6 are standard Reformed reading. See the lens card on the book map.

Check yourself

Memory verse 1 activates

Start with the sentence the whole book unpacks:

And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth. John 1:14, ESV

Read it aloud 10 times, then say it from memory 5 times. Full slate and schedule in memory verses.

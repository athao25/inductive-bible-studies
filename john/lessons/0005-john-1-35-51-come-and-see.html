Lesson 0005 · Unit 1: Chapters 1-2

John 1:35-51: Come and See

~25 min · Passage: John 1:35-51 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 1:35-51 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write down at least five observations: the first words Jesus speaks, who finds whom, how many titles Jesus is given by the end of the chapter (count them), what convinces Nathanael, and what Jesus promises in the last verse. Come back with your list in front of you.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The Baptist gives his disciples away (vv. 35-37). "Behold, the Lamb of God!", and two of his own followers leave him for Jesus. The witness succeeds by losing his audience, and the text records no complaint.
  • Jesus' first words in this Gospel are a question (v. 38). "What are you seeking?" Not a command, not a sermon: a question aimed at the heart's direction. The first words of any character in a careful book are a signature.
  • The invitation is experiential (v. 39). "Come and you will see." Not "here is the syllabus". And an eyewitness stamp: "it was about the tenth hour". Sixty years on, the author remembers the time of day his life turned.
  • Finding runs in a chain (vv. 40-45). Andrew "first found his own brother Simon"; Philip "found Nathanael". Each convert's first move is to find someone else. The verb "found" beats through the paragraph.
  • Jesus renames Simon on sight (v. 42). "You are Simon the son of John. You shall be called Cephas" (Peter, the rock). Naming is a claim of authority, and the name is a promise about what the unstable fisherman will become.
  • One honest skeptic (v. 46). "Can anything good come out of Nazareth?" Philip does not argue; he repeats Jesus' own method: "Come and see."
  • Jesus knows Nathanael before meeting him (vv. 47-48). "An Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit"; "before Philip called you, when you were under the fig tree, I saw you." Supernatural knowledge, noted quietly. Remember 2:25 is coming: "he himself knew what was in man".
  • The chapter ends in a cascade of titles. Count what chapter 1 has called him: the Word, God, the Light, the Lamb of God, the Son of God, Rabbi, the Messiah/Christ, him of whom Moses and the prophets wrote, the King of Israel, the Son of Man. Ten, before a single miracle.
  • The closing promise reaches back to Genesis (v. 51). "You will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man." The first "Truly, truly" of the Gospel, and an unmistakable echo of Jacob's dream (Gen 28:12).

Interpretation

Why is "What are you seeking?" the right first question?

Because in this Gospel, everyone is seeking something, and what you seek judges you. The crowds will seek Jesus for bread (6:26); the leaders will seek to kill him (5:18); the Greeks will come saying "we wish to see Jesus" (12:21); and in the garden he will ask its dark twin, "Whom do you seek?" (18:4), where the same question splits worshipers from arresting soldiers. Asked here of two men trailing him on a road, it does what Jesus' questions always do: it surfaces the heart. Their answer, "where are you staying?", sounds mundane and is not; the verb is meno, "abide", the word that will carry the vine chapter (15:4-7). They are asking to be with him, and his answer is this Gospel's whole method of evangelism: "Come and you will see." Not proof first, then presence; presence, then sight.

What is the cascade of titles doing?

Nathanael answered him, "Rabbi, you are the Son of God! You are the King of Israel!" John 1:49, ESV

John front-loads in one chapter what the Synoptics build toward across many: by verse 51 the reader has heard essentially every major title Jesus bears. This is deliberate. Remember the purpose statement: John writes so that you may believe that Jesus is "the Christ, the Son of God" (20:31), and chapter 1 hands you the full confession up front, on the lips of ordinary men after one meeting. The book will then spend twenty chapters showing what those titles cost and mean: the Messiah is a crucified Messiah, the King reigns from a cross, the Son of Man is glorified by being lifted up. First the names, then the redefinition. Note also who testifies: a former disciple of the Baptist, a fisherman's brother, a man from Bethsaida, a skeptic under a fig tree. The chain of witness (1:7) has already left the professionals and gone to the laity.

What does Jacob's ladder have to do with Nathanael?

And he said to him, "Truly, truly, I say to you, you will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man." John 1:51, ESV

At Bethel, Jacob dreamed of "a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven, and behold, the angels of God were ascending and descending on it" (Gen 28:12), and woke saying, "this is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven" (28:17). Jesus takes the dream and puts himself in the ladder's place: the angels ascend and descend on the Son of Man. He is the meeting point of heaven and earth, the true Bethel, the gate of heaven in person. The address is sharpened by a pun the text has set up: Jacob was the man of deceit (Gen 27:35), and Jesus has just greeted Nathanael as "an Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit". A guileless son of Israel is promised what the guileful father of Israel only dreamed. And this is the first appearance of "Son of Man" in John, Jesus' favorite self-title (from Dan 7:13-14): keep it in view, because in this Gospel the Son of Man is above all the one who must be "lifted up" (3:14, 12:34), and the ladder and the lifting are the same claim: one connector between God and man, planted in the earth.

Application

  • About God The Son knows people before they arrive: Simon's future, Nathanael's fig tree, your seeking. Nothing you bring to Christ is news to him, and he renames and receives anyway.
  • About you Answer Jesus' first question honestly: what are you seeking, in this course, in your prayers, in your week? Bread, answers, vindication, or him? Write your answer in one sentence; you will meet the question again in chapter 6.
  • Do Practice Andrew's move once this week: "found first his own brother". Identify the person nearest you who is farthest from Christ, and pray today for one natural "come and see" opening. No script needed; Philip did not argue with Nathanael, he extended an invitation.

Church prompt
Ask someone at church how they came to Christ, and listen for the Andrew in their story: almost everyone was found by someone. Then tell them who found you. Chapter 1 suggests this chain is the ordinary machinery of the kingdom.

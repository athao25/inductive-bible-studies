John 11:17-44: The Resurrection and the Life

~30 min · Passage: John 11:17-44 (ESV, read first!) · Memory verse 6 activates

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 11:17-44 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write down at least five observations: how long Lazarus has been dead, what Martha says twice, the fifth "I am" saying and its question, what makes Jesus weep and what word John uses for his emotion, and the exact words spoken at the tomb.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Four days (v. 17). The detail matters. A popular belief held that the soul lingered near the body for three days; by day four, hope was gone by anyone's reckoning, and decay was assumed (v. 39).
  • Bethany is close (v. 18). "About two miles off", so many Jerusalem mourners are present, which makes this the most publicly witnessed sign in the book.
  • Both sisters say the same sentence (vv. 21, 32). "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died." Grief with faith in it, and a hint of complaint.
  • Martha's hope is orthodox and distant (v. 24). "I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day."
  • The fifth "I am" (v. 25). "I am the resurrection and the life." He moves the doctrine from a date to a person.
  • Two promises, two conditions (vv. 25-26). Whoever believes, though he die, yet shall he live; and everyone who lives and believes shall never die. Physical death does not end life; spiritual death never touches the believer.
  • A direct question (v. 26). "Do you believe this?" The doctrine is pressed for a personal answer, and Martha gives a full confession (v. 27).
  • Mary falls at his feet (v. 32). Where Martha argued theology, Mary weeps.
  • Two strong emotion words (v. 33). "Deeply moved in his spirit" translates a word often used of anger or indignation, and "greatly troubled" is agitation. He is not merely sad; he is angry at death.
  • "Jesus wept" (v. 35). The shortest verse in English Bibles, and a different word from the loud wailing of the crowd: quiet tears.
  • The crowd reads it two ways (vv. 36-37). "See how he loved him!" and "Could not he who opened the eyes of the blind man have kept this man from dying?"
  • Deeply moved again at the tomb (v. 38). The same indignation word repeats as he approaches the grave.
  • Martha's objection is practical (v. 39). "Lord, by this time there will be an odor." Faith and realism collide out loud.
  • The reminder (v. 40). "Did I not tell you that if you believed you would see the glory of God?"
  • The prayer is for the crowd (vv. 41-42). "I said this on account of the people standing around, that they may believe that you sent me."
  • Three words that empty a tomb (v. 43). "Lazarus, come out." Loud voice, and a name. Augustine's old remark still lands: had he not named him, every grave would have emptied.
  • He comes out bound (v. 44). "Unbind him, and let him go." The community does the unwrapping.

Interpretation

What is Jesus claiming in verses 25 and 26?

Jesus said to her, "I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live, and everyone who lives and believes in me shall never die. Do you believe this?" John 11:25-26, ESV

Martha had a correct doctrine about a distant day. Jesus does not correct her theology; he relocates it. Resurrection is not primarily an event on the calendar but a person standing in front of her, which means the future she was waiting for has walked into her village. Take the two clauses separately, because they say different things. "Though he die, yet shall he live" concerns the believer who dies physically: death happens, and it is not the end. "Everyone who lives and believes in me shall never die" concerns the life the believer already has: the eternal life given now (5:24) is never interrupted by the grave. Put together, they say that for a Christian, death changes the address and not the life. Then the question, "do you believe this?", which is the hinge of the whole chapter. John has recorded an enormous claim and immediately turned it toward the reader. It is the same move as 20:31. And Martha's answer is the model: "Yes, Lord; I believe that you are the Christ, the Son of God, who is coming into the world." She confesses the person, not just the proposition.

Why does he weep if he is about to raise him?

Jesus wept. John 11:35, ESV

He knows the outcome. He announced it two days ago. Minutes from now the tomb will be empty and the mourning will turn to astonishment. And still he cries. Three things are worth taking from that. First, the incarnation is not a performance. The Word made flesh grieves at a friend's grave in a way that is real, not staged for the crowd, and this is the same person who is "deeply moved", a word carrying indignation. He is angry at death, at what sin did to the world he made, and at the wreckage in front of him. Christianity does not require you to pretend death is fine. Second, his tears are not a failure of faith, which means yours are not either. Sorrow and confidence are not competitors; 1 Thessalonians 4:13 says we do not grieve as those who have no hope, not that we do not grieve. Third, notice the timing: he weeps before he raises. He does not skip the grief to get to the miracle. If you are grieving now with a resurrection promised later, you are standing exactly where he stood. Finally, do not miss the cost. Verses 45 to 53 will show that this sign is what sets the council on the decision to kill him. Raising Lazarus is the act that seals his own death, which is the pattern of the whole Gospel: he gives life at the price of his own.

Memory verse 6

I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live, and everyone who lives and believes in me shall never die. John 11:25-26, ESV

Say it aloud three times today, once tomorrow, and again at week's end. Learn it well: this is the verse to have ready at a hospital bed or a graveside, and the question that follows it, "do you believe this?", is the one that should be asked out loud. Schedule at memory verses.

Application

  • About God He weeps and he raises. Power without compassion would be terrifying, and compassion without power would be useless. This chapter refuses to give up either.
  • About you Both sisters said the same reproachful sentence, and he received both. You may bring your "if you had been here" to him without pretending it is not there.
  • Do Think of someone grieving right now. Do not send them a verse and stop there: go, sit, and be willing to be sad with them before you offer hope. That order is his.

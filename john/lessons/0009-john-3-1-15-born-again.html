John 3:1-15: You Must Be Born Again

~25 min · Passage: John 3:1-15 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 3:1-15 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write down at least five observations: who Nicodemus is, when he comes, what he opens with, what Jesus answers instead, how many times "born" appears, and what Old Testament event Jesus reaches for at verse 14.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The hinge from 2:25 (v. 1). "He knew what was in man. Now there was a man of the Pharisees." John joins the paragraphs deliberately: Nicodemus is the specimen of what is in man at its most impressive.
  • His credentials are stacked (v. 1). Pharisee, "a ruler of the Jews" (a member of the Sanhedrin), and later "the teacher of Israel" (v. 10). If moral effort and theological training could produce the kingdom, he would already be in.
  • "By night" (v. 2). John's light and darkness vocabulary is never accidental (1:5; 3:19-21; 13:30). Fear of exposure is part of it, and so is the state he is in when he arrives.
  • He opens with a compliment about signs (v. 2). "We know that you are a teacher come from God, for no one can do these signs." That is exactly the sign-faith Jesus declined to trust in 2:23-25.
  • Jesus answers a question he did not ask (v. 3). No greeting returned, no discussion of credentials. "Unless one is born again he cannot see the kingdom of God."
  • "Again" is also "from above" (v. 3). The Greek anothen carries both; John loves such doubles. Nicodemus hears "a second time" and gets stuck (v. 4); Jesus means "from above", from God (compare 3:31, "he who comes from above").
  • Two negatives, not two options (vv. 3, 5). "Cannot see"; "cannot enter". This is not the higher path for the eager. It is the only door for anyone.
  • "Water and the Spirit" (v. 5). One preposition governs both nouns: a single birth described two ways, not two separate births.
  • Flesh begets flesh (v. 6). A category statement. Human descent, human religion, and human effort all stay on the flesh side of the line. Only Spirit produces spirit.
  • The wind picture (v. 8). One word, pneuma, means both wind and Spirit. You hear the sound, you do not control the direction: sovereign, real, and evidenced by effects.
  • The rebuke (v. 10). "Are you the teacher of Israel and yet you do not understand these things?" Jesus expected him to know this from the prophets, which tells you where to look next.
  • The serpent (vv. 14-15). Numbers 21:4-9 pulled forward: "so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life." The first of John's three "lifted up" sayings (3:14; 8:28; 12:32-33).

Interpretation

What does "born again" actually mean?

Jesus answered him, "Truly, truly, I say to you, unless one is born again he cannot see the kingdom of God." John 3:3, ESV

Birth is the perfect image because of what it excludes. Nobody arranges, assists, or consents to their own birth; you contribute nothing to an event that gives you everything. That is why the theological term for this is regeneration: God's act of granting new spiritual life to someone who was, in the categories of Ephesians 2:1, dead. The prologue already said it in advance: children of God are born "not of blood nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man, but of God" (1:13). Three human sources denied, one divine source affirmed. Note carefully what this does not mean. It does not mean faith is unnecessary; verse 15 puts belief right at the center. It means the new birth is what makes faith possible, the way life precedes breathing rather than resulting from it. That order is the heart of the Reformed reading, and it is the whole reason Jesus refuses to give Nicodemus a program to follow. There is no program. There is a birth, and it is God's to give.

Doctrine checkpoint 1: what is "water" in 3:5?
Three readings are common, and it is worth knowing all three.
  • Baptism. Christian baptism read back into the text. The problem: Christian baptism did not yet exist, and Jesus faults Nicodemus for not already knowing this (v. 10), which he could not have known from a rite not yet instituted.
  • Natural birth. Water as amniotic fluid, so "born of water and Spirit" means born physically and then spiritually. Possible, and it fits verse 6's two births, but the phrasing "born of water" for ordinary birth is not otherwise attested.
  • Ezekiel 36:25-27 (the reading this course takes). "I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean... And I will put my Spirit within you." Water and Spirit stand together there as one promise of new-covenant cleansing and renewal, exactly what the teacher of Israel should have recognized. One birth, described from two angles: washed and made alive. MacArthur, White, and Durbin all land here, and the verse 10 rebuke is the strongest argument for it.
Read Ezekiel 36:25-27 today with John 3:5 open beside it. That single cross-reference settles more than any commentary can.

Why the bronze serpent?

And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life. John 3:14-15, ESV

In Numbers 21 the people are dying of snakebite under judgment they earned. God's remedy is strange: a bronze serpent on a pole, and the promise that anyone who looks lives. No medicine, no strength required, no partial credit for looking harder. The dying either look or they do not. Jesus takes that scene as a type of himself. He will be lifted on a pole, made to bear the very thing that is killing us (2 Cor 5:21), and the response asked of the dying is a look, which John calls believing. Notice the word "must": divine necessity, not tragedy. And notice the double meaning John will exploit twice more: "lifted up" means both crucified and exalted (8:28; 12:32-33), because in this Gospel the cross is the throne. This is also the first appearance of "eternal life" in the book, John's favorite way of naming salvation: not merely endless duration but a quality of life, knowing the true God and the one he sent (17:3), begun now.

Application

  • About God He gives what he demands. He requires a birth no one can perform and then performs it, sovereignly, like wind. Salvation is his work from the first breath of it.
  • About you If Nicodemus needed this, your resume is not the exception. Every advantage he had, you may have some version of: doctrine, discipline, reputation. None of it crosses the flesh-to-Spirit line. The right response is not despair but the look of verse 15.
  • Do Read Ezekiel 36:25-27 and Numbers 21:4-9 today, ten minutes total, and write one sentence on each: what did God promise, and what did the dying have to do? Then pray the second sentence back to him.

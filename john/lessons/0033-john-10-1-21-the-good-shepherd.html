John 10:1-21: The Good Shepherd

~30 min · Passage: John 10:1-21 (ESV, read first!) · Memory verse 5 activates

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • No chapter break in the original (10:1). This is still the conversation with the Pharisees who threw the healed man out. They are the false shepherds in the picture, which makes it a rebuke before it is a comfort.
  • Thieves climb; the shepherd enters (vv. 1-2). Legitimacy is shown by the door.
  • Voice recognition (vv. 3-5). "The sheep hear his voice, and he calls his own sheep by name and leads them out... they will not follow a stranger."
  • They do not understand (v. 6). John calls it a "figure of speech", and notes the failure to grasp it, which prompts the plainer sayings that follow.
  • "I am the door of the sheep" (vv. 7, 9). Access and safety: "If anyone enters by me, he will be saved and will go in and out and find pasture."
  • The thief's threefold purpose (v. 10). "To steal and kill and destroy." Against it: "I came that they may have life and have it abundantly."
  • "I am the good shepherd" (v. 11). And the defining act: "The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep." Note "for", meaning on behalf of and in the place of.
  • The hired hand test (vv. 12-13). He flees because "he cares nothing for the sheep." Ownership, not employment, produces sacrifice.
  • Mutual knowledge (vv. 14-15). "I know my own and my own know me, just as the Father knows me and I know the Father." The measure of Christ's knowledge of his people is the Father-Son relationship.
  • Other sheep (v. 16). "And I have other sheep that are not of this fold. I must bring them also... So there will be one flock, one shepherd." Gentiles, and the "must" is divine necessity.
  • The Father's love and the laying down (v. 17). "For this reason the Father loves me, because I lay down my life that I may take it up again."
  • Voluntary, with authority both ways (v. 18). "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord. I have authority to lay it down, and I have authority to take it up again."
  • Division again (vv. 19-21). Some say he is demon-possessed; others answer, "Can a demon open the eyes of the blind?" The sign of chapter 9 is still doing its work.

Interpretation

Why shepherd language, and why now?

Ezekiel 34 is the background, and reading it will do more for this passage than any commentary. There God indicts the shepherds of Israel who fed themselves rather than the flock, who did not strengthen the weak or seek the lost, and who ruled harshly. Then he says: "I myself will search for my sheep and will seek them out... I will rescue them", and "I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David." Now stand that beside John 9. The shepherds of Israel have just cast out a sheep for the crime of seeing, and Jesus went and found him. Chapter 10 is not a change of subject; it is the interpretation of what just happened. He is claiming the role that Ezekiel gave to God himself and to the coming David, and he is doing so in front of the men he has just cast as the hired hands. Note also the flock language in verse 16. One flock, one shepherd, made of sheep from more than one fold, which is what Paul later calls the dividing wall broken down (Eph 2:14-16). The church's unity is not organizational; it is the fact that one voice called all of them.

What does the good shepherd do that no shepherd does?

I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep. John 10:11, ESV

A real shepherd risks his life for the flock, but a dead shepherd is useless to sheep; the death is at best a tragic accident of the job. Here the death is the point. Verse 15 makes it substitutionary, "I lay down my life for the sheep", and verse 18 makes it voluntary in a way no martyr's death is: he has authority to lay it down and authority to take it up again. Hold that against everything that happens from chapter 18 onward. Judas, the soldiers, Caiaphas, and Pilate are all real actors doing real evil, and none of them takes his life. He lays it down. There is also a much-debated phrase here worth handling honestly: "for the sheep". Jesus does not say "for the goats" or "for everyone indiscriminately"; the death is aimed, purposeful, and effective for a particular flock, and verse 26 will say bluntly, "you do not believe because you are not among my sheep." That is the doctrine of definite atonement in seed form, and the Reformed teachers behind this course all read it here. Take it as intended, which is not as a puzzle about who is excluded but as a comfort about what the cross accomplished. He did not make salvation merely available; he laid down his life to save specific people by name (v. 3), and he will lose none of them (6:39; 10:28).

Memory verse 5

My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me. I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand. John 10:27-28, ESV

This comes from the next section, which you will study in lesson 34, but memorize it now while the shepherd imagery is fresh. Three marks of a sheep (hear, are known, follow) and three gifts (eternal life, never perish, never snatched). Say it three times today, once tomorrow, and again at week's end. Application

  • About God He knows his people the way the Father knows the Son. That is the standard of intimacy in verse 15, and it is almost too much to take in.
  • About you The mark of a sheep is hearing his voice, which practically means Scripture increasingly sounds like the voice of someone you know rather than a text you study.
  • Do Read Ezekiel 34 this week in one sitting with John 10 beside it. Mark every promise God makes about seeking, binding up, and shepherding, and write the verse in John where you see it kept.

John 11:45-57: One Man for the People

~25 min · Passage: John 11:45-57 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 11:45-57 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write down at least five observations: how the witnesses split, what the council admits and what it fears, what Caiaphas says, how John interprets it, what is decided, and where Jesus withdraws.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The same sign, two responses (vv. 45-46). "Many of the Jews... believed in him, but some of them went to the Pharisees and told them what Jesus had done." Evidence does not compel faith.
  • The council concedes the miracle (v. 47). "What are we to do? For this man performs many signs." Nobody in the room disputes the facts. That is worth noticing: the historical question was never really in play for those closest to it.
  • The fear is institutional (v. 48). "The Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation." Their concern is the temple and their standing in it.
  • The irony is savage. They kill him to keep place and nation; within forty years Rome destroyed both. Refusing Christ to preserve something is how the thing is lost.
  • Caiaphas is contemptuous (vv. 49-50). "You know nothing at all. Nor do you understand that it is better for you that one man should die for the people, not that the whole nation should perish." Cold political arithmetic.
  • John says he prophesied (vv. 51-52). "He did not say this of his own accord, but being high priest that year he prophesied that Jesus would die for the nation, and not for the nation only, but also to gather into one the children of God who are scattered abroad."
  • "For the nation" and beyond (v. 52). The scope widens exactly as 10:16 promised: one flock from many folds.
  • The decision is formal (v. 53). "So from that day on they made plans to put him to death." A resolution, not a mood.
  • He withdraws to Ephraim (v. 54). Not fear but timing, once again.
  • The third Passover approaches (v. 55). John's clock is running out, and pilgrims are purifying themselves for a feast at which the Lamb will die.
  • The crowd speculates (v. 56). "What do you think? That he will not come to the feast at all?"
  • A warrant, effectively (v. 57). Orders that anyone knowing his location should report it. The stage for Judas is set.

Interpretation

How does God speak through a corrupt high priest?

He did not say this of his own accord, but being high priest that year he prophesied that Jesus would die for the nation, and not for the nation only, but also to gather into one the children of God who are scattered abroad. John 11:51-52, ESV

Caiaphas meant something ugly: sacrifice one troublemaker to protect the establishment. John says God meant something else with the same words, and that Caiaphas' office, however corruptly held, was still the office through which God had promised to speak. This is one of the clearest small windows in Scripture on how providence works. God does not need good intentions in order to accomplish his purpose through a person's words or actions; he governs the free and even wicked choices of men without becoming the author of their wickedness. You will see the same thing at the cross itself: "this Jesus, delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God, you crucified" (Acts 2:23). Both halves are true in one sentence. Notice as well the theological weight of what Caiaphas accidentally said: one man dies in the place of the people so the people do not perish. That is substitution stated by the enemy prosecuting the case. The high priest of Israel, in the year of the crucifixion, announced the meaning of the sacrifice he was arranging, and understood none of it.

Why does the greatest sign produce the death sentence?

Sequence matters here. Jesus raises a man who had been dead four days, in public, two miles from Jerusalem, and the direct result is a formal decision to kill him. If a miracle of that magnitude does not produce faith, the problem was never a shortage of evidence, which is precisely what John has been arguing since 2:23-25 and what Luke 16:31 says in one line: "neither will they be convinced if someone should rise from the dead." Look also at the reason given in verse 48. They do not say "he is a fraud" or "the sign was faked". They say they will lose their place. Self-interest is the engine, and it wears the clothing of responsible leadership: we are protecting the nation. Ask what that means for you. The most dangerous form of unbelief is not intellectual difficulty; it is having something you are not willing to lose. And the final irony deserves to be sat with rather than merely admired. They chose the death of Christ to keep the temple, and the temple came down anyway in AD 70. Everything held onto in place of him is eventually lost as well.

Application

  • About God He rules over hostile plans so completely that the enemy's press release states the gospel. Nothing in the passion narrative escapes his purpose.
  • About you Name the "place and nation" in your own life: the thing you would be most reluctant to lose for Christ. That is where unbelief will make its case if it ever does.
  • Do Read Acts 2:23 and 4:27-28 this week beside John 11:51-52, and write one sentence on how human responsibility and divine plan sit together in the death of Christ.

Unit 4 Recap: Light in Conflict

~25 min · No new passage · Consolidating John 7-8

Two chapters at one feast, and the temperature never drops. Booths supplied the props, water and light, and Jesus claimed both. By the end of chapter 8 there are stones in hands. Today you retrieve the unit and put it in your own words.

Say it without looking

  1. Explain how the two Booths ceremonies set up 7:37-39 and 8:12.
  2. State what John 8:58 claims and give the two reasons the grammar and the reaction both point that way.
  3. Recite memory verses 1 through 4 in order (1:14; 3:16; 6:35; 8:12).
Check your answers
(1) For seven days priests poured water from Siloam at the altar, remembering the rock in the wilderness and praying for rain; on the last day Jesus stands and offers himself as the source of living water. At night, huge lamps lit the temple court in memory of the pillar of fire; in that court he says "I am the light of the world." (2) He contrasts Abraham coming into being with his own timeless being, using the absolute "I am" of Exodus 3:14 and Isaiah; and his hearers immediately picked up stones, the penalty for blasphemy, without correction from him.

Recall: the whole unit

The unit in one frame

Chapter 7 is the anatomy of a divided crowd. His own brothers push a publicity strategy while not believing; Jesus answers that his time is fixed and their time is always available. He teaches in the temple mid-feast, gives the test that anyone can run ("if anyone's will is to do God's will, he will know"), and is met with rumor, geography, snobbery, and insult, but almost no investigation. Officers sent to arrest him come back with a review of his preaching. Nicodemus asks only for due process and is sneered at. And on the great day of the feast, into the water ceremony, Jesus shouts an invitation to anyone who is thirsty and promises rivers, which John tells us means the Spirit after the cross.

Chapter 8 turns the light on. He is the light of the world, and the promise is to followers. The dispute over testimony leads to the divide between from-below and from-above, and to the warning that unless they believe "I am", they will die in their sins. To those who believed him he says that abiding, not beginning, marks a disciple, that habitual sin is slavery, and that only the Son can free a slave. The paternity argument runs its course to the sharpest sentence in the Gospels, and then to the highest claim: before Abraham was, I am. Stones come up. He walks out. Between the two chapters, in brackets, sits the account of the woman caught in adultery, which the course handled honestly as almost certainly not part of what John wrote, and which teaches nothing that undisputed passages do not.

John 7:53-8:11: The Woman Caught

~30 min · Passage: John 7:53-8:11 (ESV, read first!) · Doctrine checkpoint 3

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 7:53-8:11 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now, including the bracketed note your Bible prints above it. Write down five observations about the scene, and write down your honest reaction to the note. Both matter today.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Doctrine checkpoint 3: does this passage belong in the Bible?

Your ESV brackets these verses and prints a note: "The earliest manuscripts do not include 7:53-8:11." That note is honest, and this course will not skip past it. Here is the situation in plain terms.

The evidence. This paragraph, called the pericope adulterae, is absent from our oldest and best Greek manuscripts, including the great fourth-century codices. It is absent from the earliest translations into several languages. Early Greek commentators who walk verse by verse through John do not comment on it, which is hard to explain if it was in their copies. Where it does appear in later manuscripts, it wanders: some place it after John 7:52, some at the end of John, some in the middle of Luke 21. Its vocabulary and style differ noticeably from the rest of John. Taken together, the evidence that it was not part of what John wrote is about as strong as textual evidence gets.

What that does and does not mean. It does not mean the event never happened; many careful scholars think it is a genuine memory of Jesus that circulated in the early church and was later written into the margin and then into the text. It does mean it should not be preached as part of John's inspired argument, and no doctrine should be built on it alone. James White has done the most accessible work here for lay readers, and his conclusion, shared by MacArthur and most Reformed scholars, is that the passage is not original to John while the account itself is not inconsistent with what we know of Christ.

Why this should increase your confidence, not shake it. The reason you know about this question at all is that Christians count, compare, and publish the evidence in the footnotes of ordinary Bibles. Textual criticism is the discipline of comparing thousands of manuscripts to establish what was written, and the New Testament is the best-attested document of the ancient world by an enormous margin. The disputed portions are few, they are marked, and not one article of the faith depends on them. A religion hiding something does not print the problem in the margin of the pew Bible.

How to handle it practically. Do not build sermons or arguments on it. Do not attack a believer who loves it. Learn what it teaches from passages that are unquestioned, which is easy, because everything true in it is taught elsewhere in John and in the Gospels.

Observation check

Reading it as a probable early account rather than as John's own text, the scene still repays observation:

  • A trap, stated as such (8:6). "This they said to test him, that they might have some charge to bring against him."
  • The trap has two jaws. Say stone her and he collides with Rome, which reserved capital punishment. Say release her and he appears to set aside Moses.
  • The case is rigged (8:4). "Caught in the act of adultery." Adultery requires two people, and the law they cite (Lev 20:10; Deut 22:22) requires both to be punished. The man is missing, which tells you what they were actually interested in.
  • She is a prop (8:3). Placed "in the midst", used as a lever against Jesus. Nobody in the crowd is concerned for her.
  • He writes on the ground (8:6, 8). The text does not say what. Guesses are endless; the honest answer is that we do not know, and the silence is probably deliberate.
  • The answer keeps the law and disarms the trap (8:7). "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her." Deuteronomy 17:7 required the witnesses to cast the first stones.
  • They leave oldest first (8:9). A quiet, devastating detail about who has the longest memory of their own record.
  • Neither condemnation nor permission (8:10-11). "Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more." Two halves, and dropping either one distorts him.

Interpretation

The one thing this account is most often misused to say

Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more. John 8:11, ESV

The first half of that sentence is quoted constantly, usually to argue that Jesus does not care much about sin or that no one may ever say anything is wrong. The second half is quoted much less. Read whole, the sentence says something more demanding than either a soft or a harsh reading allows: the only person present with the standing to condemn her declines to do so, and then commands a changed life. Grace and holiness in one breath. That combination is everywhere in the parts of John nobody disputes: "you must be born again", followed by "whoever believes is not condemned"; "the wrath of God remains", followed by "whoever comes to me I will never cast out"; and later, "sin no more, that nothing worse may happen to you" (5:14), which is nearly the same sentence in an undisputed passage. That is why this account, whatever its origin, does not teach a doctrine the rest of the Gospel lacks. If it vanished tomorrow you would lose nothing of Christ's character.

What to do with a Bible that has footnotes

Some people feel a jolt the first time they notice brackets in their Bible, and the honest thing is to name what actually follows from it. The doctrine of inspiration applies to what the biblical authors wrote. The task of textual criticism is to establish what they wrote, using the manuscript evidence we have, and the result for the New Testament is a text we can hold with enormous confidence. The two well-known disputed passages, this one and the long ending of Mark, are printed with warnings precisely because the church has never wanted to smuggle anything in. Your practical rule can be simple: read the footnotes, trust the text, and build your convictions on passages nobody disputes. There are plenty, and every one of them says the same Christ.

Application

  • About God Whether or not John wrote this paragraph, the Christ it describes is the Christ of the whole Gospel: unwilling to be used, unafraid of a trap, merciful to a woman used as a prop, and unwilling to leave her where she was.
  • About you Notice which half of verse 11 you prefer, and why. Most of us are lopsided in one direction, and the imbalance shows up in how we treat other people's sin.
  • Do Open your Bible to Mark 16:9-20 and read the note there too. Getting used to the footnotes now means no one will ever be able to rattle you with them later.

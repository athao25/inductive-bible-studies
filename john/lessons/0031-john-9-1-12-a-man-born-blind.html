Lesson 0031 · Unit 5: Chapters 9-10

John 9:1-12: A Man Born Blind

~25 min · Passage: John 9:1-12 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 9:1-12 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write down at least five observations: what the disciples assume in their question, how Jesus reframes it, what he says about day and night, what he makes and where he sends the man, and how the neighbors respond to the result.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Blind from birth (v. 1). Not an illness acquired later. Nothing in the man's own life could have caused it, which is exactly what makes the disciples' question so revealing.
  • The disciples want a cause (v. 2). "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" A closed question with two options, both of them blame.
  • Jesus rejects both options (v. 3). "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents." He is not denying that sin exists or that some suffering has direct causes (5:14); he is denying this diagnosis in this case.
  • He substitutes purpose for cause (v. 3). "But that the works of God might be displayed in him."
  • Urgency (v. 4). "We must work the works of him who sent me while it is day; night is coming, when no one can work."
  • The light saying returns (v. 5). "As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world." Chapter 8 said it in words; chapter 9 acts it out.
  • Mud, deliberately (v. 6). He spits, makes clay, and anoints the man's eyes. Making clay was one of the labors forbidden on a Sabbath by tradition, and we learn in verse 14 that it was a Sabbath. He could have spoken a word; he chose an action that would start a fight.
  • Echo of creation. Dust plus the touch of God producing a living faculty recalls Genesis 2:7. The man is not repaired; he is given eyes he never had.
  • A command with a walk attached (v. 7). "Go, wash in the pool of Siloam." Blind, muddy, across the city. Obedience before sight.
  • John translates the name (v. 7). "Which means Sent." The word John uses of Jesus constantly, sent by the Father. The pool is named after the thing Jesus is.
  • Simple report of the result (v. 7). "So he went and washed and came back seeing." No description of feeling, no crowd reaction inside the sentence.
  • The neighbors argue about identity (vv. 8-9). "Is this not the man who used to sit and beg?" Some say yes, some say he only resembles him. He keeps saying, "I am the man."
  • His testimony is factual and limited (vv. 10-12). "The man called Jesus made mud... I went and washed and received my sight." Then, "Where is he?" "I do not know." He reports what happened and admits what he does not know, which is a model witness.

Interpretation

Why does Jesus refuse the "who sinned?" question?

Jesus answered, "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him." John 9:3, ESV

The disciples asked a question people still ask at every hospital bed, and it assumes a tidy moral mechanism: suffering is repayment, so find the transgression. Scripture does teach that sin has consequences, and Jesus himself warns one man that something worse could follow continued sin (5:14). But the book of Job spent forty chapters demolishing the automatic version of that logic, and here Jesus does it in a sentence. He moves the question from backward-looking cause to forward-looking purpose. That reframing is one of the most practically useful things in the Gospel. Notice what it does not say. It does not say the man's blindness was pleasant, or that God is indifferent to it, or that suffering is always explained. It says this particular blindness exists within a purpose, that God's works would be displayed. Forty years of darkness so that one afternoon this man would see, and so that everyone reading this text ever since would learn what Christ does with the born-blind. If you are in something long and unexplained, the question this passage puts in your mouth is not "what did I do?" but "what will God display?"

Why mud, and why Siloam?

Jesus heals people with a word from twenty miles away (4:50), so the mud is not a technique. It is a message, and it does at least three things. It recalls the creation of man from dust, which fits a miracle that gives an organ that never worked rather than repairing one that failed. It provokes the Sabbath conflict deliberately, since kneading clay was forbidden by tradition, which means the confrontation of the rest of the chapter is intentional rather than accidental. And it requires obedience: a blind man with mud on his face has to walk through the streets to a specific pool before anything changes. Then the name of the pool does its own work. John stops the narrative to tell you that Siloam means "Sent", and John's favorite description of Jesus is the one whom the Father sent. The water that heals comes from the pool called Sent, and the sight comes from the Sent One himself. That is also the pool that supplied the water ceremony at Booths two chapters ago, which ties the whole section together: he is the water and he is the light, and the man who washes in the pool of the Sent One sees the light of the world.

Application

  • About God He acts in the dark places nobody can explain, and he calls the explanation "the works of God displayed" rather than a punishment ledger.
  • About you Watch your instinct when you hear of someone's disaster. If the first movement of your mind is toward what they must have done, chapter 9 is aimed at you as much as at the Pharisees.
  • Do Write down one long-running hardship in your life or in someone you love, and beneath it write verse 3 in full. Then do one small act of obedience you have been putting off, in the spirit of a man walking to Siloam before he could see.

Read John 9:13-41 in your ESV, twice, and jot five observations. It is a courtroom drama in four rounds; track who is questioned each time, how the man's answers develop, what his parents do and why, the sentence "one thing I know", and the final exchange about blindness. It is long but fast.
Primary source
In your MacArthur Study Bible (Olive Tree), read the notes on 9:2-3 (who sinned) and 9:6-7 (mud and Siloam). Five minutes. Ask one question: what does the note say about the tradition that made mud-making a Sabbath violation?
Church prompt
Ask someone at church who has a long-term illness or disability what unhelpful things Christians have said to them about it. Then read John 9:3 together. That conversation will change how you speak to the next person in that position.

Questions welcome, always: "does God cause disability?", "why does Jesus say 'we must work'?", "does verse 3 mean this man never sinned?" Ask your teacher.

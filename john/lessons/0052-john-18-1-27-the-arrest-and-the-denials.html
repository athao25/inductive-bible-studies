Lesson 0052 · Unit 8: Chapters 18-21

John 18:1-27: The Arrest and the Denials

~25 min · Passage: John 18:1-27 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 18:1-27 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write down at least five observations: what happens when Jesus identifies himself, what he says about the disciples and about the cup, what Peter does with a sword, where each denial takes place, and what detail John gives about the fire.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • A familiar garden (vv. 1-2). "Judas, who betrayed him, also knew the place, for Jesus often met there with his disciples." Jesus goes to the place his betrayer would look.
  • A small army (v. 3). A Roman cohort plus temple officers, "with lanterns and torches and weapons", to arrest a man who had been teaching publicly all week.
  • He initiates (v. 4). "Then Jesus, knowing all that would happen to him, came forward and said to them, 'Whom do you seek?'" No hiding, no capture.
  • "I am he" fells them (vv. 5-6). The Greek is ego eimi, and "they drew back and fell to the ground." A flash of the glory behind the arrest.
  • He negotiates for his disciples (v. 8). "If you seek me, let these men go."
  • John connects it to the prayer (v. 9). "This was to fulfill the word that he had spoken: 'Of those whom you gave me I have lost not one.'" Physical protection here, but John treats it as of a piece with 17:12.
  • Peter's sword (v. 10). Only John names both Peter and Malchus, likely because it was safe to do so by the time he wrote.
  • The cup (v. 11). "Shall I not drink the cup that the Father has given me?" In the prophets, the cup is the wrath of God (Isa 51:17; Jer 25:15).
  • Bound and taken to Annas first (vv. 12-13). The former high priest, father-in-law of Caiaphas, still the power behind the office.
  • Caiaphas' policy recalled (v. 14). John reminds us of 11:50: better that one man die for the people.
  • Another disciple gets Peter in (vv. 15-16). Access to the courtyard is what puts Peter beside the fire.
  • The first denial is to a servant girl (v. 17). "I am not." Note the contrast with the "I am" ringing in the garden minutes earlier.
  • A charcoal fire (v. 18). John specifies the kind of fire, and he will mention only one other charcoal fire, in 21:9. The smell will be part of the restoration.
  • Jesus answers Annas about openness (vv. 19-21). "I have spoken openly to the world... Why do you ask me? Ask those who have heard me."
  • Struck for answering (vv. 22-23). "If what I said is wrong, bear witness about the wrong; but if what I said is right, why do you strike me?" Composure under illegal treatment.
  • Denials two and three, then the rooster (vv. 25-27). John cuts back and forth between the trial and the courtyard so that the two scenes comment on each other.

Interpretation

Who is in control of this arrest?

Jesus, knowing all that would happen to him, came forward and said to them, "Whom do you seek?"... When Jesus said to them, "I am he," they drew back and fell to the ground. John 18:4, 6, ESV

John tells the arrest so that nobody can mistake who is directing it. Jesus knows everything in advance, walks toward the soldiers, asks the questions, identifies himself, drops an armed cohort to the ground with two words, sets the terms for his disciples' release, stops Peter's sword, and states his own reason for going: the Father has given him a cup. That is 10:18 in narrative form, "no one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord." Notice specifically the falling in verse 6. It is not a magic trick; it is a controlled flash of who he is, and it means every subsequent moment of the passion happens because he permits it. Then hold that beside the cup. The reason he does not call the twelve legions is not weakness but obedience, and what is in the cup is the thing that makes the cross more than an execution: the wrath of God against sin, borne in the place of others. Peter's sword is well-intentioned and completely wrong, because the cup cannot be avoided without the world being lost. Much of our anxious defending of Christ has the same shape.

Why does John juxtapose the trial with the fire?

Read the two scenes as one. Inside, Jesus is asked about his teaching and answers that he has spoken openly, hiding nothing, and is struck for it. Outside, Peter is asked three times whether he is a disciple and says no, warming himself at the enemy's fire. Faithful confession under threat, and failed confession in comfort, in adjacent paragraphs. John does not moralize; he simply cuts between them and lets you feel it. Two things follow. First, the contrast makes the atonement personal: at the very moment Jesus is bearing witness, the man who promised to die for him is disowning him, and Jesus goes to the cross for that man anyway. Second, notice how ordinary the pressure on Peter is. Not torture, not a tribunal, but a servant girl at a doorway and some men standing around a fire. The failures that undo us are rarely dramatic. And John plants the detail that pays off later: charcoal. In 21:9 there is a second charcoal fire on a beach, and three questions, and a restoration. This is a Gospel where nothing gets left unhealed.

Application

  • About God The Son walks toward the soldiers. Every step of the passion is chosen, and the cup was given by the Father and accepted by the Son.
  • About you Watch where you are standing when the questions come. Peter's problem began earlier, with self-confidence in the upper room and a sword in the garden, not at the fire.
  • Do Name the setting where you are most likely to keep quiet about Christ. Decide in advance, in one sentence, what you will say the next time it comes up. Deciding beforehand is most of the battle.

John 20:19-31: My Lord and My God

~30 min · Passage: John 20:19-31 (ESV, read first!) · Memory verse 10 · Doctrine checkpoint 5

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 20:19-31 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write down at least five observations: what he says and shows first, the commission and the breathing, what is said about forgiving sins, what Thomas demands and what he ends up confessing, the blessing in verse 29, and the purpose statement in verses 30 and 31.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Locked doors, and fear (v. 19). "The doors being locked where the disciples were for fear of the Jews, Jesus came and stood among them." The resurrection body is physical yet not limited as ours is.
  • His first word is peace (v. 19). To men who had all abandoned him. No reproach is recorded.
  • He shows the wounds (v. 20). "He showed them his hands and his side." The risen body keeps the scars, which are now identification and trophy.
  • Joy follows sight (v. 20). Exactly as promised in 16:22.
  • The commission (v. 21). "As the Father has sent me, even so I am sending you." Their mission is patterned on his.
  • He breathed on them (v. 22). The same verb used in the Greek Old Testament of God breathing life into Adam (Gen 2:7) and of the breath entering the dry bones (Ezek 37:9).
  • The forgiveness saying (v. 23). "If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them; if you withhold forgiveness from any, it is withheld." Handled in the checkpoint below.
  • Thomas was absent (v. 24). One reason to keep meeting with the church is that Christ shows up there.
  • His demand is specific (v. 25). He wants to put his finger in the mark of the nails and his hand into the side.
  • Eight days later, same greeting (v. 26). "Peace be with you."
  • He offers exactly what Thomas asked (v. 27). "Put your finger here... Do not disbelieve, but believe." Christ meets the doubter on the doubter's terms.
  • The highest confession in the book (v. 28). "My Lord and my God!" And there is no record that Thomas touched anything.
  • A blessing aimed at readers (v. 29). "Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed."
  • The purpose statement (vv. 30-31). "Now Jesus did many other signs... but these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name." This is the thesis of the whole Gospel, placed at the end.

Interpretation

Why is Thomas' confession the climax of the book?

Thomas answered him, "My Lord and my God!" John 20:28, ESV

Return to 1:1: "the Word was God." The Gospel opens with the narrator's declaration and closes with a disciple's personal confession, and the two form a frame around everything in between. Three details make verse 28 decisive. First, it is addressed to Jesus, not exclaimed to the air; John writes "Thomas answered him." Second, both nouns carry the definite article in Greek and both are applied directly to Christ, which rules out reading it as a lesser or honorary title. Third, Jesus accepts it and pronounces a blessing on everyone who will believe the same thing without seeing. He does not correct it, which is what any faithful Jewish teacher would have done instantly if the words were wrong (compare Acts 14:14-15; Rev 22:8-9). The most doubtful man in the room makes the clearest statement of Christ's deity in the New Testament, one week after refusing to believe. Note also what happens to his doubt. He is not shamed for it; he is answered. Christ appears specifically for him and offers exactly the evidence he demanded. Doubt that stays in the room where Jesus shows up tends to get resolved, which is a strong argument for taking your questions to church rather than away from it.

Doctrine checkpoint 5: the breathing and the forgiveness of sins

Two questions come out of verses 22 and 23.

What is the breathing? Since Pentecost is still fifty days away (Acts 2), most readings take this either as an acted parable anticipating the gift, or as a genuine but preliminary impartation for the interval. Either way, the vocabulary is the point: the same verb as Genesis 2:7 and Ezekiel 37:9 means John is presenting this as new creation. The Christ who was the agent of the first creation (1:3) breathes on his people at the beginning of the new one.

Do the apostles forgive sins? The Roman Catholic reading takes this as authority conferred on the church to absolve. Two considerations point elsewhere. Grammatically, the verbs for "are forgiven" and "is withheld" are in a form that can be rendered "have been forgiven", meaning the church announces a verdict already settled in heaven rather than creating it. Contextually, this comes immediately after the sending of verse 21: they are being commissioned to preach a gospel, and the gospel declares forgiveness to those who believe and its absence to those who refuse. That is exactly how the apostles behave in Acts: Peter tells the crowd their sins may be forgiven if they repent (Acts 2:38), and tells Simon he is still in the gall of bitterness (Acts 8:20-23). No apostle in the New Testament ever absolves anyone by personal authority. The church holds the keys in the sense that it holds the message, which is the reading MacArthur, White, and Durbin all take, and it makes every faithful proclamation of the gospel an act of enormous consequence.

Why does John state his purpose at the end?

Now Jesus did many other signs in the presence of the disciples, which are not written in this book; but these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name. John 20:30-31, ESV

Three things are in this sentence, and they explain the whole book you have been studying. First, selection: John chose from far more material, which means every episode in this Gospel is here on purpose. Second, aim: he is writing for belief, so the book is evangelistic and pastoral rather than merely historical, though it is that too. Third, content: not belief in general but that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, which is exactly the confession Martha made (11:27) and the substance of what Thomas just said. And the goal is life in his name, which takes you back to 1:4, 3:16, 5:24, 10:10, and 11:25. If you have worked through this course to here, this verse is your exam question. Not "do you know the seven signs and the seven I am sayings", useful as that is, but "do you believe this, and do you have life in his name?"

Application

  • About God He greets deserters with peace, keeps his scars, and answers a doubter by name with exactly what the doubter asked for.
  • About you Verse 29 is spoken about you: you have not seen, and the blessing is attached to believing anyway on the strength of the testimony you have been reading.
  • Do Write out 20:31 in your own words as a personal statement, in the first person. If you cannot yet, write down what stands in the way and take it to your teacher this week. That is the most important step in this whole course.

In your MacArthur Study Bible (Olive Tree), read the notes on 20:22 (he breathed on them), 20:23 (forgive the sins of any), and 20:28 (my Lord and my God). Five minutes. James White treats 20:28 at length as one of the clearest deity texts in the New Testament.
Church prompt
Ask someone at church what they would say to a person who claims the deity of Christ was invented by a church council centuries later. John 20:28, written by an eyewitness, is a one-verse answer.

John 1:1-18: The Word Made Flesh

~30 min · Passage: John 1:1-18 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read John 1:1-18 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Read it slowly, out loud if you can, and write down at least five observations: what the first three words echo, which nouns repeat, who the "he" of verses 6-8 is and what he is not, what verse 14 says the Word did, and who verse 18 says has made God known. Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The opening is borrowed (v. 1). "In the beginning" are the Bible's first words (Gen 1:1). John starts his Gospel where Moses started the world, and then goes further back: the Word already was.
  • Three clauses, carefully balanced (v. 1). The Word was in the beginning; the Word was with God; the Word was God. Distinction ("with God") and identity ("was God") sit side by side in a single verse.
  • Creation is total (v. 3). "All things were made through him, and without him was not any thing made that was made." Stated positively, then negatively, closing every loophole.
  • Two nouns become characters (vv. 4-5). Life and light. And a third: darkness, which "has not overcome" the light. The verb can also mean "has not understood"; John may intend both.
  • A witness interrupts (vv. 6-8, 15). Twice the poem pauses for John the Baptist, and twice we are told what he is not: "He was not the light". The negative is deliberate; the Baptist's own words in tomorrow's passage will repeat it.
  • The tragedy is stated flatly (vv. 10-11). "The world was made through him, yet the world did not know him. He came to his own, and his own people did not receive him." The maker unrecognized in his own workshop.
  • The exception has a strange birth (vv. 12-13). Those who did receive him were "born, not of blood nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man, but of God". Three human causes denied, one divine cause affirmed.
  • The climax is physical (v. 14). "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us." Not appeared as, not entered into: became. And "we have seen his glory": the author claims his own eyes.
  • Moses frames the ending (vv. 16-18). Law through Moses; grace and truth through Jesus Christ; and "no one has ever seen God", which is exactly Moses' story (Ex 33:20). The prologue ends inside Exodus.

Interpretation

Why call him "the Word"?

In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. John 1:1, ESV

Because in Genesis 1, God creates by speaking. "And God said, 'Let there be light,' and there was light" (Gen 1:3). Your word is your self-expression, your mind going out from you to be heard; God's Word is his perfect self-expression, and John's claim is that this Word is not a something but a someone, eternally with God, eternally God, the one through whom every made thing was made. Greek philosophy also used logos for the rational principle ordering the universe, so the term built a bridge to every reader John had, Jew and Greek alike. But the content is from Moses, not Athens: John 1 is a deliberate rewrite of Genesis 1, and its argument is that when you meet Jesus of Nazareth you meet the voice that said "let there be light".

"The Word was God": is the grammar solid?

You will meet people at your door who translate verse 1 "the Word was a god" (the Jehovah's Witnesses' New World Translation). This is the one place the course asks you to file a grammar fact for future use. In Greek the clause runs "and God was the Word", with "God" placed first for emphasis and without the article, which is how Greek says the Word was God by nature while keeping him distinct from the Father he was "with". James White's The Forgotten Trinity works this through in patient detail, and it is worth knowing that no serious Greek grammarian defends the "a god" rendering. John himself settles it internally anyway: his Gospel opens with "the Word was God" (1:1), centers on "before Abraham was, I am" (8:58), and closes on "My Lord and my God" (20:28). The bookends are load-bearing.

What does "dwelt among us" carry?

And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth. John 1:14, ESV

The verb is literally "tabernacled": pitched his tent. To a reader soaked in the Old Testament this detonates a whole storyline. God's glory once filled the tabernacle (Ex 40:34-35), the tent where God dwelt among his people yet behind a veil. John says the Word became flesh and tabernacled among us, and this time the glory was seen, not hidden: "we have seen his glory". Then verse 14's last phrase, "full of grace and truth", quotes the LORD's own self-description when he passed before Moses: "abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Ex 34:6). Verses 17-18 finish the thought: Moses received the law and was refused the face ("you cannot see my face", Ex 33:20); in the Son, "who is at the Father's side", God has now been seen and "made known", literally exegeted. Everything the tabernacle rehearsed, the incarnation performs.

Why deny "the will of man" a role in the new birth?

Verses 12-13 hold a balance you should memorize now, because chapter 3 and chapter 6 will hang on it. Verse 12 is a real offer and a real act: "to all who did receive him, who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God". Verse 13 then tells you where that receiving came from: a birth "not of blood nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man, but of God". Births are not self-caused; no one wills himself into existence, physically or spiritually. This is regeneration, and it is a point on which all three of your teachers agree without daylight: believing is genuinely yours, and it is God's gift before it is your act. Hold verse 12 and verse 13 together and refuse to drop either; that is simply what the text does.

Application

  • About God He is not silent. From "let there be light" to the Word made flesh, God is a self-revealing God, and his final word is not a book or an angel but his own Son (Heb 1:1-2). When you want to know what God is like, verse 18 gives the method: look at Jesus, who has made him known.
  • About you If you have received him, verse 12 gives your legal standing: child of God, by right, given. And verse 13 removes all ground for pride in it: you did not achieve that birth any more than your first one. Grace runs all the way down.
  • Do Write out John 1:1 and John 1:14 by hand today, side by side. Under them write one sentence: "The one who was God became flesh, and his glory was seen." Five minutes. This pairing is the whole Gospel in miniature, and verse 14 is your memory verse this week.

John 15:1-17: The Vine and the Branches

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The seventh "I am" (vv. 1, 5). "I am the true vine"; "I am the vine; you are the branches." True, as opposed to a vine that failed.
  • Israel was the vine (Old Testament background). Psalm 80:8-16; Isaiah 5:1-7; Jeremiah 2:21; Ezekiel 15. In every case the vine disappoints, and Isaiah 5 is explicit: he looked for justice and found bloodshed.
  • The Father is the vinedresser (v. 1). The gardener, not a bystander.
  • Two operations (v. 2). Fruitless branches taken away; fruitful branches pruned "that it may bear more fruit." Pruning is a compliment with a knife in it.
  • Already clean (v. 3). "Already you are clean because of the word that I have spoken to you." The same root word as "prunes" in verse 2, and the same idea as 13:10.
  • Abide is the imperative (v. 4). "Abide in me, and I in you." Ten uses of the word in this short passage.
  • Total dependence (v. 5). "Apart from me you can do nothing." Not little, nothing.
  • The fate of the withered branch (v. 6). Thrown away, withered, gathered, burned. Held in tension with 10:28, and handled in the checkpoint below.
  • Abiding shapes prayer (v. 7). "If you abide in me, and my words abide in you, ask whatever you wish, and it will be done for you." The conditions are in the first half.
  • Fruit glorifies the Father (v. 8). And it proves discipleship: "so prove to be my disciples."
  • Love, obedience, joy (vv. 9-11). Abide in my love; keep my commandments; "that my joy may be in you, and that your joy may be full."
  • The measure of love (vv. 12-13). "Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends." He is hours from proving it.
  • Friends, not servants (vv. 14-15). "No longer do I call you servants, for the servant does not know what his master is doing; but I have called you friends, for all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you."
  • He chose them (v. 16). "You did not choose me, but I chose you and appointed you that you should go and bear fruit and that your fruit should abide."
  • The paragraph ends where it started (v. 17). "These things I command you, so that you will love one another."

Interpretation

What does abiding actually mean?

I am the vine; you are the branches. Whoever abides in me and I in him, he it is that bears much fruit, for apart from me you can do nothing. John 15:5, ESV

The image does the defining. A branch does not strain to produce grapes; it remains attached and the life of the vine does the rest. So abiding is not an advanced spiritual technique or a mystical mood. Verse 7 gives it content: "if you abide in me, and my words abide in you". Verse 9 adds: "abide in my love". Verse 10 adds: "if you keep my commandments, you will abide in my love." Put together, abiding is a continuing, dependent relationship maintained by his word, his love, and obedience, which is to say it looks a great deal like an ordinary Christian life lived deliberately: Scripture, prayer, obedience, repentance, repeat. Two clarifications keep it from distortion. First, "apart from me you can do nothing" is not hyperbole about ministry effectiveness; it is a statement about spiritual fruit, none of which is manufacturable. Plenty of activity can be produced without Christ, and none of it counts as fruit. Second, notice the direction of causation in verse 16: he chose you, appointed you, and intends that your fruit should abide. Fruitfulness is his design for you before it is your project.

Doctrine checkpoint 4: who are the branches that are burned?

Verse 6 is the hardest verse in the chapter: "If anyone does not abide in me he is thrown away like a branch and withers; and the branches are gathered, thrown into the fire, and burned." Set against 10:28-29 and 6:39, it raises the obvious question. Three readings are common.

  • Loss of salvation. Genuine believers who stop abiding are finally lost. This reading has to be squared with "they will never perish", "no one will snatch them", and "I should lose nothing", which is a heavy load, and with 1 John 2:19, where those who depart are said never to have been of us.
  • Loss of reward or of usefulness, not of life. The burning describes a wasted Christian life rather than damnation. Possible, but the fire language is severe and the "thrown away" is hard to soften.
  • Attachment without life (the reading this course takes). The branch is connected to the vine outwardly, in the visible community, without ever drawing life from it. That is exactly Judas, whose feet had been washed at the same table two chapters ago, and exactly the many disciples of 6:66 who walked away. Being in the vineyard is not the same as being alive. On this reading, verse 6 is the same warning as Matthew 7:21-23 and 1 John 2:19, and it does not threaten the security of anyone who genuinely belongs to Christ.

MacArthur, White, and Durbin land here, and the decisive argument is contextual: the passage is spoken in a room that had just contained a fruitless branch by the name of Judas. Take the warning seriously and let it do its intended work, which is self-examination rather than anxiety: the question is not "have I abided perfectly?" but "am I attached to Christ himself, or only to Christian things?"

What does it mean that he calls them friends?

No longer do I call you servants, for the servant does not know what his master is doing; but I have called you friends, for all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you. John 15:15, ESV

Two things are worth noticing before this becomes sentimental. First, the basis of the friendship is disclosure: he has told them what the Father is doing. Friendship with God is not a feeling of closeness but a granted access to his purposes through his word, which means your Bible is the instrument of it. Second, the friendship does not cancel his lordship. Verse 14 says "you are my friends if you do what I command", which would be a strange sentence between equals. It is friendship with the King, initiated entirely from his side (v. 16, "you did not choose me, but I chose you"), and it costs him his life (v. 13). Abraham was called the friend of God (Isa 41:8; James 2:23), and Moses spoke with God as a man speaks to his friend (Ex 33:11); here that title is extended to ordinary men in a rented room, hours before they abandon him.

Memory verse 8

Say it aloud three times today, once tomorrow, and again at week's end. Schedule at memory verses.

Application

  • About God The Father prunes the branches he loves. If life has been cut back lately, this passage offers a category other than punishment or bad luck.
  • About you "Apart from me you can do nothing" is either a burden or a relief depending on how much you have been trying to carry. Read it as permission to stop straining and to stay attached.
  • Do Audit your week for abiding: how many days did his words get into you? Pick a fixed time and place for tomorrow and the next six days, and treat it as staying attached rather than as a duty performed.

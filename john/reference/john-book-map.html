John Book Map

The whole book at a glance. Return here every time a lesson asks "where are we?"

The one-sentence summary

Jesus of Nazareth is the eternal Word made flesh, the Son of God, who reveals the Father through signs and "I am" claims, is lifted up on the cross at his appointed hour, rises bodily, and gives eternal life to everyone who believes in his name.

Author and setting

The author never names himself; he is "the disciple whom Jesus loved" (13:23, 21:20-24), and the early church unanimously identified him as John the son of Zebedee, one of the inner three (with Peter and James). He writes as an eyewitness: "we have seen his glory" (1:14), "he who saw it has borne witness" (19:35). The Gospel was written late, most likely from Ephesus around AD 80-90, after the other three Gospels were in circulation. About ninety percent of its material appears in no other Gospel: no genealogy, no birth narrative, no parables of the Synoptic kind, no institution of the Supper; instead, seven signs, long private conversations, and the upper room. John also states his purpose outright, the clearest mission statement in the four Gospels: "these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name" (20:31).

The book's skeleton: signs, then glory

After a prologue (1:1-18) that tells you who Jesus is before he speaks a word, the book falls into two great halves, with an epilogue. The hinge is 12:23: "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified."

Key themes to track

  • Believe: the verb appears about 98 times (the noun "faith" never). The book's stated purpose (20:31): belief in the Son is the sole doorway to life.
  • Life: eternal life is present tense in John ("whoever believes in the Son has eternal life", 3:36); it begins now, not at death.
  • Light and darkness: from the prologue (1:4-5) to Nicodemus at night (3:2) to the blind man (9) to Judas going out "and it was night" (13:30).
  • Glory: the Word's glory seen (1:14), manifested at Cana (2:11), and supremely revealed, not in spite of the cross but in it (12:23, 17:1).
  • The hour: "my hour has not yet come" (2:4, 7:30, 8:20) until at last "the hour has come" (12:23, 13:1, 17:1). The cross runs on the Father's clock.
  • Witness: a courtroom word. The Baptist, the works, the Father, the Scriptures, the Spirit, and the author himself all testify (1:7, 5:31-47, 15:26-27, 21:24).
  • The Father and the Son: "Father" occurs well over 100 times. The Son does nothing of himself, and whoever has seen him has seen the Father (5:19, 14:9).
  • The Spirit, the Helper: promised as "another Helper" (14:16), the Spirit of truth who glorifies Christ (16:14); given in fullness once Jesus is glorified (7:39, 20:22).

John and the Old Testament

John quotes the Old Testament less often than Matthew but builds on it more deeply, usually by allusion. "In the beginning" (1:1) rewrites Genesis 1:1. The Word "dwelt" (literally tabernacled) among us (1:14) echoes the tent of meeting, and "grace and truth" echoes the LORD's self-declaration in Exodus 34:6. The Lamb of God (1:29) gathers Passover (Ex 12), the substitute ram (Gen 22), and the slaughtered Servant (Isa 53). Angels ascend and descend on the Son of Man (1:51) as on Jacob's ladder (Gen 28:12). The bronze serpent lifted up (3:14, Num 21:9), the manna (6:31-33, Ex 16), the rock's water and the pillar's light at the Feast of Booths (7:37-39, 8:12), the shepherd of Ezekiel 34 (ch. 10), and the pierced one of Zechariah 12:10 (19:37) all stand under the surface. When a lesson meets one of these, read the Old Testament context first; John assumes you know it.

Where your teachers stand in John
Happy news: the eschatology forks that dominated Matthew 24-25 barely arise in John, and on this Gospel's great doctrines, the deity of Christ (1:1, 8:58, 20:28) and God's sovereignty in salvation (6:37-44, 65), MacArthur, White, and Durbin agree, and the lessons will say so. The live checkpoints are smaller and different: what "born of water and the Spirit" means (3:5); whether the bread of life discourse is about the Lord's Supper (6:52-58); the manuscript question over the woman caught in adultery (7:53-8:11), which White has written on at length; what the fruitless branches are in the vine (15:1-8); and what "receive the Holy Spirit" grants the church (20:22-23). Lessons there present the options and argue from the text, not from labels. James White's decades of work on John 1:1 and John 6 against Arian and synergist readings make him this course's specialist witness.

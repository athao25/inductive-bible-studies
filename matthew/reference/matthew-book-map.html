The one-sentence summary

Jesus of Nazareth is Israel's promised Messiah-King, the fulfillment of the Old Testament, who is rejected by his own, atones for sin by his death, rises again, and sends his church to disciple all nations under his total authority.

Author and setting

Matthew, also called Levi, was a tax collector whom Jesus called to be one of the Twelve (Matt 9:9, 10:3); only his own list of apostles keeps the label "the tax collector", a mark of humility. The early church unanimously received this Gospel as his. It was written at a relatively early date, before the destruction of the temple in AD 70, to a predominantly Jewish audience: the genealogy runs from Abraham, Jewish customs go unexplained, and the Old Testament is quoted more than 60 times to prove that Jesus is the Messiah of promise. Matthew writes "kingdom of heaven" where the other evangelists write "kingdom of God", guarding Jewish sensibilities about the divine name.

The book's skeleton: five discourses

Matthew alternates narrative and teaching. Five great discourses each end with the formula "when Jesus had finished these sayings" (7:28, 11:1, 13:53, 19:1, 26:1); a prologue and an epilogue bracket the whole. Many see an echo of the five books of Moses: Jesus is the greater Moses teaching from the mountain.

Key themes to track

  • Fulfillment: "this was to fulfill what was spoken by the prophet", about a dozen times; the OT quoted 60+ times. Jesus is the Messiah of promise.
  • Kingdom of heaven: Matthew's signature phrase, 32 times. Announced (4:17), described (5-7), hidden and growing (13), consummated (25).
  • Son of David / Son of Man: royal credentials from the genealogy onward; Daniel's cloud-riding figure who receives dominion (26:64).
  • Rejection: no Gospel paints Israel's rejection of her King more starkly, from Herod to the cross; yet the King is victorious.
  • The church: the only Gospel to use the word (16:18, 18:17); built by Christ, unconquerable by the gates of hell, sent to the nations.
  • Immanuel: "God with us" (1:23) to "I am with you always" (28:20); the book is bracketed by the presence of Christ.

Matthew and the Prophets

Matthew leans on Isaiah at every hinge: the virgin sign (1:23 quoting Isa 7:14), Galilee of the Gentiles (4:15-16 quoting Isa 9:1-2), the Servant who bears our diseases (8:17 quoting Isa 53:4), the chosen Servant with the Spirit (12:18-21 quoting Isa 42:1-4), lips-and-heart worship (15:8-9 quoting Isa 29:13), and the voice in the wilderness (3:3 quoting Isa 40:3). Other prophets carry their own weight: Bethlehem from Micah (2:6 quoting Mic 5:2), the exodus-son from Hosea (2:15 quoting Hos 11:1), Rachel weeping from Jeremiah (2:18 quoting Jer 31:15), the humble king on a donkey from Zechariah (21:5 quoting Zech 9:9), and the struck shepherd (26:31 quoting Zech 13:7). When a lesson meets one of these, read the Old Testament context first; Matthew always assumes you know it.

Where your teachers diverge
The fork passages in Matthew are the law (5:17-20), the kingdom parables (13:31-33), the church and the keys (16:18-19), and above all the Olivet Discourse (24-25). MacArthur (premillennial, futurist): Matthew 24 describes a still-future tribulation before Christ's return; his Study Bible calls preterism a serious blunder. White (amillennial): the kingdom is Christ's present reign; Olivet blends near judgment and final coming. Durbin (postmillennial, partial-preterist, our home lens): "this generation" (24:34) means what it says, so much of Matthew 24 was fulfilled in the AD 70 destruction of Jerusalem, while 24:36 onward looks to the still-future bodily return; the mustard seed and leaven promise the gospel's triumph before that return. Lessons at those passages present all three and argue from the text, not from labels.

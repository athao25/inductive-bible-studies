Lesson 0075 · Unit 8: Chapters 26-28

Matthew 27:45-56: The Torn Veil

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 27:45-56 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 27:45-56 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations. Watch the clock (Matthew keeps telling you the hour), watch the sky, watch what happens the moment he dies, and count how many separate things tear, shake, split, or open in verses 51-53.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Before you read further

This is the summit of the Gospel. Everything from the genealogy forward has been walking toward these twelve verses, and everything after them looks back on these twelve verses. Matthew reports them with almost no commentary; he simply tells you what happened and lets the events speak. Read slowly today. If you come to the end of this lesson having only felt the weight of it, that is not a failure.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Matthew times the darkness precisely (v. 45). "From the sixth hour there was darkness over all the land until the ninth hour," that is, noon until three in the afternoon. Three full hours, and the middle of the day, when the sun should be at its strongest.
  • The only words from the cross Matthew records are a question (v. 46). Other Gospels give other sayings. Matthew gives one, and it is "Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?" He keeps the original words and then translates them, which he rarely bothers to do.
  • The cry comes at the end of the darkness, not the start (v. 46). "About the ninth hour." Three silent hours first, then the loud voice.
  • The bystanders mishear him (vv. 47-49). They think "Eli" is a call for Elijah. The sour wine and the waiting are not mercy; they are curiosity, "let us see whether Elijah will come to save him."
  • He cries out a second time and dies (v. 50). Note Matthew's wording: he "yielded up his spirit." A crucified man normally suffocates when he can no longer push himself up to breathe, silently and by inches. Jesus dies loudly, and the verb is active.
  • Four things happen at once, and all of them are God's doing (vv. 51-53). The temple curtain is torn, the earth shakes, the rocks split, the tombs open. Every verb is passive: torn, shaken, split, opened. Matthew never names an agent, because you already know who it is.
  • The tear runs downward (v. 51). "From top to bottom." Matthew could have said only that it tore. He tells you which direction.
  • The raised saints wait for him (vv. 52-53). The tombs open at his death, but those raised come out "after his resurrection." Matthew is careful to keep Jesus first in line.
  • The confession comes from the execution squad (v. 54). The centurion and his men, who had done the nailing, say "Truly this was the Son of God!" These are the same words the mockers used against him fifteen verses earlier (v. 43).
  • The women are still there, and they are named (vv. 55-56). "Many women there, looking on from a distance, who had followed Jesus from Galilee, ministering to him." Matthew names three. He has not mentioned the eleven since verse 56 of chapter 26, when they all fled.

Interpretation

Why does the sky go dark at noon?

Now from the sixth hour there was darkness over all the land until the ninth hour. Matthew 27:45, ESV

Whatever the mechanism, this is not an eclipse. Passover falls at full moon, and a solar eclipse is impossible at full moon; also, no eclipse lasts three hours. Matthew is not reporting weather. He is reporting a sign, and the prophets had already taught Israel to read it. Amos, announcing the day of the LORD's judgment, wrote: "I will make the sun go down at noon and darken the earth in broad daylight" (Amos 8:9). Joel says the sun will be turned to darkness before the day of the LORD (Joel 2:31). In the Old Testament, midday darkness means one thing: God has come in judgment. Remember also the ninth plague, three days of darkness over Egypt, immediately before the night the lambs died (Exodus 10:21-23).

So the sign in the sky interprets what is happening on the cross. From the outside, this looks like a Roman execution of a failed Galilean preacher. The darkness says: judgment is falling here. And the shock is where it falls. The wrath announced by the prophets against a guilty people is landing on the one innocent man in the story. MacArthur on chapter 27 dates the hours for you (the crucifixion began at nine in the morning, so he had already hung for three hours in daylight before the dark came). Three hours of pain in the light, then three hours of something else in the dark.

"My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"

And about the ninth hour Jesus cried out with a loud voice, saying, "Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?" that is, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" Matthew 27:46, ESV

This is the hardest sentence in the Gospel, and it deserves to be handled on three levels, carefully and in order.

First, it is a quotation. These are the opening words of Psalm 22. A first-century Jew who heard a line of a psalm heard the whole psalm; citing the first line was how you named it. And Psalm 22 is uncanny here. It has the mocking heads shaking (22:7), the taunt "He trusts in the LORD; let him deliver him" (22:8, which the crowd had just used in verse 43), the pierced hands and feet (22:16), and lots cast for clothing (22:18, which just happened in verse 35). But the psalm does not end in the pit. It turns: "he has not hidden his face from him, but has heard, when he cried to him" (22:24), and it ends with "All the ends of the earth shall remember and turn to the LORD, and all the families of the nations shall worship before you" (22:27), closing on a people not yet born who will be told "that he has done it" (22:31). So the cry is real anguish, and it is also, in the mouth of the one praying it, a deliberate reach for a psalm that ends in vindication and a worldwide worshiping church. He is not quoting despair. He is quoting the whole thing.

Second, the forsakenness is real, not merely felt. It would be easy, and wrong, to soften this into "he felt abandoned though he was not." Scripture will not let us. The New Testament says what was happening: "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Corinthians 5:21), and "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us—for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree'" (Galatians 3:13). This is substitution, one standing in the place of another and bearing what was owed. What was owed to sinners is judicial abandonment by God, the sentence of being cut off from his favor. Jesus bore that, in the place of his people, in the dark. The "why" is not confusion; he had told the disciples for three chapters that this was coming. It is the cry of a righteous man under a wrath he did not earn, asking the question out loud so that we would hear the cost.

Guard the doctrine here: the Trinity was not broken
You will sometimes hear this verse preached as though the Trinity came apart for three hours, or as though the Father stopped loving the Son. Do not say that, and do not believe it. God is one in essence and three in persons, and that eternal union cannot be severed; if it could, God would not be God. Nor did the Son cease to be the beloved Son. Scripture says the opposite about this very moment: "For this reason the Father loves me, because I lay down my life" (John 10:17). What Jesus bore was not the loss of the Father's love but the judicial abandonment that sinners deserve, the withdrawal of the comfort and favor of God experienced by the one who stood in their place. Reformed theologians have handled this with unusual care, and none of them claim to have explained it. Neither will we. Two guardrails and then silence: the union of the persons held, and the suffering was real. Between those two truths lies a mystery that should make you worship rather than speculate.

Third, notice what he still calls him. "My God, my God." Under the full weight of it, faith does not let go. That is not a small detail; it is the last thing the psalm-praying Son does before he dies.

The bystanders hear none of this. "Eli" sounds like "Elijah," and Jewish expectation held that Elijah would come before the day of the LORD (Malachi 4:5). So they offer sour wine, cheap soldier's vinegar, on a reed, not to comfort him but to keep him conscious a little longer for the show: "let us see whether Elijah will come to save him" (v. 49). Men stand within earshot of the atonement and treat it as entertainment. That is possible. It is possible now.

What do the four signs mean?

And Jesus cried out again with a loud voice and yielded up his spirit. And behold, the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom. And the earth shook, and the rocks were split. The tombs also were opened. And many bodies of the saints who had fallen asleep were raised, and coming out of the tombs after his resurrection they went into the holy city and appeared to many. Matthew 27:50-53, ESV

Start with the death itself. Matthew writes that he "yielded up his spirit." The wording is a giving, not a succumbing. Crucifixion kills by exhaustion; a man at that stage has no breath left for a loud cry. Jesus has one. He had said, "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord" (John 10:18), and Matthew's verb agrees. He was not overpowered. He finished, and then he let go.

The curtain (v. 51). The temple had a heavy woven curtain, the veil, separating the Most Holy Place from everything else (Exodus 26:33). Behind it was the presence of God, and behind it no one went, ever, except the high priest, once a year, on the Day of Atonement, with blood, and not without fear (Leviticus 16; Hebrews 9:7). That curtain was Israel's daily lesson in one word: closed. At the moment Jesus dies, it tears in two. Hebrews tells you exactly what that means: "we have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus, by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh" (Hebrews 10:19-20). The way in is open, and it is open through a torn body. And note the direction of the tear, which Matthew goes out of his way to record. From the top. No human hand starts there. MacArthur says it plainly and rightly: "The fact that it tore 'from top to bottom' showed that no human had split the veil. God did it." The Father's verdict on the Son's work was published in the temple within the hour.

The earthquake and the split rocks (v. 51). Creation itself reacts. In the Old Testament, the earth shakes when God draws near (Exodus 19:18; Psalm 68:8). The centurion sees this and it is what breaks him open (v. 54).

The opened tombs and the raised saints (vv. 52-53). These are the hardest verses in Matthew, and honesty serves you better than confidence here. The account is unique to Matthew; no other Gospel or epistle mentions it, and it has been debated for centuries. The main approaches: some read it straightforwardly, that tombs were split open at the death and that on Sunday, after Jesus rose, a number of Old Testament believers were raised, walked into Jerusalem, were seen, and were then taken to glory (this is MacArthur's reading, "a kind of foretaste of 1 Thess. 4:16"). Some read the appearances as visions granted to many rather than resurrections of the same kind as Christ's. Some read the passage as Matthew's deliberately apocalyptic way of announcing what the death accomplished, using the prophetic imagery of Ezekiel 37, where graves open and the dead of Israel rise. This course does not think you should pretend to certainty you do not have, and none of these readings is a denial of Scripture's truthfulness; they differ on genre and on detail. What is not in doubt is the theological point, and Matthew makes sure of it with one phrase: "after his resurrection." The graves crack open at his death, and yet nobody precedes him out. Christ is "the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20), the first sheaf of a harvest that necessarily follows. His resurrection does not merely prove that resurrection is possible; it pulls his people out after him. Death lost its grip on the graveyard the day he died.

Who is left at the cross when the dust settles?

When the centurion and those who were with him, keeping watch over Jesus, saw the earthquake and what took place, they were filled with awe and said, "Truly this was the Son of God!" Matthew 27:54, ESV

Stop and take in who says this. Through the whole Gospel, "Son of God" has come from the Father at the baptism and the transfiguration (3:17; 17:5), from demons (8:29), from Peter under revelation (16:16), from the high priest as an accusation (26:63), and from mockers as a taunt (27:40, 43). The first human being in Matthew to look at the crucified Jesus and confess it as a fact is a Roman officer of the occupying army who has just supervised the execution. A Gentile. A pagan. The man holding the hammer. That is the shape of this Gospel: Israel's leaders condemn him and a Gentile soldier confesses him, which is precisely where chapter 28 is heading when Jesus sends his people to "all nations."

And then Matthew turns the camera to the women (vv. 55-56). Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James and Joseph, and the mother of the sons of Zebedee, among many who had followed him from Galilee and supported his ministry. They are "looking on from a distance," which is likely all a woman associated with a condemned man could safely do. But they are there. Peter is not there. The eleven fled in 26:56. Matthew, who names names, records that the last friendly faces at the cross belonged to women whose discipleship had looked like cooking and funding and following, and who did not run. Keep them in view: these same women watch the burial (27:61), come to the tomb at dawn, and become the first witnesses of the resurrection (28:1-10). In a culture that discounted female testimony, God entrusted the central fact of the faith to the people who stayed.

Application

  • About God: he does not forgive by ignoring sin. He judges it, in the dark, at noon, on his own Son, and then he tears the curtain open himself from the top. Both halves of that are the same act of love.
  • About you: the way in is open, and it cost this. If you are a Christian, no doubt about your welcome survives verse 51; you do not approach God on the strength of your week. And if you are still watching from a distance, be careful: men stood close enough to hear the cry and heard only a rumor about Elijah.
  • Do: today, pray once with Hebrews 10:19-22 open in front of you, out loud, beginning by naming what the torn curtain means for your access. Then read Psalm 22 straight through in one sitting, all thirty-one verses, and mark where it turns. Fifteen minutes, both of them.

Church prompt
Ask someone at your church, ideally an elder or your pastor, how they would explain "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" to a new believer without dividing the Trinity. Listen for the guardrails: the union of Father and Son held, and the suffering was real and judicial. It is a good question to ask before Good Friday rather than after, and it will tell you a great deal about how carefully your church handles hard doctrine.

Questions welcome, always: "was the darkness worldwide or only over Judea?", "if the Son was forsaken, was God divided?", "why does only Matthew report the raised saints?" Ask your teacher.

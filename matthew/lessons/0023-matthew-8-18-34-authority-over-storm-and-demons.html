Matthew 8:18-34: Authority Over Storm and Demons

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 8:18-34 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Two would-be followers are wedged between two miracles (vv. 18-22). Matthew has just finished a healing block (8:1-17) and is about to start a nature miracle. He interrupts the power display with a conversation about cost. That placement is the argument: the same authority that calms a sea also claims your whole life.
  • The volunteer is a scribe, and the hesitant one is already a disciple (vv. 19, 21). The order is deliberate. An expert in the Law says "I will follow you wherever you go"; a man already following asks for an exemption. Enthusiasm and obedience are not the same thing.
  • "Son of Man" appears here for the first time in Matthew (v. 20). It is Jesus' own answer to the scribe's offer, and its first outing in this Gospel describes homelessness, not glory.
  • Jesus never says yes or no to either man (vv. 20, 22). He answers the scribe with a fact about himself, and the disciple with a command, "Follow me." Neither man's later choice is recorded. Matthew leaves the reader holding the question.
  • The disciples get into the storm by obeying (v. 23). "When he got into the boat, his disciples followed him." The verb is the same idea as verse 22. They are exactly where the command put them, and the storm comes anyway.
  • He is asleep, and then he is sovereign (vv. 24-26). Verse 24 gives you a man so exhausted that a swamping boat does not wake him. Two verses later he speaks to weather and it obeys. Matthew puts full humanity and full deity within three lines and does not blink.
  • The rebuke comes in a surprising order (v. 26). "Why are you afraid, O you of little faith?" comes first. Then "he rose and rebuked the winds and the sea." He addresses their hearts while the boat is still filling.
  • Matthew counts two demoniacs (v. 28). Mark 5:2 and Luke 8:27 describe one. Note the difference now; we will handle it below rather than smooth it over.
  • The demons have better theology than the town (vv. 29, 34). They call him "Son of God" and concede a coming judgment on a schedule they did not set. The townspeople see the same power and ask him to go away.

Interpretation

What does "leave the dead to bury their own dead" actually demand?

"And a scribe came up and said to him, 'Teacher, I will follow you wherever you go.' And Jesus said to him, 'Foxes have holes, and birds of the air have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head.' Another of the disciples said to him, 'Lord, let me first go and bury my father.' And Jesus said to him, 'Follow me, and leave the dead to bury their own dead.'" Matthew 8:19-22, ESV

Take the first man first. The scribe offers unlimited commitment ("wherever you go"), and Jesus replies with a fact about accommodation. Foxes and birds have addresses; the King of the kingdom does not. MacArthur on chapter 8 observes that this scribe "was breaking with his fellow scribes by publicly declaring his willingness to follow Jesus," yet "had not counted the cost in terms of suffering and inconvenience." Jesus does not recruit him with a promise; he shows him the ledger.

The second saying is harder, and it deserves care rather than a slogan. Burying a father was, in Jewish life, close to the most sacred obligation a son could have, ranked above nearly every other duty. Read flatly, Jesus appears to forbid a man from attending his own father's funeral, which would put him at odds with the fifth commandment he elsewhere defends fiercely (15:3-6). So what is the request?

Two readings carry real weight. First, the father may not be dead yet. "Let me bury my father" functioned as an idiom for staying home until a parent's affairs were settled; MacArthur takes it exactly this way, calling it "a common figure of speech meaning, 'Let me wait until I receive my inheritance.'" Second, Jewish burial in this period was often a two-stage affair: the body was entombed at once, then about a year later the bones were collected into an ossuary in a rite called secondary burial. A man in the middle of that year could truthfully say he was still "burying" his father. Either way, the request is not "give me an afternoon"; it is "give me a year, or a decade." Notice too that the man is standing in front of Jesus and speaking, which is hard to reconcile with a corpse waiting at home on a day when burial happened within hours.

Now the shape of the answer becomes visible. Jesus is not devaluing family or funerals. He is refusing to be slotted in after them. "Leave the dead to bury their own dead" turns on two senses of the word: let the spiritually dead handle the affairs of the dying world, because you have been called into life. The kingdom's claim outranks the most sacred family duty precisely because it outranks everything; a claim with an exception clause would not be a King's claim at all. He will say it in plain words later: "Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me" (10:37). Only God may talk like this. That is the point of putting the saying next to a man who commands the sea.

Who is Jesus calling himself in verse 20, and why start there?

"I saw in the night visions, and behold, with the clouds of heaven there came one like a son of man, and he came to the Ancient of Days and was presented before him. And to him was given dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him; his dominion is an everlasting dominion, which shall not pass away, and his kingdom one that shall not be destroyed." Daniel 7:13-14, ESV

Son of Man is the title Jesus uses for himself more than any other, 83 times in the Gospels and always on his own lips. It sounds humble in English, and it is, but its home address is Daniel 7, where a human figure approaches God's throne and receives everlasting worldwide dominion. So the title is a double exposure. Heard one way it means "a man like you." Heard with Daniel open, it means "the man to whom all nations are given." MacArthur names both halves: "an obvious reference to the humanity and the humility of Christ. Yet, it also speaks of his everlasting glory."

That is why Matthew's first use of it is a word about having nowhere to sleep. The one with an indestructible kingdom has no pillow. The humility is not a phase he passed through before the glory; in Daniel the dominion is given to the human figure who comes to the throne, and the road to that throne runs through the homelessness of verse 20 and eventually through a cross. Keep the title in view: it will return at his trial (26:64), where he quotes Daniel 7 to the high priest and is condemned for it.

Why is "who is this?" a question with only one possible answer?

"And when he got into the boat, his disciples followed him. And behold, there arose a great storm on the sea, so that the boat was being swamped by the waves; but he was asleep. And they went and woke him, saying, 'Save us, Lord; we are perishing.' And he said to them, 'Why are you afraid, O you of little faith?' Then he rose and rebuked the winds and the sea, and there was a great calm." Matthew 8:23-26, ESV

Start with the geography, because it makes the storm ordinary. The Sea of Galilee sits about 690 feet below sea level with Mount Hermon towering to the north, and cold air funneling down the gorges can turn the lake violent without warning. Fishermen knew this water. When professional fishermen say "we are perishing," believe them.

Now the two rebukes. Matthew's order is not the order we would choose: Jesus deals with their fear before he deals with their weather. "Little faith" is not "no faith"; they did, after all, wake the right person. Their faith was enough to run to him and not enough to rest in him, which is a description most of us will recognize. And notice the timing again, because it preaches: he can address your heart in the middle of the storm, before anything outside you has changed.

Then he speaks, and the lake goes flat. Their question, "What sort of man is this, that even winds and sea obey him?" (v. 27), is not a puzzle with several solutions. Any Israelite raised on the Psalms knew who commands the sea:

"You rule the raging of the sea; when its waves rise, you still them." Psalm 89:9, ESV
"He made the storm be still, and the waves of the sea were hushed." Psalm 107:29, ESV

In the Old Testament, stilling the sea is not a thing a great prophet does; it is a thing the LORD does, and the sea is the standing symbol of chaos he alone masters. Moses stretched out his hand and God parted the water. Jesus speaks to the water himself. MacArthur is right to call verse 27 "convincing proof of his deity," and the same conclusion is available to us: the only answer to "who is this?" that fits the evidence is the one the Psalms already gave.

One pastoral note before we leave the boat. The disciples are in that storm because they obeyed. Verse 23 says they followed him into it. Obedience is not a weather charm, and a storm is not automatic evidence that you took a wrong turn.

Two demoniacs or one, and why does the town want him gone?

"And behold, they cried out, 'What have you to do with us, O Son of God? Have you come here to torment us before the time?'" Matthew 8:29, ESV

Handle the number honestly first. Matthew reports two demon-possessed men; Mark 5 and Luke 8 tell the same event but follow one man, the one who ends up "clothed and in his right mind" and begging to come along. That is a difference of selection, not a contradiction. A contradiction would be Mark saying "only one man was there." He never says it. Reporting one of two is how ordinary truthful witnesses work; if you say two friends visited you, someone who mentions the one who did the talking has not lied. MacArthur's read is that "evidently one was more dominant than the other," which is a reasonable guess at why the other two Gospels narrow the focus. What matters for our method is the habit: notice the difference, name it plainly, and interpret Matthew on his own terms. He counts two, and two is a fitting number of witnesses (Deut. 19:15).

Now the confession. The demons identify him correctly on sight, "Son of God," while the region's residents will shortly show no idea who he is. They also ask "before the time," which concedes two enormous things at once: he has the authority to torment them, and there is a fixed appointment at which he will. They know they are on a clock and that he holds it. MacArthur puts it sharply: "Their eschatology was factually correct, but it is one thing to know the truth, and quite another thing to love it (cf. James 2:19)." Correct doctrine is not conversion. Demons are orthodox.

Then the pigs, and then the ending that should keep you up at night:

"And behold, all the city came out to meet Jesus, and when they saw him, they begged him to leave their region." Matthew 8:34, ESV

Two men who could not be passed on the road are free. The whole city comes out, sees the power that freed them, and asks it to go. MacArthur suggests either financial loss or plain terror at such spiritual power; both are likely, and the herd (2,000 animals, per Mark 5:13) was serious money. Either way the verdict is the same. They weighed the presence of the Son of God against their livestock and their comfort, and the livestock won. Matthew has just shown you a sea that obeyed instantly and a city that would not. Nothing in the chapter is more frightening than that.

Application

  • About God The one who had nowhere to lay his head is the one whose word flattens a storm. Power and poverty sit in the same person, and both are for you: he commands the chaos, and he entered the homelessness.
  • About you Two questions from the two halves. First, what is your "let me first"? Name the thing you have quietly placed ahead of obeying him, and be honest that it is probably a good thing, not a bad one, which is what makes it effective. Second, is there any place where you would rather Jesus stayed out, because his presence there would cost you money, standing, or a comfortable arrangement? The Gadarenes were polite about it. They just asked him to leave.
  • Do Today, take the "let me first" you just named and do one concrete thing that puts it second: make the call, cancel the delay, start the obedience you have been staging for later. Then, in the middle of whatever is currently unsettled, pray verse 25 word for word and stop asking him to be safe.

Church prompt
The Gadarenes preferred their economy to Christ's presence, and congregations can make the same trade in slower motion. This week ask a pastor, elder, or small group one question: where has our church been tempted to keep Jesus at a comfortable distance because obeying him plainly would cost us something (money, members, or reputation)? Ask it about the church, then let someone ask it back about you.

Questions welcome, always: "was the man's father really dead or not?", "why does Matthew say two men when Mark says one?", "if I obeyed, why am I in a storm?" Ask your teacher.

Matthew 21:18-46: The Rejected Stone

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 21:18-46 (ESV, read first!)

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Four scenes, one argument (vv. 18-46). A cursed tree, a question about authority, a parable of two sons, a parable of tenants. Matthew does not label the connection, but every scene is about the same people: the men who run the temple Jesus had just cleared out the day before.
  • The tree had leaves and nothing else (v. 19). "He went to it and found nothing on it but only leaves." The tree was not bare. It was advertising. On a fig tree, leaves normally come with early fruit, so full foliage is a promise the tree did not keep.
  • This is the only destructive miracle Jesus performs (v. 19). Everywhere else in the Gospels his power heals, feeds, calms, and raises. Once, exactly once, it withers something. That singularity is itself an observation worth writing down.
  • The disciples ask about the mechanism, not the meaning (v. 20). "How did the fig tree wither at once?" They watch a parable and ask a how question. Jesus answers the how question, then the four verses that follow answer the one they did not ask.
  • The challenge is doubled (v. 23). "By what authority are you doing these things, and who gave you this authority?" Two questions, not one: what kind of authority, and who issued it. They are asking for his credentials, not his identity.
  • They deliberate out loud and Matthew records it (vv. 25-26). "If we say, 'From heaven,' he will say to us, 'Why then did you not believe him?' But if we say, 'From man,' we are afraid of the crowd." Read that twice. They weigh consequences of both answers and never once ask which answer is true.
  • Both parables are set in a vineyard (vv. 28, 33). The two sons are sent to work in a vineyard; the tenants are leased a vineyard. Matthew has been building this image since 20:1.
  • The tenants' reasoning is spoken aloud (v. 38). "This is the heir. Come, let us kill him and have his inheritance." They know exactly who he is. Recognition is not the problem in this chapter.
  • They pass sentence on themselves (v. 41). Jesus asks what the owner will do; they answer, "He will put those wretches to a miserable death." Then verse 45: "they perceived that he was speaking about them."
  • Fear of the crowd appears twice (vv. 26, 46). It shapes their answer at the start and stays their hands at the end. The same motive brackets the whole confrontation.

Interpretation

1. Why would Jesus curse a tree?

And seeing a fig tree by the wayside, he went to it and found nothing on it but only leaves. And he said to it, "May no fruit ever come from you again!" And the fig tree withered at once. Matthew 21:19, ESV

Read flat, this looks like a hungry man losing his temper at a plant. It is not, and the key is that the fig tree is not a random roadside object. It is a standing Old Testament image for Israel. "When I would gather them, declares the LORD, there are no grapes on the vine, nor figs on the fig tree; even the leaves are withered" (Jer. 8:13). "Like the first fruit on the fig tree in its first season, I saw your fathers" (Hos. 9:10). Joel uses it the same way (Joel 1:7, 12). A first-century Jew hearing about a fruitless fig tree under judgment would not think about horticulture.

So this is an enacted parable: a message preached with an action instead of a sentence, the way Old Testament prophets sometimes buried a sash or lay on their side for a year. And notice how precisely the picture fits what Jesus had done the previous afternoon. He walked into a temple full of activity, currency exchange, sacrificial animals, and religious traffic, and called it a den of robbers. Leaves everywhere; no fruit. MacArthur says it plainly: the cursing "was a purposeful divine object lesson, not an impetuous act of frustration," and the barren fig tree "symbolizes divine judgment on Israel because of their spiritual fruitlessness . . . despite an abundance of spiritual advantages" (MacArthur on chapter 21, note on 21:19). Hold onto that phrase, despite an abundance of spiritual advantages. The charge is never that they had too little. It is that they had a great deal and produced nothing with it.

2. Does verse 22 promise that believing hard enough gets you what you ask?

"Truly, I say to you, if you have faith and do not doubt, you will not only do what has been done to the fig tree, but even if you say to this mountain, 'Be taken up and thrown into the sea,' it will happen. And whatever you ask in prayer, you will receive, if you have faith." Matthew 21:21-22, ESV

This verse gets lifted out of its paragraph and sold. Read in place, it is a promise about the immeasurable power of God reaching into ordinary people's prayers, spoken to men who had just watched that power operate. "Moving mountains" was a standard Jewish idiom for accomplishing the impossible; teachers who untangled hard problems were nicknamed mountain-movers. Jesus is not making an offer about geology or about your circumstances on demand.

Guard rail: faith is trust, not leverage
The name-it-and-claim-it reading says that sufficiently confident belief compels the outcome, and that a disappointing outcome proves your belief was defective. That teaching quietly makes God a mechanism and makes suffering people responsible for their own suffering. Scripture rules it out from several directions. John writes that we have confidence "that if we ask anything according to his will he hears us" (1 John 5:14). James says some prayers go unanswered because they are asked "to spend it on your passions" (James 4:3). Paul asked three times for a thorn to be removed and was told no by a Christ who loved him (2 Cor. 12:8-9). Even MacArthur, no friend of doubt, notes that "do not doubt" here "presupposes that the thing requested is actually God's will," since only God-given faith is that free of doubt. Faith is confidence in a Person who is free, wise, and good. It is not a currency you hand him.

3. What is wrong with the way the chief priests think?

And they discussed it among themselves, saying, "If we say, 'From heaven,' he will say to us, 'Why then did you not believe him?' But if we say, 'From man,' we are afraid of the crowd, for they all hold that John was a prophet." So they answered Jesus, "We do not know." Matthew 21:25-26, ESV

This is one of the most quietly devastating pieces of reporting in the Gospels, and it is the diagnosis of the entire chapter. Give them credit: their analysis is competent. They see both branches, they price both outcomes, they pick the answer that costs least. What is missing is a single line. Nowhere in their deliberation does anyone say, "but which is it? Was John from God or not?" Truth is simply not one of the variables. And so "we do not know" is a lie, and they know it is a lie, and Jesus declines to hand more light to men who have already refused the light they were given.

That is why the two sons follow immediately.

"Truly, I say to you, the tax collectors and the prostitutes go into the kingdom of God before you. For John came to you in the way of righteousness, and you did not believe him, but the tax collectors and the prostitutes believed him. And even when you saw it, you did not afterward change your minds and believe him." Matthew 21:31-32, ESV

One son says no and goes; the other says "I go, sir," and does not. The parable makes the point that doing is what counts, not saying, and Jesus makes them supply the verdict themselves. Then he applies it in the most offensive way available in that courtyard: the collaborating tax men and the prostitutes are ahead of the clergy in the queue. Be careful how you hear that. It is not that God prefers scandalous sin to respectable religion, or that a rude answer is more honest than a polite one. The difference is one thing only: repentance. The first son "changed his mind and went." The second never moved. And note the sting in the tail of verse 32: the leaders watched the notorious repent, saw it happen, and it changed nothing in them. Witnessing grace in someone else is not the same as receiving it.

4. Who loses the kingdom in verse 43, and who gets it?

The last parable is not a fresh invention. It is a retelling of a song every literate Jew knew: Isaiah 5:1-7, where God plants a vineyard, fences it, digs a winepress, builds a tower, and looks for grapes, and it yields wild grapes. Isaiah names the meaning outright: "the vineyard of the LORD of hosts is the house of Israel." Matthew's hearers would catch the allusion in the first sentence, because Jesus repeats Isaiah's details almost item for item (v. 33). What Jesus changes is the focus. Isaiah indicts the vineyard; Jesus indicts the tenants, the men leased the land to work it. That is a narrower target: the leadership standing in front of him.

"Finally he sent his son to them, saying, 'They will respect my son.' But when the tenants saw the son, they said to themselves, 'This is the heir. Come, let us kill him and have his inheritance.' And they took him and threw him out of the vineyard and killed him." Matthew 21:37-39, ESV

Sit with what is happening. Jesus is standing in the temple, days from Friday, telling the men who will arrange his execution the story of his execution, in advance, out loud, with the motive correctly identified. The servants are the prophets (1 Kings 22:24; 2 Chron. 24:20-21; Neh. 9:26). The son is himself. And their reasoning is not confusion but calculation: kill the heir, keep the estate. Then he asks them what the owner should do, and in verse 41 they answer, "He will put those wretches to a miserable death and let out the vineyard to other tenants." They have now condemned themselves twice in one conversation, once over the two sons and once here.

"The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone; this was the Lord's doing, and it is marvelous in our eyes"? Matthew 21:42, ESV, quoting Psalm 118:22-23

The builders are the ones qualified to judge stone. They examine it, rule it unusable, and throw it on the pile, and God sets it at the corner where the whole building is squared. That is the rejection and the resurrection in one image, and Psalm 118 is the very psalm the crowds had been shouting from two days earlier at the entry. Then verse 43: "Therefore I tell you, the kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a people producing its fruits."

Fork: what verse 43 does and does not say

The dispensational reading (MacArthur's family of interpretation) takes this as the removal of kingdom privilege from the nation of Israel in that generation, with the church as the present recipient, and a national restoration of Israel still ahead at Christ's return. On the crucial point MacArthur is not evasive here; his note on 21:41 says the kingdom and its advantages "would now be given to 'other tenants,' symbolizing the church," and on v. 43 simply, "a people producing its fruits. The church."

The covenantal reading (the postmillennial home lens of this course, and the amillennial one alike) presses the wording. Jesus says the kingdom goes to a people, an ethnos, producing its fruits, and Peter later uses that exact language of the church: "a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation" (1 Pet. 2:9), in a paragraph that also quotes this same cornerstone psalm. On this reading the vineyard is not repossessed and shelved for later; it is re-tenanted now, with a people drawn from every nation, believing Jews included, and it is expected to bear fruit.

What both must hold alongside it is Romans 11. Paul asks whether God has rejected his people and answers, "By no means!" (Rom. 11:1), describes natural branches broken off and wild branches grafted in, warns the grafted-in against arrogance, and says the broken branches can be grafted back (11:17-24). Whatever verse 43 means, it cannot mean that God is finished with Jewish people, and it cannot license the church to preen. Both readings claim Romans 11; they differ on whether its future is a distinct national program or the ongoing ingathering of Jews into one olive tree. Argue it from those texts, not from the labels.

Application

  • About God He is patient past reason (servant after servant, "finally he sent his son") and he is not mocked. The same chapter shows both, and the cornerstone verse shows that human rejection does not derail his building; it becomes the load-bearing corner of it.
  • About you Two diagnostics. The fig tree asks whether your leaves match your fruit, whether the visible religious activity is producing anything. And verse 25 asks how you decide things: when a hard question comes, do you first ask what is true, or first ask what an answer will cost you?
  • Do Today, find one place where you said "I go, sir" and did not go: a promise, an apology, a call, a task owed to someone at home. Do that one thing before you sleep. Then in prayer, name one belief you hold mainly because it is comfortable, and ask God plainly whether it is true.

Church prompt
The fig tree charge is leaves without figs: real activity, no produce. Ask someone at church this week what fruit our congregation would point to from the last year if a stranger asked, and be specific rather than general (people converted, marriages held together, poor people actually fed, sins actually repented of). Ask it about yourself first, and ask it without cynicism; the point of the passage is repentance, not audit.

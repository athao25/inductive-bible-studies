Lesson 0077 · Unit 8: Chapters 26-28

Matthew 28:1-15: He Has Risen

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 28:1-15 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 28:1-15 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations. Watch especially for who is told what, in what order; for every time someone is told not to be afraid; and for the two completely different stories that leave the tomb that morning.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

A word before you start

You have spent several lessons in the dark: a betrayal, a sham trial, a scourging, a cross, a borrowed grave, a stone, a seal, a guard. Today the tone changes and it is allowed to change. This is the best news in the book, and Matthew tells it with earthquakes and lightning and running. Read it like good news, because it is.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The same two women who watched him die and be buried come back (v. 1). "Mary Magdalene and the other Mary" were at the cross (27:56) and sitting opposite the tomb when the stone went into place (27:61). Matthew has kept the same pair of eyes on every stage, so nobody can say the women went to the wrong grave.
  • The earthquake is the second one (v. 2). The earth shook when he died (27:51), and it shakes again when the tomb opens. Matthew brackets the death and the resurrection with the same sign.
  • The angel sits on the stone (v. 2). He rolls it back and then sits down on it. That is a posture of finished work, and of contempt for the seal that Rome had set (27:66).
  • The guards trembled and became "like dead men" (v. 4). The soldiers assigned to prove that no one rose end up lying on the ground like corpses, while the corpse they were guarding is up and gone. Matthew does not comment. He does not need to.
  • The angel's message comes in six parts (vv. 5-7). Do not be afraid; I know whom you seek; he is not here; he has risen, as he said; come, see the place; go quickly and tell. Fear answered, then fact, then evidence, then commission.
  • "As he said" (v. 6). Three words that do enormous work. Jesus had said this in 16:21, 17:22-23, 20:18-19, and 26:32. The angel's proof is not a new argument; it is that the man kept his word.
  • They leave with "fear and great joy" (v. 8) and they run. Matthew keeps both emotions. Reverence and gladness are not opposites in this chapter.
  • They take hold of his feet (v. 9). Not a vision, not a ghost, not a felt presence. Feet you can grip. And what they do while gripping them is worship.
  • He calls the disciples "my brothers" (v. 10). Every one of them had fled (26:56) and the loudest had denied him three times. The first word about them from the risen Christ is a family word.
  • The guards go to the priests, not to Pilate (v. 11). They report to the men who wanted the grave sealed, and the money comes out again (v. 12), just as it did for Judas (26:15).
  • Matthew tells you the counter-story is still circulating (v. 15). "This story has been spread among the Jews to this day." He is writing while people are still repeating it, and he answers it by printing it.

Interpretation

Why was the stone moved, if he had already risen?

"And behold, there was a great earthquake, for an angel of the Lord descended from heaven and came and rolled back the stone and sat on it." Matthew 28:2, ESV

Read verse 6 carefully and you will see the order of events: "He is not here, for he has risen." He was already gone when the stone moved. The angel does not open a door for Jesus; he opens a door for witnesses. MacArthur on chapter 28 puts it exactly right: the stone was rolled back "not to let Jesus out, for if he could rise from the dead, he would need no help escaping an earthly tomb, but to let the women and the apostles in."

That is why the angel says, "Come, see the place where he lay." God's first move on Easter morning is to make the evidence public. And notice who is flat on the ground while the invitation is being issued: the professional witnesses Rome posted, "like dead men" (v. 4), traumatized by the very event they were stationed there to disprove. The soldiers assigned to keep the tomb shut are the only people in the scene who see nothing.

Why does it matter that the women are the first witnesses?

"And behold, Jesus met them and said, 'Greetings!' And they came up and took hold of his feet and worshiped him." Matthew 28:9, ESV

Take the historical point first, because it is one of the strongest single observations in the whole resurrection account. In first-century Jewish and Roman society, a woman's testimony carried little or no legal weight; in many settings women could not serve as witnesses in court at all. That is not a compliment to the culture, it is a description of it. Now suppose you are inventing a resurrection story in that world and you want skeptics to believe it. You would put the discovery in the hands of men with standing, ideally men whose word a court would accept. You would never, ever build your case on two women.

Matthew builds it on two women. So do all four Gospels. The most natural explanation for why the writers kept a detail that weakened their case in the eyes of their own contemporaries is that it is what happened, and they were not free to improve it. Historians call this kind of evidence a criterion of embarrassment: material that a fabricator would have removed.

Then look at what the women do with his feet. They "took hold" of them. Whatever a risen body is, it is a body. It has feet that can be grasped and held. This closes off the idea, then and now, that the resurrection was a shared vision or an inner conviction that the cause lives on. Matthew is not describing an experience the disciples had; he is describing a man they touched. And their response to touching him is worship, which in this Gospel is offered to God (4:10) and now, without a word of correction from Jesus, is offered to him.

Why "my brothers", and why is the counter-story worth printing?

"Then Jesus said to them, 'Do not be afraid; go and tell my brothers to go to Galilee, and there they will see me.'" Matthew 28:10, ESV

Count what these men had done. All of them fell asleep while he prayed in agony. All of them ran when the swords came out. Peter swore he had never met the man. There has been no apology, no meeting, no confession; nothing has been repaired yet. And the first thing the risen Lord says about them is "my brothers." MacArthur's note is a single line, and it is enough: "my brothers. I.e., the disciples." The forgiveness arrives before the conversation does. If you have ever assumed you have to get your repentance polished before Christ will own you, verse 10 was written for you.

Now the other story, running down the same road at the same hour (vv. 11-15). The guards report the truth to the priests. The priests hold a meeting, and out comes "a sufficient sum of money," the same word for silver that bought Judas (26:15). The instruction is: say the disciples came by night and stole him away while we were asleep. Sit with the absurdity for a second. These are the only witnesses, and they are being paid to testify to something they say they slept through. MacArthur is blunt: "The story was obviously bogus, and not a very good cover-up. They could not possibly know what had happened while they were asleep." Add that the penalty for a Roman guard sleeping on duty was severe, which is exactly why the priests have to promise, "if this comes to the governor's ears, we will satisfy him" (v. 14).

Here is the part worth writing in your margin: the enemies' own story concedes the central fact. Nobody in Jerusalem is saying the body is still in the tomb. The priests, who had every motive and the legal standing to produce a corpse and end the movement in an afternoon, instead purchase an explanation for why the grave is empty. And Matthew's aside that this story "has been spread among the Jews to this day" (v. 15) tells you why chapter 27 ended with a guard and a seal in the first place: he has been building the answer to this exact rumor for two chapters, so that when you hear it, you already know who paid for it.

Application

  • About God: he keeps his word to the day. "He has risen, as he said" is not only the best news in the book, it is the proof that every other thing Jesus said stands. A man who predicts his own resurrection and then performs it has no unreliable sentences.
  • About you: your standing with Christ does not wait on your performance. Deserters were called brothers before they said a word. The unbelief in this chapter is also worth watching: the priests have the evidence and buy a story instead, which is a reminder that hard hearts are not short of facts.
  • Do: tell one person today, out loud, that Jesus rose from the dead. That is literally the assignment given in verse 7, "go quickly and tell." Text a friend, say it at dinner, or say it to your own household. If you are carrying something you think has disqualified you from him, read verse 10 aloud with your own name in place of "my brothers."

Church prompt
Ask someone at your church what they would say to a friend who asked, "how do you know Jesus actually rose?" Then offer the two things you learned today: the women as first witnesses, which no first-century author would invent, and the fact that the earliest opposing story already admits the tomb was empty. See whether those are new to them. Most Christians have never been shown that the oldest counter-explanation on record is itself evidence.

Questions welcome, always: "how do the four Gospel accounts of the morning fit together?", "if the guards were unconscious, how did they know anything to report?", "what kind of body did Jesus have if it could be touched?" Ask your teacher.

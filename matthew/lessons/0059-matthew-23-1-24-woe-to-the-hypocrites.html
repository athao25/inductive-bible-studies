Lesson 0059 · Unit 6: Chapters 19-23

Matthew 23:1-24: Woe to the Hypocrites

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 23:1-24 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 23:1-24 twice in your ESV yet, stop and do it now. Write at least five observations: note who Jesus is speaking to in verse 1, count how many times the word "woe" appears, find the one clause in verse 23 that most readers skip, and notice what verse 3 tells the crowd to keep doing.
Read this chapter honestly
This is the most severe public address Jesus ever gave, and it is uncomfortable. Two things to hold from the first line. First, the target is named: "the scribes and the Pharisees" (v. 2), a religious leadership class, not Jews as such. Jesus was Jewish. His disciples were Jewish. The earliest church was Jewish, and the crowd he is protecting in verse 1 was Jewish. Second, this way of speaking is not new: Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos, and Micah all thundered against the leaders of their own people, from inside, as sons of the covenant. Jesus stands in that line. This chapter has been ripped out of that setting and used to justify hatred of Jewish people, which is a wicked misuse of it and a reading Matthew himself could not have recognized. Read it the way it is written: God's Messiah grieving over the shepherds of God's people, and, as you will see, over the city he loves.

Recall first

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The audience shifts (v. 1). "Then Jesus said to the crowds and to his disciples". After chapters of direct debate with the leaders, he now speaks about them, publicly, to the people they teach. This is a warning to the flock as much as a verdict on the shepherds.
  • An affirmation before the attack (vv. 2-3). "Do and observe whatever they tell you". Jesus does not tell the crowd to abandon the law being taught. He splits their teaching from their lives: "for they preach, but do not practice."
  • Burdens laid, never lifted (v. 4). The verbs are physical: they "tie up" heavy burdens and "lay them on people's shoulders", but will not "move them with their finger". A finger is the smallest possible effort.
  • A stated motive (v. 5). "They do all their deeds to be seen by others." Everything that follows in verses 5-7 is evidence for that one sentence: broad phylacteries, long fringes, the best seats, the greetings, the title.
  • Three titles, one reason each (vv. 8-10). Not rabbi, "for you have one teacher"; not father, "for you have one Father, who is in heaven"; not instructor, "for you have one instructor, the Christ." Each prohibition is grounded in someone else already holding the office.
  • The hinge sentence (vv. 11-12). "The greatest among you shall be your servant", then the reversal. Matthew has printed this before (20:26-27), and it is the standard the woes measure against.
  • A repeated formula (vv. 13, 15, 16, 23). "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites!" recurs like a refrain, twice interrupted by the variation "blind guides" (vv. 16, 24). Seven woes run through verse 36; your passage covers the first four.
  • Sizes and distances are the joke (vv. 15, 23-24). Sea and land crossed for one convert; mint, dill, and cumin weighed out; a gnat strained and a camel swallowed. Jesus argues by absurd proportion.

Interpretation

1. "Practice and observe whatever they tell you": how can he say that?

"The scribes and the Pharisees sit on Moses' seat, so do and observe whatever they tell you, but not the works they do. For they preach, but do not practice." Matthew 23:2-3, ESV

Moses' seat was a real thing, a literal stone seat in some synagogues, and a figure for teaching authority over the law. MacArthur on chapter 23 compares it to "a university's 'chair of philosophy'" and notes the phrase may be rendered "[they] have seated themselves in Moses' seat", which would make the authority self-claimed rather than granted. He also supplies the necessary limit on verse 3: obey them "insofar as it accords with the word of God", since Jesus has already condemned their extrabiblical traditions outright (15:3-9).

That limit is right, and it keeps verse 3 from contradicting chapter 15. But do not soften what remains. Jesus tells a crowd to keep obeying Scripture even when it comes out of a corrupt mouth, because the authority sits in the text, not in the man reading it. This is why hypocrisy in leaders, real as it is, is never a valid reason to stop obeying God. The word hypocrite is the Greek word for a stage actor, someone playing a part behind a mask. The mask is the charge; the script itself may still be true.

2. "Call no man your father": a ban on words, or on a posture?

"But you are not to be called rabbi, for you have one teacher, and you are all brothers. And call no man your father on earth, for you have one Father, who is in heaven. Neither be called instructors, for you have one instructor, the Christ." Matthew 23:8-10, ESV

These verses get used as a club, usually against Roman Catholics, and they are not built for that job. Notice the internal problem with a wooden reading: it would forbid the word "teacher" too, and Scripture itself gives that title to men (Eph 4:11), just as Paul calls himself the Corinthians' father: "I became your father in Christ Jesus through the gospel" (1 Cor 4:15). Paul is not violating a command from his Lord. He is doing exactly what Jesus permits, and using an affectionate word for a real relationship. MacArthur states it cleanly: "Here Jesus condemns pride and pretense, not titles per se... Christ is merely forbidding the use of such names as spiritual titles, or in an ostentatious sense that accords undue spiritual authority to a human being, as if that person were the source of truth rather than God."

So the point is a reservation, not a vocabulary rule. Ultimate spiritual authority, ultimate fatherhood, ultimate instruction: those belong to God, and any human office that starts absorbing them has stopped being an office and become an idol. The sober version of this test is not what your pastor is called. It is whether anyone in your life functions as the final word, the one whose judgment you would follow past Scripture. If so, the warning lands, whatever the word on the nameplate. And the corrective is right there in the same sentence: "you are all brothers."

3. Woes, gnats, and the clause everyone skips

Hear the word "woe" correctly before you read the four. In the prophets it is a cry as much as a sentence, the sound made over a funeral (see Isa 5:8-23, or the whole book of Lamentations behind it). It means "how terrible for you", spoken by someone who is not enjoying this. Verse 37, next lesson, will prove the tone: "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem... how often would I have gathered your children together." Judgment and grief are the same voice here.

First (v. 13), the shut door. "You shut the kingdom of heaven in people's faces. For you neither enter yourselves nor allow those who would enter to go in." Catch the irony: they are standing in a doorway they refuse to walk through, blocking it with their bodies. MacArthur ties it to Romans 10:3, men "seeking to establish a righteousness of their own", whose legalism "effectively obscured the narrow gate". (Your Bible may footnote a verse 14 about devouring widows' houses; the earliest manuscripts of Matthew lack it, and it appears in Mark 12:40.)

Second (v. 15), the wrong-way missionary. "You travel across sea and land to make a single proselyte" (a proselyte is a Gentile convert to Judaism), "and when he becomes a proselyte, you make him twice as much a child of hell as yourselves." This one should unsettle any zealous church. The energy is admirable: oceans crossed, cost paid, one soul sought. The verdict is still damning, because evangelistic effort does not sanctify the message it carries. You can be tireless and be multiplying your own error.

Third (vv. 16-22), the oath game. Swear by the temple, not binding; swear by the gold of the temple, binding. Swear by the altar, nothing; swear by the gift on it, binding. MacArthur calls it "an arbitrary distinction... which gave them a sanctimonious justification for lying with impunity." Jesus attacks the logic at its root: the temple sanctifies the gold, the altar sanctifies the gift, and behind both stands "him who dwells in it" (v. 21). They had inverted the direction of holiness, making the money holier than the house of God, which is exactly what you would expect from men who love the best seats. And the whole system existed to let a man appear to swear while keeping an escape hatch. Compare 5:33-37: let your yes be yes.

Fourth (vv. 23-24), mint, dill, and cumin. This is the famous one, and it is almost always quoted at half length.

"Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you tithe mint and dill and cumin, and have neglected the weightier matters of the law: justice and mercy and faithfulness. These you ought to have done, without neglecting the others." Matthew 23:23, ESV

They were tithing garden herbs, which the tithe law (Lev 27:30) was not really written to cover; MacArthur pictures them "perhaps even counting individual dill seeds". Meanwhile justice, mercy, and faithfulness went unweighed. Now read the last clause slowly: "These you ought to have done, without neglecting the others." Jesus does not sneer at the dill. He does not say small obedience is worthless, or that people who care about details are the problem. He refuses the trade. The hypocrite's move is to buy the cheap obedience and use it as currency against the expensive one, and Jesus simply declines the transaction: do both. That clause is a mercy to anyone who has quietly assumed that caring about the little commands is legalism. It is not. Neglecting justice while you do them is.

Then the image, and it is deliberately funny: "You blind guides, straining out a gnat and swallowing a camel!" Some Pharisees strained wine through cloth to avoid ingesting a gnat, the smallest unclean creature (Lev 11:23). The camel is the largest unclean animal (Lev 11:4). Picture the man delicately filtering his cup and then, without pausing, swallowing a camel whole, humps and legs and all. Jesus is drawing a cartoon. He does it because ridicule can get past a defense that argument cannot, and because self-righteousness is, finally, absurd.

Application

  • About God He is not indifferent to how his people are led. The severity here is not a loss of temper; it is love for a crowd being crushed under burdens their teachers would not lift with one finger.
  • About you The trade Jesus refuses is the one you are most likely to make: precision in the cheap obediences, silence in the costly ones. Ask which of your disciplines are visible and which are not, and notice which list is longer.
  • Do Pick one of the three weightier matters, justice, mercy, or faithfulness, and take a concrete step today: pay something you owe, forgive a debt you are entitled to collect, keep a promise you have been quietly letting slide. One thing, done today, in the category you find expensive.

Church prompt
Verses 5-7 are about visibility: the seats, the greetings, the title. Ask yourself honestly this week, and if you can, ask a friend at church to answer it about you: what do people at my church know me for, and did I arrange it that way? Then the harder half, which is not about you: is there anyone in your congregation carrying a burden the leadership has laid on them without offering a finger to help lift it? If so, the response Jesus models is not gossip about the leaders, it is going and lifting.

Questions welcome, always: "does verse 9 mean I cannot call a priest father?", "if the Pharisees taught rightly, what exactly was wrong with their teaching?", "how do I tell conviction about small obedience from legalism?" Ask your teacher.

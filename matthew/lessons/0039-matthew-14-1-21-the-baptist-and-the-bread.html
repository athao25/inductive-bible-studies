Lesson 0039 · Unit 5: Chapters 14-18

Matthew 14:1-21: The Baptist and the Bread

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 14:1-21 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
Stop here if you have not read Matthew 14:1-21 twice in your ESV and written at least five observations. Unit 5 opens today, and it opens with two banquets set side by side. Read the whole span in one sitting so you feel the seam at v. 13, and resist the urge to treat vv. 1-12 as a detour before the miracle you already know. Mark every mention of food, and mark who is eating with whom.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Herod's conclusion is a confession (vv. 1-2). He hears about Jesus and reaches for the one explanation that should terrify him: "This is John the Baptist. He has been raised from the dead." Nobody asked him. The guilty man supplies the name of his own victim, unprompted, to his servants.
  • The story is told in flashback (vv. 3-12). Verse 3 reaches backward, "For Herod had seized John and bound him." John is already dead when the chapter opens. Matthew is not reporting news; he is explaining a haunting.
  • The charge against Herod is a Bible verse (v. 4). "It is not lawful for you to have her." John's crime was quoting the law of God to a ruler about his marriage. That is the whole indictment.
  • Herod is afraid of the wrong thing (v. 5). He wanted to kill John but feared the crowd, "because they held him to be a prophet." Public opinion restrains him where conscience does not.
  • The setting is a birthday party (vv. 6-8). A dance, a delighted host, a reckless oath, and a girl coached by her mother. The request is specific and grotesque: the head, on a platter, here, at the table. A serving dish is the murder weapon's delivery vehicle.
  • The stated motive is embarrassment (v. 9). "The king was sorry, but because of his oaths and his guests he commanded it to be given." A prophet dies so a man does not have to look foolish in front of dinner guests.
  • John's disciples do two things (v. 12). They bury the body, and "they went and told Jesus." That sentence is the hinge of the chapter.
  • Jesus withdraws, and the crowd follows (v. 13). He goes by boat to "a desolate place by himself"; they go on foot from the towns and arrive first. His grief is interrupted before it starts.
  • Count the numbers in vv. 17-21. Five loaves, two fish, twelve baskets, five thousand men "besides women and children." Two of those numbers are small, two are not, and the last one is not the crowd's actual size.

Interpretation

Why does Matthew put a beheading next to a picnic?

At that time Herod the tetrarch heard about the fame of Jesus, and he said to his servants, "This is John the Baptist. He has been raised from the dead; that is why these miraculous powers are at work in him." Matthew 14:1-2, ESV

Matthew is a careful arranger, and here he sets two feasts back to back and lets you compare them. Herod's is indoors, exclusive, expensive, lubricated by wine and vanity, and it ends with a corpse. Jesus' is outdoors, open to anyone who walked there, catered with a boy's lunch, and it ends with more food than it started with. One king spends a life to protect his reputation. The other spends himself to feed strangers. You are being shown two kinds of kingship, and the passage never says so out loud.

A tetrarch is one of four rulers over a divided territory, and this Herod is Herod Antipas, son of the Herod who killed the infants in chapter 2. Matthew still calls him "king" in v. 9 because that is what Galileans called him. MacArthur on chapter 14 untangles the family: Herodias was Antipas' niece and his brother Philip's wife, so the marriage compounded incest and violated Leviticus 18:16, which is exactly what John said. On the oath in v. 9 MacArthur is blunt and right: "Herod was widely known for his duplicity, so it was not honesty that he was concerned about, but rather the appearance of things. He did not want to be embarrassed in front of his dinner guests." Herod keeps a wicked promise because breaking it would cost him face. That is not integrity. That is cowardice with good manners.

Notice also what this does to the political weather. The man who killed the forerunner now has Jesus on his mind. From here on the menace is real and closing, and Matthew wants you to read the rest of the Gospel knowing that the state has already shown what it does to prophets.

Why does Jesus give the disciples an impossible command?

Now when Jesus heard this, he withdrew from there in a boat to a desolate place by himself. But when the crowds heard it, they followed him on foot from the towns. Matthew 14:13, ESV

Do not rush past the withdrawal. Jesus has just been told that his kinsman and forerunner was executed at a party, and he pulls away to be alone. This is grief, genuine and human, and the Gospel records it without apology. Then the crowd arrives, and v. 14 says he had compassion on them and healed their sick. He does not send them away to protect his sorrow. He absorbs the interruption.

Evening comes, and the disciples make the only sensible proposal available: send them away to buy food (v. 15). It is not callous. It is logistics. Jesus answers with one short clause that breaks the logistics open.

But Jesus said, "They need not go away; you give them something to eat." Matthew 14:16, ESV

He knows they cannot. MacArthur's note on this verse is worth having in front of you: Jesus "wanted the disciples to state it plainly so the record would be clear that a miracle by his power occurred." The command exists to produce the inventory in v. 17, five loaves and two fish, so that nobody can later explain the meal away. But there is a second thing happening. The bread passes through their hands. He gives it to the disciples and the disciples give it to the crowds (v. 19). They are not spectators at the miracle; they are the distribution. That is a picture of ministry with the pressure taken off: the supply is never yours, the handing out always is.

What does a meal in the wilderness say about who Jesus is?

Then he ordered the crowds to sit down on the grass, and taking the five loaves and the two fish, he looked up to heaven and said a blessing. Then he broke the loaves and gave them to the disciples, and the disciples gave them to the crowds. Matthew 14:19, ESV

Four verbs, in order: took, blessed, broke, gave. Hold that sequence, because Matthew uses it again in exactly this order at the table on the last night.

Now as they were eating, Jesus took bread, and after blessing it broke it and gave it to the disciples, and said, "Take, eat; this is my body." Matthew 26:26, ESV

The repetition is deliberate. The hillside meal is a rehearsal, and it teaches the same lesson from the other end: bread that multiplies because he breaks it, and later a body broken so that a multitude is fed. This is the only miracle recorded in all four Gospels, which tells you the early church could not stop telling it.

Then the setting. A desolate place, a hungry multitude, and bread from heaven at the end of the day. Israel ate manna in the wilderness because God fed them there (Exodus 16), and every Jew in that crowd grew up on the story. Jesus does not cite it; he performs it. What God did for Israel through Moses, Jesus does with his own hands, and he does not ration. Twelve baskets are left over, one for each of the twelve men who said it could not be done. Sufficiency would have been a miracle. Surplus is a sermon.

One more thread. Matthew says he "had compassion on them" (v. 14), the same verb he used in 9:36, where the reason is spelled out: the crowds were "harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd." Mark's account of this same day says it outright (Mark 6:34). So the shepherd sits the sheep down on green grass and feeds them, which is Psalm 23 acted out in front of five thousand men. The kingdom of heaven arrives here not as a decree but as dinner.

Application

  • About God He grieves and he gives, in the same afternoon, without either one canceling the other. And he is not stingy: the God of this passage ends with leftovers. Whatever else you believe about him, do not believe he is calculating the minimum.
  • About you Herod is not a monster you could never become. He is a man who knew what was right, felt sorry about it, and then did the wrong thing because of who was watching. Ask where the opinion of a room has already decided something for you that Scripture had decided differently.
  • Do Feed someone today with what is actually in your hands. Not a plan, not a donation you will make later: a meal, a coffee, groceries left on a porch, one specific person before you sleep. Five loaves were enough because they were handed over.

Church prompt
Next time your church takes the Lord's Supper, watch the minister's hands and count the four actions. Then ask someone afterward what they think the connection is between the hillside in Matthew 14 and the table in Matthew 26. It is a good conversation because it is not controversial, it is just noticing, and most believers have never had the two placed side by side.

Questions welcome, always: "why did God let John die in prison after the Jordan and the voice from heaven?", "did the crowd know a miracle had happened, or just that they were full?", "is the feeding meant to teach us about communion, or am I reading that in?" Ask your teacher.

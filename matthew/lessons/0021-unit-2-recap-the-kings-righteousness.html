The Sermon in one view

Compare your own sense of the shape with this. Matthew's first discourse is not a loose anthology of sayings; it is an argued whole, and every part you studied has a job:

  • The portrait (5:1-12). The Beatitudes do not open with commands but with a description. Poor in spirit, mourning, meek, hungry for righteousness, merciful, pure, peacemaking, persecuted: this is the citizen of the kingdom of heaven, and every trait is receptive before it is active. Blessedness is announced, not earned.
  • The identity (5:13-16). Before a single demand, Jesus tells them what they already are: salt and light. Influence is assumed, visibility is assumed, and the point of the good works is that the Father gets the glory (5:16).
  • The thesis (5:17-20). Here the Sermon states its own argument. Christ came to fulfill the Law and the Prophets, not to abolish them, and the righteousness of the kingdom must exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees. Everything after this verse is that thesis being unpacked.
  • The six antitheses (5:21-48). Six times: "You have heard that it was said... But I say to you." Murder becomes anger, adultery becomes lust, divorce, oaths, retaliation, and love of neighbor becomes love of enemy. Notice the direction of every move: outward act driven inward to the heart, and the section caps at "You therefore must be perfect" (5:48).
  • The three acts of piety (6:1-18). Same structure three times: giving, praying, fasting; the hypocrites do it to be seen, you do it in secret, and your Father who sees in secret rewards you. If 5:21-48 attacks a shallow ethic, this attacks a theatrical one.
  • The center (6:9-13). The Lord's Prayer sits inside the middle panel of that triad, at the structural heart of the whole Sermon. Six petitions: three for God's name, kingdom, and will, then three for bread, forgiveness, and deliverance. The kingdom's righteousness has a prayer at its core, not a technique.
  • Treasure and anxiety (6:19-34). Two treasuries, two eyes, two masters, and then the long argument from birds and lilies against worry, resolved by the command to seek first the kingdom and his righteousness (6:33). The heart's direction, not just the hand's action.
  • Judging and asking (7:1-12). The speck and the log, the dogs and the pearls, then ask, seek, knock, and the golden rule sealed with "for this is the Law and the Prophets" (7:12), which deliberately echoes 5:17 and closes the frame.
  • The four closing binaries (7:13-27). Two gates and two ways, two trees with two kinds of fruit, two claims on the last day ("Lord, Lord" versus doing the Father's will), and two builders with two foundations. No third option is offered anywhere.
  • The reaction (7:28-29). The crowds are astonished, and Matthew tells you why: "he was teaching them as one who had authority, and not as their scribes." The Sermon ends by pointing not at its content but at its Speaker.

Interpretation

What is the Sermon actually for?

Christians who agree that this is the word of God have read its purpose in three main ways. Take each seriously.

Reading one: an impossible ideal. The Sermon sets a standard no one can meet (5:48, "You therefore must be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect"), so its function is to crush self-righteousness and drive the hearer to grace. Nobody is meant to do it; they are meant to despair of doing it. This reading has real textual footing, and it is close to what MacArthur on chapter 5 says at the head of the discourse: the Sermon is "a masterful exposition of the law", showing "that its demands were humanly impossible", which "closes off every possible avenue of human merit". Its weakness is the ending. A sermon designed only to produce despair does not close with a builder who hears and does and stands.

Reading two: an interim or kingdom-age ethic. Older dispensational writers argued that this is the constitution of the millennial kingdom, offered to Israel and postponed when the King was rejected, so it applies fully to a future age rather than to the church now. The motive is honest: they were protecting the gospel of grace from being turned into a new legalism. But note carefully, because it matters for reading your own study Bible fairly: MacArthur's notes do not take that route. He treats the Sermon as present exposition of the moral law aimed at real holiness and real conversion, and at 6:33 he glosses "the kingdom" as "the sphere of salvation" rather than a postponed political order. The strongest objection to reading two is textual: Jesus addresses persecution, courts, taxes, hypocrites, and false prophets, none of which fit a consummated kingdom.

Reading three (this course's reading): the charter of life under the King, now. The Sermon is genuinely binding on disciples in this age, and genuinely impossible apart from the new birth and the Spirit. Both halves are required. Drop the binding half and the closing parable becomes nonsense; drop the impossible half and you have rebuilt Pharisaism with better vocabulary. Because it is impossible, it crushes self-righteousness; because it is binding, it directs discipleship. Those are not competing functions but the same word doing two things to the same person, first killing and then guiding.

Does 5:20 raise a bar or close a door?

"For I tell you, unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven." Matthew 5:20, ESV

Both, and that is the point. To the crowd, the Pharisee was the ceiling of human righteousness, so "exceeds" slams a door on anyone hoping to climb in on performance: MacArthur's note calls it "an impossible barrier to works-salvation". Yet the very next verse begins six paragraphs describing what exceeding actually looks like in anger, lust, speech, and enemies. If 5:20 were only a door slammed, 5:21-48 would be cruel. Read it as the hinge: the righteousness required cannot be produced, only received (see the first Beatitude, 5:3), and once received it is real, internal, and visible. Note too what kind of exceeding is meant. Not more rules kept, but a different kind of righteousness, one that reaches the heart the Pharisee left untouched.

Why does it end with a building code?

"Everyone then who hears these words of mine and does them will be like a wise man who built his house on the rock." Matthew 7:24, ESV

This is the sentence that decides the question above. Jesus does not end by saying "everyone who despairs of these words", nor "everyone who will keep these words in the age to come". He ends with hearing plus doing, in the present tense, as the difference between a house that stands and a house that falls with a great fall. Both builders heard the same sermon; only one was changed by it. That is why this course reads the Sermon as a charter rather than merely a mirror. It is a mirror first, in 5:20 and 5:48, and it never stops being one; but a mirror is not what you build a house on. Notice also the audacity, which the crowd caught immediately in 7:28-29: obedience to these words of mine is what makes a life stand at the judgment. Only the King himself can talk that way.

Application

  • About God He does not lower his standard to what you can reach (5:48), and he does not leave you at the standard without help (7:7-11). The same Father who demands perfection is the one you are taught to call Father in 6:9.
  • About you You are somewhere on the map you just reviewed. Most of us are secretly comfortable with one panel of it and quietly avoiding another. The panel you would rather not reread is probably the one addressed to you.
  • Do Pick the single sentence from Matthew 5-7 that most convicted you across these ten lessons. Write it out by hand on a card today, and name one specific action it requires of you before you sleep tonight. One sentence, one action.

Read them side by side and ask the question this lesson asked: does his framing make the Sermon a mirror only, or a mirror and a blueprint? For preaching, his Sermon on the Mount series is in the GTY Matthew archive; the messages on 7:24-27 are the best test of how he holds the two halves together.
Church prompt
Ask someone at church this week which of the three readings above they were taught, and whether they were ever told the Sermon was "not for today". It is a common thing to have absorbed without noticing. Listen for how they handle 7:24. This is a good conversation, not an argument to win.

Questions welcome, always: "if the standard is impossible, in what sense am I commanded to keep it?", "why is the Lord's Prayer in the middle instead of the end?", "did Jesus preach this once or many times?" Ask your teacher.

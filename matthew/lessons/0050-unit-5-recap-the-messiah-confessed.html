Matthew 14-18: The Messiah Confessed

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 14-18 as a whole (ESV) · Unit 5 recap

Did you reread Matthew 14-18?
No new passage today. Before you go further, read straight through Matthew 14, 15, 16, 17, and 18 in one sitting. It takes about thirty minutes. Do not stop to study; read for the shape of the whole. Jot down two things as you go: every place someone says out loud who Jesus is, and the exact verse where the story stops moving outward toward crowds and starts moving toward Jerusalem. The architecture of Unit 5

Unit 4 collected the responses people made to Jesus. Unit 5 narrows to one response, from one man, in one place, and then bends the entire Gospel around it. Everything in chapters 14 through 16 climbs toward Caesarea Philippi. Everything from 16:21 forward is shaped by the cross. Find that hinge and the unit stops being a string of episodes and becomes a single argument.

The climb: what sort of man is this?

Watch the disciples' own words about Jesus tighten across the Gospel. Three moments, three stages.

  • A question, in the boat (8:27). After the storm: "What sort of man is this, that even winds and sea obey him?" They have a category problem. They do not know what he is.
  • Worship, in the boat (14:33). After he walks on the water and catches Peter: "And those in the boat worshiped him, saying, 'Truly you are the Son of God.'" Six chapters later, the same setting, and the question has become a statement, with worship attached.
  • A name, at Caesarea Philippi (16:16). "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God." Peter now uses the title. Messiah, the anointed King the prophets promised, is no longer a rumor in the crowd (16:14). It is a confession from inside the circle, and Jesus says flesh and blood did not reveal it (16:17).

Notice what Matthew stacks under that climb. Two feedings, one Jewish and one Gentile (14:13-21; 15:32-39). A rescue on the sea. A Gentile mother whose faith Jesus calls great (15:28). A running argument with the Pharisees about tradition, defilement, and signs (15:1-20; 16:1-12). The evidence is piled up, and then the question is put: "But who do you say that I am?"

The hinge: from that time

From that time Jesus began to show his disciples that he must go to Jerusalem and suffer many things from the elders and chief priests and scribes, and be killed, and on the third day be raised. Matthew 16:21, ESV

Matthew uses the phrase "from that time" only twice in the Gospel, at 4:17 where the preaching of the kingdom begins and here. MacArthur on chapter 16 reads it the same way: "This marks the beginning of a new emphasis in Matthew's account," a turn from public ministry to private instruction that "took on a new, somber tone." The confession does not conclude the unit. It unlocks the next subject. Only once they can say Christ does he tell them what Christ has come to do.

The descent toward Jerusalem

  • First passion prediction and its price (16:21-28). Suffer, be killed, be raised. Then the cost is transferred to them: deny yourself, take up your cross, follow me. Your memory verse for this unit comes from the far side of the hinge, not the near side.
  • The transfiguration as confirmation (17:1-13). Six days later, three of them see the glory, Moses and Elijah appear, and the Father says, "listen to him." The order matters. He tells them about the cross first, and then shows them who is carrying it. Glory is given as reassurance before suffering, not as an escape from it.
  • Second passion prediction (17:22-23). "The Son of Man is about to be delivered into the hands of men, and they will kill him, and he will be raised on the third day." The response this time is not argument but grief: "they were greatly distressed."
  • A discourse for cross-bearers (18:1-35). The fourth of Matthew's five teaching blocks (see discourse) lands here, and its subject is how such a community treats its own. Become like children. Do not cause a little one to stumble. Go after the one that strays. Confront sin privately first, and aim at restoration. Forgive seventy-seven times. That teaching is unlivable for people chasing greatness; it is the only sane arrangement for people who have just been told to carry a cross.

So the unit is symmetrical. Chapters 14 to 16:20 answer who he is. Chapters 16:21 to 18 answer what that means for him and for anyone who follows him.

Interpretation

Why does confession keep collapsing into failure?

Read the unit again for the pattern and it becomes almost comic, except that Matthew is not laughing.

  • Peter walks on water and then sinks, and hears "O you of little faith, why did you doubt?" (14:29-31).
  • Peter confesses Christ and is blessed for it, and six verses later hears "Get behind me, Satan!" (16:16-23).
  • Peter sees the glory of God on the mountain and starts talking about building tents (17:4), and the Father interrupts him mid-sentence.
  • The disciples watch five thousand fed, then four thousand fed, then argue about having forgotten bread (16:5-12).
  • Three come down from the transfiguration to find the other nine unable to help one afflicted boy (17:14-20).

Five times in five chapters is not accident; it is composition. Matthew is telling you that the men who got the confession right are the same men who cannot hold it steady for a week. And he is refusing to clean it up. He does not hide Peter's rebuke, and Peter, who almost certainly stands behind much of this material, did not ask him to.

Take the pastoral point plainly. If genuine faith looked like unbroken competence, no one in this chapter list qualifies. What Jesus does with the failures is instructive: he catches Peter before he corrects him (14:31), he rebukes and keeps him, he calls the disciples faithless in 17:17 and then hands them a mustard seed as the working measure of faith in 17:20. MacArthur notes that the disciples' problem at 17:19 was probably "a failure to make God, rather than their own gifts, the object of their confidence." That is a diagnosis you can use. The remedy for sinking is not a stronger grip; it is a nearer Christ. So do not be embarrassed by this unit, and do not be embarrassed by yourself. Matthew was not.

Why do Gentiles keep showing up in the middle of all this?

She said, "Yes, Lord, yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters' table." Then Jesus answered her, "O woman, great is your faith! Be it done for you as you desire." Matthew 15:27-28, ESV

Only two people in this entire Gospel are told their faith is great, and both are Gentiles: the centurion in 8:10 and this Canaanite mother. She is not from the covenant people, she has no claim, she says so, and she gets everything she asked for.

Then watch the geography. Jesus goes around the eastern side of Galilee into Gentile territory, heals crowds there, "and they glorified the God of Israel" (15:31), and feeds four thousand of them from seven loaves. MacArthur's own note is helpful here: Christ ended his Galilean ministry with the five thousand and "ended his ministry in the Gentile regions by feeding the 4,000." Two feedings, two peoples, one table.

None of this is a change of plan. Jesus states the priority honestly in 15:24, the lost sheep of the house of Israel first, and the widening is inside the promise, not a departure from it. Matthew has been signaling it since the magi came to a Jewish baby in chapter 2. It arrives at the last verses of the book: "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations" (28:19). Keep the thread in view for the rest of the course. A King who feeds four thousand outsiders halfway through will not end the book with a border.

Application

  • About God He asked for the confession before he explained the cross, and he showed the glory before he walked into the suffering. He is not careless with people he is about to ask hard things of. The same Lord who says "take up your cross" is the one who reached out his hand immediately and caught a sinking man.
  • About you Peter is not the cautionary tale of this unit; he is the portrait of an ordinary believer. Right confession, unstable follow-through, kept anyway. If your Christian life has looked like alternating clarity and collapse, that is not evidence you were never converted. It is evidence you are in chapter 16 with everyone else.
  • Do Today, write out Matthew 16:24 by hand and under it name one specific thing this week that "deny himself" actually means for you: a purchase, an argument you want to win, an hour, a grudge from chapter 18 you are still holding. One item, named, dated. Then do that one thing before you sleep.

Church prompt
Matthew 18 is the New Testament's clearest procedure for handling sin between believers, and most Christians have never seen step one used. Ask your elders how it actually works in your church: who goes privately, what counts as "listening," and what restoration looks like when it succeeds, since success in this passage is "you have gained your brother," not a verdict. Then ask a friend the harder version: is there a step-one conversation you have been avoiding by telling other people about it instead?

Questions welcome, always: "why did Jesus call the Canaanite woman a dog?", "if Peter confessed rightly in 16:16, how could he be Satan's mouthpiece in 16:23?", "does 'take up your cross' mean every Christian should expect to suffer?" Ask your teacher.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • A repeated two-part formula (vv. 21, 27, 31). "You have heard that it was said ... But I say to you." Three times here, and it will sound three more times before the chapter ends (vv. 33, 38, 43). Six in all. Whatever else is happening, Matthew wants you to feel a pattern.
  • He quotes the Ten Commandments (vv. 21, 27). "You shall not murder" (Ex 20:13) and "You shall not commit adultery" (Ex 20:14), sixth and seventh commandments, both quoted exactly. Jesus picks the commands nobody in the crowd had broken.
  • The verb moves inside (vv. 22, 28). Murder becomes anger and insult; adultery becomes a look "with lustful intent." In both cases the external act is not denied, it is traced back to its source.
  • Three escalating penalties, one target (v. 22). Angry: liable to judgment. "Raca" (the ESV renders it "insults"): liable to the council. "You fool!": liable to the hell of fire. The offenses barely differ; the escalation is rhetorical, and the point is that contempt is capital.
  • Two illustrations, both about hurry (vv. 23-26). One at the altar in Jerusalem, one on the road to court. Different settings, same verb pressure: "leave your gift ... go ... first be reconciled" and "come to terms quickly." Worship gets interrupted; a lawsuit gets settled out of court.
  • The brother is the one with the grievance (v. 23). Not "you have something against your brother" but "your brother has something against you." The offender is sent, not the offended.
  • Body parts and drastic verbs (vv. 29-30). Right eye, right hand; "tear it out," "cut it off," "throw it away." Notice the reason given each time: "it is better that you lose one of your members than that your whole body be thrown into hell."
  • The divorce paragraph opens differently (v. 31). Not "you have heard that it was said" but "It was also said," and what follows is not a commandment but a fragment of Deuteronomy 24:1, quoted the way people used it: "let him give her a certificate of divorce."

Interpretation

Is Jesus correcting Moses?

"You have heard that it was said to those of old, 'You shall not murder; and whoever murders will be liable to judgment.' But I say to you that everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment." Matthew 5:21-22, ESV

No. He said so two verses before this: "I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them" (5:17). Watch his wording. He never says "it is written," the formula he used against Satan three times in chapter 4. He says "you have heard that it was said." What people heard was the synagogue reading plus the accumulated rabbinic commentary on it, and that commentary had a habit of stopping at the surface. MacArthur on chapter 5 puts it plainly: "Jesus was not altering the terms of the law in any of these passages. Rather, he was correcting what they had 'heard'—the rabbinical understanding of the law." The clue is in v. 21 itself: "you shall not murder" is Scripture, but "whoever murders will be liable to judgment" is courtroom procedure, the human system that could only ever prosecute the act.

These six paragraphs are usually called the antitheses, from a Greek word for setting one thing against another. The name is a little misleading. Jesus is not setting himself against Moses; he is setting the law's true reach against a reading that had shrunk it to what a court can prove.

Now feel the second half of the formula. "But I say to you." Every rabbi of that era argued by citation: Rabbi Hillel said, and Rabbi Shammai replied, and it was taught in the name of Rabbi so-and-so. Jesus cites nobody. He puts his own bare "I" over against the accumulated authority of every teacher in Israel, and he does it while expounding the law God gave at Sinai. Either that is blasphemous arrogance or he is the Lawgiver himself standing on a hillside explaining what he meant. Chapter 7 records the crowd's reaction: "he was teaching them as one who had authority, and not as their scribes" (7:29). Keep that in view; it is the real subject of the whole section.

If anger is murder and a look is adultery, what am I supposed to do with my eyes?

"But I say to you that everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart." Matthew 5:28, ESV

Read v. 28 precisely, because it is regularly misquoted. It is not the first glance, and it is not the involuntary noticing that eyes do. The Greek phrase behind "with lustful intent" describes looking in order to desire: the second look, the one you choose, the imagination you feed on purpose. Temptation is not sin; cultivating it is. The same distinction governs anger. Jesus himself was angry (Mark 3:5), and Scripture can command anger without sin (Eph 4:26). What v. 22 condemns is the anger that has curdled into contempt, the settled verdict that another image-bearer is worthless. "Raca" was an Aramaic sneer, roughly "empty-head." MacArthur glosses "You fool!" the same way, literally "empty-headed," and notes that "an abusive insult carries the same kind of moral guilt as an act of murder." Contempt is murder in seed form; it wants the person gone.

"If your right eye causes you to sin, tear it out and throw it away. For it is better that you lose one of your members than that your whole body be thrown into hell." Matthew 5:29, ESV

This is hyperbole, deliberate exaggeration used to make a point land. Two reasons we can say so with confidence. First, Jesus nowhere commends self-mutilation, and no apostle or church of the New Testament ever practiced it; the consistent apostolic cure for lust is the renewed mind and the fear of God, not the knife. Second, it would not work. A blind man can still lust, and a one-handed man can still sin, because the problem was never the eye. MacArthur says exactly this: Jesus "was not advocating self-mutilation (for this would not in fact cure lust, which is actually a problem of the heart)."

But do not let "hyperbole" become an off switch. Hyperbole is not softening, it is amplification. Jesus is saying that if amputation would save you, it would be a bargain. That is the price he puts on this sin, and it is meant to be felt. The obedience it calls for is real and unglamorous: delete the app, change the route home, hand someone your password, end the friendship that is going somewhere. Whatever your right hand is, the command is to treat it as expendable.

What exactly is Jesus settling about divorce?

"It was also said, 'Whoever divorces his wife, let him give her a certificate of divorce.' But I say to you that everyone who divorces his wife, except on the ground of sexual immorality, makes her commit adultery, and whoever marries a divorced woman commits adultery." Matthew 5:31-32, ESV

There was a live argument in first-century Judaism, and Jesus is walking straight into it. Deuteronomy 24:1 speaks of a man who divorces his wife because he "has found some indecency in her." Two rabbinic schools fought over that phrase. The school of Shammai read "indecency" narrowly, as sexual sin. The school of Hillel, by far the more popular, read it broadly, as anything that displeased the husband; the famous examples include spoiling a meal and being less attractive than another woman. In practice the Hillelite reading made divorce available on demand, and the certificate became mere paperwork. MacArthur describes the same distortion: the rabbis treated Deut 24:1 "as if it were given merely to regulate the paperwork when one sought divorce," when in fact Moses gave that certificate as a concession "to protect the woman who was divorced," not to license the man.

Jesus lands with Shammai and then goes past him. He does not debate the definition of indecency; he restores the definition of marriage, which is what he will spell out at length in 19:1-12 by going back behind Moses to Genesis. Here he gives the compressed verdict: a divorce on any other ground does not dissolve the marriage in God's sight, so the remarriage that follows is adultery. The exception clause, "except on the ground of sexual immorality," is real and is not a loophole; the Greek word porneia covers sexual unfaithfulness broadly. Notice, too, whom Jesus is protecting. In that world the discarded wife had no income and no standing. He puts the guilt on the man who discards her.

Say the pastoral part plainly, because this verse has landed on real people. Some of you were divorced against your will. Some were divorced before you knew Christ. Some divorced for reasons you now know were not these. This text is not a scarlet letter; the same Sermon that says "everyone who is angry" convicts every person reading it. If Jesus has closed off self-justification here, he has closed it off everywhere, and the cross is not smaller than a broken marriage. We will handle the hard cases carefully in lesson 0051 on Matthew 19:1-15. If this passage is bleeding today, do not sit with it alone; bring it to your pastor or your teacher this week.

A note on how the Sermon is used
Some futurist readers treat the Sermon on the Mount as the ethic of a future earthly kingdom rather than a rule for the church now. MacArthur does not, and neither does this course. He reads it as "a masterful exposition of the law and a potent assault on Pharisaic legalism," aimed at showing that the law's demands are humanly impossible so that sinners are left "dependent on nothing but divine grace." That is right, and it is not the whole. The law that drives you to grace also becomes the shape of the life grace produces. Read chapters 5-7 as both: the mirror that shows you your need, and the family portrait of the household you have been adopted into.

Application

  • About God He legislates the heart because he sees the heart. No human court can indict contempt or a cultivated look, so no human standard was ever going to save anyone. That is bad news you must feel before 5:48 and the cross make sense.
  • About you Take the honest inventory. You have probably never killed anyone and never committed adultery, and by this passage you are guilty on both counts. If the standard is the heart, self-justification is finished, which is exactly where grace can reach you.
  • Do Two things today, both small and both concrete. First, ask who has something against you (v. 23), and today send the text or make the call; do not draft the perfect message, send the imperfect one. Second, name one specific input that feeds lust or contempt, and cut it off before you sleep: delete it, block it, unfollow it, hand the password to someone. Right hand, gone.

Church prompt
Verses 23-24 assume a worshiper on his way to the altar, which for us is the Lord's Table. Before the next time your church takes communion, ask yourself whether there is a brother or sister in that room who has something against you, and go first. If you want to raise it with someone, a good question for an elder is how your congregation handles reconciliation between members, and whether anything is ever said about it before the Table.

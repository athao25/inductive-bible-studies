Lesson 0068 · Unit 7: Chapters 24-25

Matthew 25:31-46: The Sheep and the Goats

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 25:31-46 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 25:31-46 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations. Watch especially for how many times the list of six needs is repeated, for what title Jesus gives himself in verse 34, and for the reaction of both groups when the verdict is announced. Then read one verse further, 26:1, and notice the sentence that opens it.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • This is not really a parable (vv. 31-32). Chapter 25 has given you two parables in a row, so it is easy to file this as a third. It is not. It is a plain description of a coming event with one simile dropped into it: "as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats." The throne, the nations, the two destinies, and the six deeds are all stated directly. Only the shepherd is a picture.
  • The setting is total (v. 31). The Son of Man comes "in his glory, and all the angels with him," and sits. Compare 16:27 and 24:30, where the same three elements appeared as prediction. Here they are simply assumed and the scene moves on to what happens next.
  • Everyone is there (v. 32). "All the nations" are gathered. The Greek word behind "nations" is the ordinary word for Gentiles or peoples; the point of the phrase is scope, not politics.
  • The title changes in verse 34. Verse 31 says "the Son of Man." Verse 34 says "the King." That is the only place in this Gospel where Jesus, speaking in his own voice, flatly calls himself the King. Others have used the word about him (2:2, 21:5, and soon 27:11, 29, 37). He has withheld it until now.
  • Six needs, listed four times (vv. 35-36, 37-39, 42-43, 44). Hungry, thirsty, a stranger, naked, sick, in prison. Count them: the verdict-giving list, the righteous asking their question, the indictment, and the condemned asking theirs. Nothing else in the Sermon on the Mount or the discourses gets that treatment. The repetition is the emphasis.
  • Both groups are astonished (vv. 37, 44). This is the observation to build everything else on. The sheep say "Lord, when did we see you hungry...?" The goats say the same thing in reverse. Neither side saw the criterion coming. Neither side was keeping score.
  • Asymmetry in the sentences (vv. 34, 41). The kingdom is "prepared for you from the foundation of the world." The fire is "prepared for the devil and his angels." One is prepared for them before time began; the other was prepared for someone else.
  • The same adjective twice (v. 46). "Eternal punishment" and "eternal life" use one and the same Greek word, in one sentence, in parallel. Whatever weight you give the second, the grammar gives to the first.
  • The discourse closes (26:1). "When Jesus had finished all these sayings." You have now met that formula five times (7:28, 11:1, 13:53, 19:1, 26:1), and this one adds the word all. The teaching of the King is complete. From here the book is passion narrative.

Interpretation

Who are "the least of these my brothers"?

"And the King will answer them, 'Truly, I say to you, as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me.'" Matthew 25:40, ESV

This is the honest question in the passage, and there are two serious answers. Take them both at their strongest.

The narrower reading: "my brothers" means Jesus' disciples, and especially his messengers under persecution. The argument is Matthew's own usage. In 12:48-50 Jesus points at the disciples and says "here are my mother and my brothers," defining the word for the rest of the book; after the resurrection he says "go and tell my brothers" (28:10), meaning the eleven. And in the mission discourse he already stated the exact logic of verse 40: "Whoever receives you receives me" (10:40), down to a cup of cold water given "to one of these little ones" (10:42). On this reading the nations are judged by how they treated Christ's people, hungry and imprisoned precisely because they carried his name, and therefore by how they treated Christ. MacArthur on chapter 25 takes this line: the verse "refers in particular to other disciples," commending those on the right "for the way they received his emissaries."

The broader reading: "the least of these" means the needy and suffering as such. The argument is the list itself, which is a list of ordinary human misery with nothing about preaching, faith, or persecution in it, and the fact that the King identifies with the sufferer rather than with the message. It also fits the shape of Matthew's ethic from 5:7 and 9:13 forward.

Notice what does not change between the two. On either reading, the person in front of you who is hungry, sick, or locked up is the test, and you do not get to sort him into a category first. And the broader duty is not in doubt anywhere else in Scripture: Isaiah 58:6-7, Proverbs 19:17, Galatians 6:10, James 1:27, 1 John 3:17. If the narrower reading is right, this text is about the nations and Christ's church, and the general duty of mercy rests on those other passages. If the broader reading is right, this text says it too. Either way you are without excuse tomorrow morning.

Both groups are surprised. What does that rule out?

"Then the righteous will answer him, saying, 'Lord, when did we see you hungry and feed you, or thirsty and give you drink?'" Matthew 25:37, ESV

Hold the two questions side by side. The righteous do not know they did it. The unrighteous do not know they failed to. Now try to read the passage as a system for earning a verdict: it collapses immediately. Nobody in the story was working the system, because nobody in the story knew what the system was. Whatever the deeds are doing here, they are not payment presented at a window.

What they are doing is showing. The sheep acted that way because that is what they had become; the mercy was reflex, invisible to them, and therefore honest. The goats had no such reflex and did not notice its absence, which is exactly how a missing heart works. The deeds are evidence in a trial, not currency at a counter.

And the King says so in the same breath he passes sentence: "inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world" (v. 34). Two words carry it. Inherit is what sons receive, not what workers earn. Prepared from the foundation of the world puts God's choice before their first act of kindness by the width of all history. Matthew has dropped election language into the center of a judgment-by-works scene without the least embarrassment, and MacArthur's note on the verse says it cleanly: the deeds "are the fruit, not the root of their salvation." Compare James 2:14-26, which argues the same thing from the other direction. This passage is not teaching salvation by charity. It is teaching that grace leaves fingerprints, and that the last day reads them.

When is this judgment, and how long does it last?

"And these will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life." Matthew 25:46, ESV

Two questions, and only one of them is genuinely open. On timing, MacArthur reads verse 31 as the start of a thousand-year earthly reign, making this a separate "judgment of the nations" on the living, distinct from the great white throne of Revelation 20:11-15. That follows from his premillennial and futurist framework rather than from anything in Matthew 25, where no interval, no millennium, and no second judgment appear. This course's postmillennial home reading, and the amillennial reading with it, take verses 31-46 as the one final judgment at the end of history, when all are raised and all are judged at once. Unit 7 recap tomorrow will lay the three views out together. For now, notice that the text gives you a throne, all the nations, two verdicts, and no third act.

On duration the text is not open at all. One Greek adjective, aiōnios, governs both halves of verse 46 in strict parallel. This is the strongest single verse against annihilationism, the view that the lost are finally extinguished rather than punished forever. The grammar will not allow the punishment to be shorter-lived than the life, because the sentence measures them with the same word. That is a sobering thing to say and there is no pleasure in saying it; say it anyway, gently, because Jesus said it, and he said it in the same sentence that promises life.

Application

  • About God: the Judge on the throne is the one who was himself hungry, a stranger, stripped, and condemned. He is not judging misery from a distance. He judges it from inside it, which is why he can say "you did it to me" and mean it literally.
  • About you: the mercy that counts on that day is the mercy you will not remember. If you can produce a tally, that is a warning sign, not a comfort. Pray instead to become the kind of person who acts and forgets.
  • Do: pick one of the six from the list, and do it today, for one actual named person. Take a meal to someone sick. Write to a prisoner or to a persecuted believer through your church. Include the stranger or newcomer nobody sat with last Sunday. Do it in a way that generates no story you can tell.

Church prompt
Ask someone in leadership at your church this week: which of the six needs in verses 35-36 does our congregation actually meet, and which one do we quietly skip? Most churches are good at two or three of them and have no practice at all for the others, usually the prisoner and the stranger. Ask what it would take to start.

Questions welcome, always: "if works are only evidence, why is the verdict announced on the basis of works?", "does 'all the nations' include Israel?", "what about people who never heard of Christ at all?" Ask your teacher.

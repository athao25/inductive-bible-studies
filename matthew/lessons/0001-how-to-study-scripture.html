Lesson 0001 · Orientation

How to Study Scripture

~25 min · No passage prerequisite · The one skill every other lesson builds on

Before Matthew, the tool. This course uses inductive study: you draw meaning out of the text instead of reading your assumptions into it. Today you learn the three steps and practice them on a single verse of Matthew.

Why method matters

Scripture is God speaking (2 Tim 3:16). If God has spoken, then the meaning of a passage is not up to us; our job is to hear what he actually said. That conviction, common to MacArthur, White, and Durbin and to the whole Reformed tradition, is why they all preach expositionally: verse by verse, letting the text set the agenda. You are about to do the same thing at a personal scale, 20 minutes at a time, for 28 chapters.

The three steps

StepQuestionDiscipline
1. ObservationWhat does it say?Look long. List facts: who, what, when, repeated words, connectors, contrasts.
2. InterpretationWhat does it mean?What did the author intend the original audience to understand? Context first; Scripture interprets Scripture.
3. ApplicationWhat must change?What does this reveal about God? What in me must be confessed, believed, obeyed?

The order is not decoration. Skip observation and your interpretation floats free of the text. Skip interpretation and your application applies something the passage never said. The full cheat sheet lives at reference: inductive method; it is your daily template from tomorrow on.

One rule for your helps
You own the MacArthur Study Bible in Olive Tree. Excellent tool, wrong first move. Always make your own observations before opening any note or commentary. Helps confirm and correct your reading; they must never replace it.

Practice: Matthew 9:9

One verse. Read it slowly, twice. Then, before scrolling on, write down at least four observations on paper.

As Jesus passed on from there, he saw a man called Matthew sitting at the tax booth, and he said to him, "Follow me." And he rose and followed him. Matthew 9:9, ESV

Done writing? Compare with what careful observation surfaces:

  • Who acts first: "he saw a man". Jesus is the subject of the seeing, the speaking, and the calling. Matthew does nothing until he is addressed.
  • Where the man is sitting: "at the tax booth". A tax collector worked for Rome against his own people; the job was regarded as legalized theft and national betrayal. Jesus calls him from the booth, not after he has cleaned himself up.
  • Who is writing: the author names himself in the third person, "a man called Matthew". He does not soften the setting or add a word of self-defense.
  • The size of the command: two words. "Follow me." No terms are negotiated, no explanation is offered, no probation is proposed.
  • The speed of the response: "he rose and followed him". Two verbs, no deliberation, no recorded conversation. The verse spends more words on the tax booth than on the decision.
  • What is missing: no mention of what it cost him, no mention of his feelings, no mention of the other disciples' reaction. Silences are observations too, and this one is worth marking.

Notice what happened: none of that required a commentary. It required slowing down. That is the skill this whole course trains.

A word about "boring" passages
Tomorrow you will meet a genealogy, and the day after, a list of names that runs seventeen verses. Nobody's favorite. But the rule holds: boring passages are almost always under-observed passages. The genealogy is where Matthew makes his opening legal argument, and you will see it once you look long enough.

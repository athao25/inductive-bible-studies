The man and the moment

Matthew, also called Levi, was a tax collector: a profession Jews regarded as traitorous collaboration with Rome. You met his call yesterday in the practice verse (Matt 9:9), and in his own list of apostles only Matthew keeps the shameful label "the tax collector" (10:3), a quiet mark of humility. The early church unanimously received this Gospel as his, written before the temple fell in AD 70. He wrote as an eyewitness, to a predominantly Jewish audience, with one purpose: to prove that Jesus of Nazareth is Israel's long-promised Messiah-King.

Three fingerprints of that Jewish aim, all visible in chapter 1: the genealogy starts at Abraham (Luke's goes back to Adam); the Old Testament is quoted more than 60 times, often with the formula "this was to fulfill what was spoken by the prophet"; and Matthew writes "kingdom of heaven" (32 times, nowhere else in Scripture) where the others write "kingdom of God", guarding Jewish reverence for the divine name.

New word: the Synoptics
Matthew, Mark, and Luke are called the Synoptic Gospels, from a Greek word meaning "seeing together": they share much of the same material in much of the same order, unlike John. When two of them tell the same event, comparing can sharpen what Matthew chose to include. But interpret Matthew on his own terms first; his selections are his argument.

One book, five sermons

Matthew alternates story and sermon. Five great teaching blocks (discourses) each close with the same formula, "when Jesus had finished these sayings" (7:28, 11:1, 13:53, 19:1, 26:1):

DiscourseChaptersTheme
Sermon on the Mount5-7The righteousness of the kingdom
Mission10The kingdom's messengers sent out
Parables13The kingdom's hidden growth
Community18Life together under the King
Olivet24-25Judgment and the coming of the King

A prologue (1-4) and an epilogue, the Great Commission (28:16-20), bracket the whole. Many readers hear an echo of the five books of Moses: Jesus teaches from a mountain as the greater Moses. Keep the eight-unit course map open in the book map; every future lesson will orient itself on it.

The book's center of gravity

Everything in Matthew argues one claim: Jesus is the King. The genealogy gives his legal credentials (son of David, son of Abraham, 1:1). The fulfillment quotations give his prophetic warrant. The miracles give his royal power. The discourses give his royal law. And the ending gives his royal claim in full: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" (28:18). Yet no Gospel paints Israel's rejection of her King more starkly; the crucified King wears the mocking title "King of the Jews" (27:37) and turns it into his coronation.

The book is also bracketed by presence: he is named Immanuel, "God with us" (1:23, quoting Isaiah 7:14), and his last words are "I am with you always, to the end of the age" (28:20).

Your teachers, one map, three lenses
Three trusted Reformed teachers walk with you through this course, and all three read Matthew as inerrant Scripture whose subject is Christ the King. They part ways on eschatology, above all at the Olivet Discourse (ch. 24-25). MacArthur reads it as a still-future tribulation; White blends near judgment with the final coming; Durbin, our home lens, reads "this generation" (24:34) as the AD 70 destruction of Jerusalem with the bodily return still ahead. You will meet smaller forks at 5:17-20, 13, and 16:18-19. Lessons there will argue from the text, not from labels. See the lens card on the book map.

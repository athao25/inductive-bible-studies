Matthew 28:16-20: All Authority

~30 min · Passage: Matthew 28:16-20 (ESV, read first!) · Memory verse 10 activates today

Write at least five observations: where they go and who told them to go there, the two reactions in verse 17 and how many of each, the word "all" and every place it occurs, which verb in verses 19-20 is the command and which words hang off it, and what the very last clause of the book promises. Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The location was assigned in advance (v. 16). "To Galilee, to the mountain to which Jesus had directed them." Not a chance meeting. Look back at 26:32 and 28:7 and 28:10: he named Galilee before he died, the angel repeated it, and the risen Christ repeated it again. Three notices for one appointment.
  • They are "the eleven" (v. 16). Matthew does not write "the twelve." The gap where Judas stood is left open in the text, three chapters after 26:14-16. The book never smooths it over.
  • A mountain, again (v. 16). Matthew stages his hinge moments on mountains: the temptation offer (4:8), the great sermon (5:1), the withdrawal to pray (14:23), the feeding and healing crowds (15:29), the transfiguration (17:1), the Olivet Discourse (24:3), and now this. When you see a mountain in Matthew, expect something decisive.
  • Two reactions in one sentence (v. 17). "When they saw him they worshiped him, but some doubted." Both clauses are about the same group in the same moment. Worship is the main verb of the sentence; the doubt is the concession attached to it.
  • Jesus closes the distance (v. 18). "And Jesus came and said to them." He approaches them, doubters included. Nobody is dismissed and no one is rebuked before he speaks.
  • "All" occurs four times in three verses (vv. 18-20). All authority, all nations, all that I have commanded, all the days (the phrase behind "always"). Circle them in your Bible. They are the skeleton of the paragraph.
  • One command, three attached words (vv. 19-20). In the Greek there is a single imperative here, "make disciples." "Going," "baptizing," and "teaching" are participles: verb forms that hang on the main verb and describe how it is carried out. English flattens this into four commands of equal weight; the sentence itself does not.
  • Two anchors, one on each end (vv. 18, 20). The Commission opens with a statement about Christ's authority and closes with a promise of Christ's presence. The command is sandwiched between two things he does, not two things you do.
  • The book stops without a scene (v. 20). No ascension, no crowd, no closing narration. Matthew ends on a spoken promise. The last word of the Gospel is about a duration: "to the end of the age."

Interpretation

1. Why does Matthew keep verse 17 in the text?

"And when they saw him they worshiped him, but some doubted." Matthew 28:17, ESV

Imagine writing the triumphant final scene of your case that Jesus is Israel's King, and inserting, in the middle of it, that some of the eyewitnesses were not sure. No inventor of a legend does this. The verb rendered "doubted" is not the word for settled unbelief; it carries the sense of wavering, being pulled two ways, the same word used of Peter sinking in 14:31. These are men looking at something their categories cannot yet hold.

MacArthur puts the point well and it deserves quoting: "That simple phrase is one of countless testimonies to the integrity of Scripture. The transparent honesty of a statement like this shows that Matthew was not attempting to exclude or cover up facts that might lessen the perfection of such a glorious moment" (MacArthur on chapter 28, note on 28:17). He also suggests, reasonably, that the doubt implies a larger crowd than the eleven, possibly the gathering of more than five hundred that Paul reports in 1 Corinthians 15:6.

Then notice what Jesus does with the wavering: nothing. He does not wait for unanimous confidence before commissioning them. The mission is given to a mixed assembly of worshipers and waverers, which is the only kind of assembly the church has ever been.

2. What exactly is being commanded?

"Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you." Matthew 28:19-20a, ESV

The grammar carries real freight here, so take it in one careful step. The sentence has one imperative: make disciples. Three participles surround it: going, baptizing, teaching. A participle in this construction does not compete with the main verb; it tells you the circumstance and the means. So the command is not "travel," with discipling as one option among things you might do once you arrive. The command is "make disciples," and going, baptizing, and teaching are how that happens.

Be fair about one nuance: a participle placed before an imperative like this normally shares the command's force, so "go" is not optional, and translations that render it "Go" are not wrong. The point is one of emphasis, not permission. Whether or not you cross an ocean, you have not obeyed this verse until someone is being discipled. That reframing is worth more than it looks: it moves the Commission out of the missionary-society category and into the ordinary Tuesday of every Christian.

Now the four parts, in the order the sentence gives them.

The basis: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" (v. 18). "Therefore" in verse 19 makes this the ground of everything after it. Two things to see. First, this is Daniel 7:14 arriving: "to him was given dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him." Jesus has used Son of Man language from Daniel 7 throughout the book, most sharply to Caiaphas in 26:64. Here the granted dominion is announced as fact, in the perfect tense: it has been given, and it stands given. Second, and this is the one that will keep you awake, look back at 4:8-9. On a mountain the devil showed him "all the kingdoms of the world and their glory" and said, "All these I will give you, if you will fall down and worship me." On another mountain, after the cross, the same Christ says all authority in heaven and on earth has been given to him. He got everything Satan offered and infinitely more, and he got it by the route Satan was trying to help him avoid. The crown came through the cross, not around it.

The scope: "make disciples of all nations" (v. 19). In 10:5-6 he had told these same men, "Go nowhere among the Gentiles and enter no town of the Samaritans, but go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel." That restriction was for that mission, and it is now lifted in the plainest terms: all nations. But the deeper move is backward, to Genesis 12:3, where God promised Abraham that "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed." Matthew's very first verse called Jesus "the son of David, the son of Abraham" (1:1), and you may have wondered why Abraham needed naming. Here is the answer, twenty-eight chapters later. The blessing-to-the-nations half of the Abrahamic promise is switched on by this command. Matthew ends where he began, and the genealogy turns out to have been pointing at the Great Commission the whole time.

The sign: "baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (v. 19). Read that slowly with a Jewish ear. The word is name, singular, not "names," followed by three persons joined by "and." A first-century Jew confessed daily that "the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4). For a Jewish writer to place Father, Son, and Spirit inside one divine name, without a word of apology, is a staggering thing, and it happens here as a settled assumption rather than an argument. MacArthur's note is exactly right and admirably brief: "The formula is a strong affirmation of trinitarianism" (note on 28:19). Baptism is the visible enrollment: the disciple is publicly marked as belonging to this God.

The content: "teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you" (v. 20). Two words do the work. Observe, not "hear": the goal is obedience, not information. And all: not the comfortable parts. That includes everything you worked through in the Sermon on the Mount, chapters 5 through 7, anger and lust and oaths and enemy-love and treasure and worry, the whole demanding thing. MacArthur draws the practical conclusion: "The kind of evangelism called for in this commission does not end with the conversion of the unbeliever" (note on 28:20). A decision is not a disciple. A disciple is someone being taught to obey, over years, by other disciples.

3. What is promised at the very end?

"And behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age." Matthew 28:20b, ESV

Go back to 1:23: "they shall call his name Immanuel (which means, God with us)." The Gospel opens by naming him God-with-us and closes with him saying "I am with you." Everything between those two sentences is the demonstration. MacArthur calls it "a touching echo of the beginning of Matthew's Gospel" (note on 28:20), and it is more than touching: it is the frame that holds the book together.

Two details. "Always" is literally "all the days," which fits the four other "alls" and means the ordinary ones, not just the heroic ones. And notice the placement: the promise of presence is attached to the command to go. He does not say "I am with you while you rest." The guaranteed companionship is promised to people on mission. If you want to test the promise, you have to be somewhere it applies.

Fork #5: how much does the mission succeed, and when?

The command is agreed by everyone. The question this passage forces on the three lenses is what "make disciples of all nations" is expected to accomplish before Christ returns. Argue from the text, not from the label.

  • Postmillennial (Durbin; this course's home lens). The discipling of the nations genuinely succeeds within history, before the return. Textual case: the authority is stated as already total and already given, not as something Christ will acquire later, so the mission goes out from a throne rather than toward one. The object is nations, not merely individuals scattered among nations. The kingdom parables of chapter 13 described growth from a mustard seed to a tree and leaven working through the whole lump. And Genesis 12:3 promised blessing to all the families of the earth, a promise that on this reading is meant to be visibly kept in history. Weakness to own: Jesus also predicted lawlessness increasing and love growing cold (24:12), and the New Testament nowhere says persecution ends before he comes.
  • Amillennial (James White). The mission really does gather God's people from every nation throughout this whole age, a genuine and worldwide success, but it runs alongside continuing opposition the entire way; the consummation comes only at the return. Textual case: "to the end of the age" implies the age keeps its character until it ends, and the promise of presence is exactly the kind of promise you need if the going stays hard. Revelation 7:9 shows the harvest complete, but shows it at the end. Weakness to own: it can leave "all nations" feeling thinner than the word sounds, and it must explain why chapter 13's growth imagery is so expansive.
  • Premillennial and dispensational (MacArthur). The mission calls out a people from among the nations during this present age; the nations as nations are discipled globally only in the millennial kingdom, after Christ returns bodily to reign. His own note on 28:20 says the presence lasts "until he returns bodily to judge the world and establish his earthly kingdom," which tells you where he locates the kingdom's public triumph. Textual case: he takes "all authority" as a statement of Christ's rightful lordship and deity now (his note on 28:18 calls it "clear proof of his deity"), exercised openly later, and he reads the Olivet Discourse as forecasting hard days rather than a discipled world. Weakness to own: the perfect tense in verse 18 says the authority has been given already, and the command sends disciples to nations, not merely into them.

What all three share, and it is most of it: Christ possesses universal authority now; the church is under orders now; the nations really will be his; and he will return bodily to judge the living and the dead. The disagreement is about the shape of the curve between now and then, not about the destination. And here is the practical point worth carrying out of this lesson: whichever way you land, the command does not change and the promise does not change. Make disciples. He is with you. An optimist and a pessimist about the next thousand years get the identical marching orders and the identical companion.

Application

  • About God He finishes what he starts. The promise to Abraham in Genesis 12, the name Immanuel in Matthew 1, the crown offered by Satan in Matthew 4 and won at Calvary instead: all of it converges in five verses. Nothing was improvised.
  • About you You are commissioned as you are, not as you wish you were. Some on that mountain doubted and were sent anyway. Wavering faith is not a disqualification; it is the normal condition of the people Christ sends.
  • Do Name one person today, out loud or on paper, whom you could actually disciple or be discipled by: a child, a new believer at church, a friend who asks questions. Then take one step in the next 24 hours, a text message, an invitation to read a chapter of Matthew together, a question you ask over coffee. One person, one step, today. That is what the main verb asks for.

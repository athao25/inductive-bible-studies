Lesson 0051 · Unit 6: Chapters 19-23

Matthew 19:1-15: Marriage and Children

~30 min · Passage: Matthew 19:1-15 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If not, stop here. Read Matthew 19:1-15 twice in your ESV and write at least five observations. Watch two things especially: which book of the Bible Jesus quotes when asked about Moses, and the exact verb the Pharisees use in verse 7 compared with the verb Jesus uses in verse 8.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. From the last lessons, without looking:

Unit 6 opens here. Chapter 19 begins the long road to Jerusalem: from this point the geography narrows, the opposition hardens, and every scene moves toward the cross. Note the hinge in verse 1, "when Jesus had finished these sayings", the same formula that closes each of Matthew's five teaching blocks (7:28, 11:1, 13:53, 19:1, 26:1). The community discourse of chapter 18 is over; the King starts walking south.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Galilee is left behind (vv. 1-2). "He went away from Galilee and entered the region of Judea beyond the Jordan." The Galilean ministry that has occupied us since chapter 4 is finished. And before any controversy begins, Matthew records mercy: "large crowds followed him, and he healed them there."
  • The question is a test, not an inquiry (v. 3). "And Pharisees came up to him and tested him by asking." Matthew tells you the motive before he tells you the words. Whatever answer Jesus gives will be used against him.
  • The loaded phrase is "for any cause" (v. 3). Not "is divorce lawful?" but "is it lawful to divorce one's wife for any cause?" Those three words are the whole trap, and they are quoted from a live argument among the rabbis.
  • He answers Moses with Genesis (vv. 4-5). "Have you not read...?" and then two quotations, Genesis 1:27 and Genesis 2:24. Asked about a law given in the wilderness, he goes back to the garden.
  • The subject of the joining is God (v. 6). "What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate." Marriage in this sentence is not primarily something a couple does; it is something God does, and the only command in the sentence is addressed to everyone else.
  • The verbs do not match (vv. 7-8). They say Moses "command"; Jesus says Moses "allowed". One word changes a mandate into a concession, and Jesus changes it deliberately.
  • "From the beginning it was not so" (v. 8). He grants that the permission is real and dates it: it is later than creation and lower than creation. The hardness of heart is theirs, stated to their faces, "because of your hardness of heart".
  • The disciples flinch, and not where you expect (v. 10). They do not argue for easier divorce. They conclude, "If such is the case of a man with his wife, it is better not to marry." That reaction is a measurement of how strict his answer sounded.
  • He does not soften it, but he does qualify it (vv. 11-12). "Not everyone can receive this saying, but only those to whom it is given", followed by three kinds of eunuchs and an invitation, not a rule: "Let the one who is able to receive this receive it."
  • The disciples rebuke, and then are rebuked (vv. 13-14). Children are brought for prayer, the disciples block them, and Jesus reverses it: "Let the little children come to me and do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of heaven."
  • The scene ends with touch, not talk (v. 15). "And he laid his hands on them and went away." The last act of a passage full of argument is a blessing on people too small to argue.

Interpretation

Why does Jesus refuse the question as it was asked?

And Pharisees came up to him and tested him by asking, "Is it lawful to divorce one's wife for any cause?" Matthew 19:3, ESV

You need the background or the trap is invisible. Deuteronomy 24:1 speaks of a man who divorces his wife because he "has found some indecency in her", and first-century Judaism was split over that phrase. The school of Shammai read "indecency" narrowly, as sexual sin. The school of Hillel, far more popular, read it broadly, as anything displeasing to the husband; the surviving examples include ruining a meal. MacArthur on chapter 19 puts it bluntly: the Hillelites "took a wholly pragmatic approach, and permitted a man to divorce his wife indiscriminately."

So the Pharisees are not asking a question; they are handing him a party badge. Side with Hillel and he contradicts what he already said in the Sermon on the Mount (5:31-32). Side with Shammai and he alienates the crowds and, in Perea, Herod's territory, edges toward the very subject that cost John the Baptist his head (14:3-4).

He takes neither badge. "Have you not read that he who created them from the beginning made them male and female, and said, 'Therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh'?" (vv. 4-5). Three moves in one sentence. He goes behind Moses to creation, which is older than the law and therefore not a concession to anything. He cites design, male and female, as the given shape of the thing. And he cites the one flesh union as a real change of state, not a legal fiction: two people become a single thing, which is why separating them is a tearing and not a filing.

"So they are no longer two but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, let not man separate." Matthew 19:6, ESV

That is the thesis of the whole passage. Marriage is a creation ordinance: something God instituted for humanity as such, before Israel, before the law, before the fall was ever remedied. Not a contract two parties invent and society may redefine at will, and not a religious ceremony the church owns. The Pharisees wanted to debate the terms of exit. Jesus makes them look at who does the joining, and their question dies. If God tied the knot, no human court is competent to call it untied at pleasure.

Did Moses command divorce, and does a permission make a thing good?

They said to him, "Why then did Moses command one to give a certificate of divorce and to send her away?" He said to them, "Because of your hardness of heart Moses allowed you to divorce your wives, but from the beginning it was not so." Matthew 19:7-8, ESV

Watch the correction; it is one word wide and it settles the argument. They say "command". He says "allowed". MacArthur's note is exactly right here: Deuteronomy 24:1-4 "was not a 'command' for divorce, but a limitation on remarriage in the event of a divorce", and in Matthew 19:8 "the stress is certainly on the word 'allowed'." The certificate itself was mercy toward the woman. In a world where a discarded wife had no income and no legal standing, the document proved she was free and not a runaway, so it protected the vulnerable party in a disaster the law did not create.

Two principles come out of this that will serve you across the whole Bible. First, a permission is not a mandate. That Moses regulated something does not mean God desired it; much of the civil law is damage control among sinners. Second, a concession is not an endorsement. "Because of your hardness of heart" names the reason the concession exists, and "from the beginning it was not so" names the standard the concession never replaced. Later revelation regulating a hard reality does not overwrite earlier revelation establishing a good one. That is why Jesus reasons from Genesis to Deuteronomy and never the reverse.

"And I say to you: whoever divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, and marries another, commits adultery." Matthew 19:9, ESV

The exception clause is real and it is narrow. The Greek word behind "sexual immorality" is porneia, a broad term covering sexual unfaithfulness of various kinds, and Jesus states the exception twice in Matthew (5:32 and here), so it is not an accident of one sentence. MacArthur reads verse 9 as "clearly permitting the innocent party in such a divorce to remarry", and takes the whole answer as Jesus siding with Shammai.

Now be told plainly what the text does and does not settle, because this is where Christians who take Scripture with equal seriousness genuinely differ. That divorce is contrary to God's creation design, that Moses permitted rather than commanded it, that the permission arises from hard hearts: all of this is agreed. What is disputed is remarriage. Some hold that the exception clause frees the innocent party to remarry, as MacArthur does. Others hold that it permits separation while the marriage bond itself stands until death. And beyond Matthew, Paul addresses a case Jesus is not asked about here, the believer deserted by an unbelieving spouse: "In such cases the brother or sister is not enslaved" (1 Corinthians 7:15). Some read that as a second ground; others read it more narrowly. These are not liberal-versus-conservative positions; they are careful readers weighing the same texts.

So this lesson will not pronounce on your situation, and it should not. What a page can do is set the standard, name the disagreement honestly, and refuse to pretend the hard cases are easy. What a page cannot do is know your history. If you carry a wound here, and many readers do, take it to your pastor and to people who know your name, not to an internet argument. Note where Jesus puts the weight in this passage: not on cataloguing exceptions, but on the goodness of what God joined and the hardness of the hearts that break it. The Sermon on the Mount already established that the same standard convicts everyone who reads it (5:21-30). No one comes to this text clean, and the cross is not smaller than a broken marriage.

Why does a teaching on marriage end with a word about singleness?

The disciples said to him, "If such is the case of a man with his wife, it is better not to marry." But he said to them, "Not everyone can receive this saying, but only those to whom it is given." Matthew 19:10-11, ESV

The disciples' reaction is a thermometer. Men raised on the Hillelite consensus hear Jesus close the exit and conclude that marriage has become too dangerous to enter. Whatever else that proves, it proves he was not heard as a moderate. MacArthur's note is fair: they "correctly understood the binding nature of marriage, and that Jesus was setting a very high standard."

His reply neither scolds them nor agrees with them. He says the saying about not marrying can only be "received" by those to whom it is given, and then names three kinds of people who do not marry: those so from birth, those made so by others, and "those who have made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven" (v. 12). The third group is figurative, a chosen celibacy for the sake of the kingdom of heaven, not self-mutilation, which the law forbade (Deuteronomy 23:1). Paul walks the same road in 1 Corinthians 7:7, calling both marriage and singleness gifts.

Two guardrails, since this verse has been abused in both directions. Jesus is not commanding celibacy, and he is not ranking it above marriage; MacArthur is right that "in no way did Christ suggest that celibacy is superior to marriage", and Genesis 2:18 with 1 Timothy 4:3 stand against any such idea. But neither is singleness a waiting room or a failure to launch. It is a genuine calling given to some, with real freedom in it for the kingdom's work, and a church that treats marriage as the only adult state is not reading verse 12.

Why do children close the passage?

Then children were brought to him that he might lay his hands on them and pray. The disciples rebuked the people, but Jesus said, "Let the little children come to me and do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of heaven." Matthew 19:13-14, ESV

Matthew's placement is not random. Marriage, then children: this is the household in order, and the same disciples who just called marriage too costly now decide that children are too small to be worth the Master's time. They rebuke; Jesus rebukes the rebuke. The word "hinder" is the point. Nobody in the story is attacking the children. The obstacle is a group of serious men doing crowd control, and that is usually how it works. Access to Christ is more often blocked by protective religious efficiency than by open hostility.

Then the reason: "to such belongs the kingdom of heaven". Straight back to 18:1-4, where he set a child among them and said "whoever humbles himself like this child is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven". Luke's parallel calls them "infants" (Luke 18:15), which sharpens it: these are not children who have argued their way in. They are carried, and they belong. And notice the tense. The kingdom does not merely await such people; it presently belongs to them, which fits how this course has been reading the kingdom all along, a reign already advancing rather than only a future arrangement.

MacArthur's note on 19:14 goes further, arguing that God "graciously extended" mercy to infants who die, so that they "are sovereignly regenerated and granted entrance into the kingdom, not because they are deserving of heaven, but because God in his grace chooses to redeem them" (he cites 2 Samuel 12:23 and is careful to add that this does not free them from inherited guilt in Adam). That is a warm and, I think, defensible hope, held by many Reformed writers, but be clear that it is an inference drawn from texts like 2 Samuel 12:23 rather than the direct claim of Matthew 19:14. What this verse says is what Jesus said: the kingdom is made up of people like these, and no disciple gets to run the door.

Application

  • About God He is the one who joins. Marriage is his institution before it is anyone's arrangement, which is why he can speak about it with an authority no culture, court, or committee possesses. And the same God who guards the one flesh union also stoops to bless people too small to contribute anything.
  • About you Two questions. Where do you argue like a Pharisee, looking for the widest lawful exit rather than the original design? And whom are you hindering? Every one of us has a category of person we quietly treat as not worth the Master's time: children, the disabled, the new believer with clumsy questions, the person whose marriage failed.
  • Do One thing today, chosen by your situation. If married: name one habit that erodes the "one flesh" reality (contempt, screens, a running private list of grievances), tell your spouse you saw it, and change one concrete thing this week. If single: pick one kingdom use of your freedom that a married person could not easily do, and schedule it. If divorced or divorcing: write down the one question you most fear asking, and send a message to your pastor today asking to talk. And whatever your state, find a child this week and give them your unhurried, undistracted attention.

Church prompt
Ask an elder this week what your church actually teaches about divorce and remarriage, and whether that position is written down anywhere a member could read it. Two follow-ups worth asking gently: how does the church care for someone divorced against their will, and how does it care for the long-term single member? The goal is not to litigate anyone's past; it is to know what your church believes before a crisis makes you ask in a hurry.

Matthew 5:1-12: The Beatitudes

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 5:1-12 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If not, stop here. Read Matthew 5:1-12 twice in your ESV and write at least five observations: where Jesus goes, what posture he takes, who is actually in front of him, the repeated shape of each line, which two lines end the same way, and where the pronouns change. Then come back and compare.

The sermon you are entering

Chapters 5-7 are the first and longest of Matthew's five discourses, and the church has called it the Sermon on the Mount since Augustine. It is one connected address, not a scrapbook of sayings: the King who was announced in chapter 4 now describes the life of his kingdom. Do not read it as a ladder of entrance requirements. Chapter 4 already told you the kingdom comes by grace to those who repent; chapter 5 tells you what the people who receive it look like. Over the next ten lessons you will work through it a paragraph at a time, and it ends with crowds "astonished at his teaching, for he was teaching them as one who had authority" (7:28-29).

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • A mountain and a seat (v. 1). "Seeing the crowds, he went up on the mountain, and when he sat down, his disciples came to him." He climbs, then sits. Sitting was the teaching posture of a rabbi, not a rest stop; MacArthur on chapter 5 notes on 5:1 that "this was the normal posture for rabbis while teaching."
  • Two audiences, one sermon (vv. 1-2). The crowds are present, but the disciples are the ones who "came to him" and are taught. The sermon is addressed to those already following, and overheard by everyone else.
  • A fixed formula, nine times (vv. 3-11). Every line begins "Blessed are." Eight of them are third person ("they"), and the ninth turns to second person ("you," v. 11).
  • Every blessing carries a promise (vv. 3-10). None is left hanging: kingdom, comfort, inheritance, satisfaction, mercy, sight of God, sonship, kingdom. Circle the word "for" in each line; it is the hinge.
  • The first and eighth promises match exactly (vv. 3, 10). "Theirs is the kingdom of heaven" opens and closes the list. Everything between sits inside that bracket.
  • Two tenses (vv. 3-10). The first and last promises are present ("theirs is"); the middle six are future ("they shall"). The kingdom is owned now; parts of the payout are still coming.
  • The list moves inward, then outward (vv. 3-9). Poverty of spirit, mourning, meekness, and hunger describe a man's posture before God. Mercy, purity of heart, and peacemaking show up in how he treats people.
  • Persecution gets two lines, not one (vv. 10-12). The only item Jesus expands is the hardest one, and he ends with a command: "Rejoice and be glad." He also anchors it in history, "so they persecuted the prophets who were before you" (v. 12).

Interpretation

What does "blessed" actually mean?

"Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven." Matthew 5:3, ESV

The Greek word is makarios. Some translations render it "happy," and that is where readers go wrong, because we hear "happy" as a mood that rises and falls with the day. Makarios names a condition, not a feeling: to be blessed is to stand in the settled favor of God, whatever your circumstances or emotions are doing. That is why Jesus can call mourners blessed without contradiction. A man weeping over his sin is not cheerful, and he is blessed. MacArthur's note on 5:3 says the word means "happy, fortunate, blissful," then immediately corrects the misreading: "Here it speaks of more than a surface emotion. Jesus was describing the divinely-bestowed well-being that belongs only to the faithful." Divinely bestowed is the key phrase. Blessedness is something God declares over you, not something you generate.

Read the eight lines as a portrait, then, not a ladder. Jesus is not handing out eight assignments to complete before the kingdom of heaven opens; he is describing the family resemblance of people the kingdom has already claimed. The proof is in verse 3 itself: the very first quality is having nothing to offer. If the list were a ladder, the bottom rung would be an admission that you cannot climb.

How does the progression work, and what do the terms not mean?

The order is not random. Each step makes the next one possible.

  • Poor in spirit (v. 3). The word for poor describes a beggar, someone crouched and destitute, not merely low on funds. This is not material poverty (Jesus does not bless empty wallets) and not low self-esteem (which is still a verdict you pass on yourself). It is spiritual bankruptcy honestly admitted: no merit, no leverage, nothing to trade. MacArthur calls it "the opposite of self-sufficiency."
  • Those who mourn (v. 4). Grief in general is not in view here; grief over sin is. See a man's bankruptcy clearly and he does not shrug, he grieves. Paul names the same thing as "godly grief" that "produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret" (2 Cor 7:10). The comfort promised is the comfort of forgiveness.
  • The meek (v. 5). Not timidity, not spinelessness. The Greek word was used of a horse broken to the bridle: full power, under a rider's command. Moses was "very meek, more than all people who were on the face of the earth" (Num 12:3), and he faced down Pharaoh. Meekness is strength that has stopped defending itself, because a man who knows he is bankrupt has nothing left to protect. The promise, "they shall inherit the earth," quotes Psalm 37:11.
  • Hunger and thirst for righteousness (v. 6). Appetite, not achievement. Bankrupt, grieving, and yielded, a man finally wants what he cannot manufacture. This is the opposite of Pharisee self-righteousness, which is already full.

Then the portrait turns outward. The merciful (v. 7) give away what they have received; a man who has been forgiven a debt he could never pay does not squeeze his debtors. The pure in heart (v. 8) are undivided, single rather than double-minded, and they are promised the thing Moses was denied: they "shall see God." The peacemakers (v. 9) do not merely avoid conflict, they actively make peace, which is exactly what God did at the cross; that is why the title fits, "they shall be called sons of God." Family likeness earns the family name.

Why does the list end in persecution, and why does the pronoun change?

"Blessed are you when others revile you and persecute you and utter all kinds of evil against you falsely on my account. Rejoice and be glad, for your reward is great in heaven, for so they persecuted the prophets who were before you." Matthew 5:11-12, ESV

Up to verse 10 Jesus has been describing a type of person: "blessed are they." In verse 11 he looks his disciples in the face and says "blessed are you." The portrait becomes a mirror, and the first application of the sermon is a warning: living like this will cost you. Notice the qualifier twice over. It is persecution "for righteousness' sake" (v. 10) and "on my account" (v. 11), and the evil said about you is "falsely." Being disliked for being obnoxious is not a beatitude.

Structurally the persecution line closes the bracket. Verse 3 and verse 10 both promise "theirs is the kingdom of heaven," which frames the whole list: the same people who begin with nothing end up with a kingdom, and the world's hostility does not dent the deed. Verse 12 goes further and commands joy, on two grounds: a reward that is kept in heaven where nobody can confiscate it, and the company you are in, the prophets. If they treated Isaiah and Jeremiah that way, opposition is a confirmation, not a contradiction.

One more thing about the setting. A mountain, a mediator seated above the people, a law being given: Matthew has already shown you Jesus called out of Egypt (2:15) and tested forty days in the wilderness (4:1-2), and now he puts him on a mountain teaching. The echo of Sinai is deliberate, and the point is that this is the greater Moses. But notice the difference. Sinai came with smoke, fire, and a boundary the people could not cross (Ex 19:12-18). Here the disciples "came to him," and he starts by pronouncing blessing, not curse.

Application

  • About God He gives the kingdom to beggars. Every quality in this list is something he produces, and every promise attached is something he pays. The order of verse 3 settles the argument about grace before the sermon is thirty seconds old.
  • About you Use the list as a mirror, not a whip. If you read it and think "I do not look much like that," you have just performed verse 3 and are standing in the right place. Self-sufficiency is the only posture the passage rules out.
  • Do Today, take one line and act on it before bed. Meekness: is there a fight you are winning that you should stop pressing? Mercy: is there someone whose debt to you (money, apology, reputation) you can release today? Peacemaking: send one message that starts a repair you have been avoiding. Pick one, do it today, and write down what happened.

Primary source
In your local MacArthur notes copy, read the header note on 5:1-7:29 and then the notes on 5:3, 5:4, 5:5, and 5:6 (4 min). The header note frames the whole sermon as an exposition of the law that "closes off every possible avenue of human merit," which is the same anti-ladder point this lesson made from verse 3. His note on 5:5 is the cleanest one-line definition of meekness you will find: "not weakness, but supreme self-control." For a fuller treatment, the GTY Matthew archive has a long sermon series on the Beatitudes; start with the messages on 5:3, since everything after it depends on getting poverty of spirit right.
Church prompt
The Beatitudes describe a congregation, not just an individual. This week, ask one member or elder which of these eight your church finds hardest to practice together, and listen rather than argue. Then watch for the merciful and the peacemakers in your own body on Sunday; find one person who quietly does this and tell them you noticed. Blessedness usually goes unnoticed by design (6:1-4), which is exactly why someone should say it out loud.

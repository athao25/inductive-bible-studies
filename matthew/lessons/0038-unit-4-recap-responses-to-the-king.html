Matthew 11-13: Responses to the King

The architecture of Unit 4

Unit 3 asked what the King can do. Unit 4 asks what people do with him. Matthew has gathered three chapters around a single question, and the answers he collects run the entire spectrum from honest confusion to settled hatred. Lay them out in order and the design is unmistakable.

  • Honest doubt, from a friend (11:2-6). John the Baptist, in Herod's prison, sends to ask, "Are you the one who is to come, or shall we look for another?" This is not a scoffer. It is the man who baptized him. Jesus does not rebuke the question; he answers it with evidence (11:5) and then praises John publicly (11:7-11).
  • Indifference, from towns that saw everything (11:20-24). Chorazin, Bethsaida, Capernaum. No mob, no plot, no mockery on record. They watched mighty works and simply carried on. Jesus says Sodom would have repented, and that their judgment will be heavier.
  • Revelation and concealment, from the Father (11:25-27). The wise and understanding do not have it; little children do. The difference is traced not to intelligence or effort but to the Father's gracious will.
  • Invitation, to anyone worn out (11:28-30). Right in the middle of the rejection, the widest offer in the Gospel: come, all who labor, and I will give you rest.
  • Legal objection, from the Pharisees (12:1-14). It starts as a dispute over grain plucked on the Sabbath and a withered hand healed on the Sabbath. It ends in verse 14 with a murder plot. Two Sabbath scenes and they are already conspiring how to destroy him.
  • Slander, aimed at the source of his power (12:22-32). Unable to deny the exorcism, they explain it: he casts out demons by Beelzebul. Jesus answers that a divided kingdom falls, that the strong man has been bound, and that this particular charge is blasphemy against the Spirit.
  • Sign-seeking that nothing would satisfy (12:38-42). They have just watched a demon leave a blind and mute man. They ask for a sign. He offers Jonah, and points out that Nineveh and the queen of the South did better with less.
  • His own family, standing outside (12:46-50). Mother and brothers send for him from beyond the crowd, and he redefines family around doing the will of his Father.
  • His hometown, taking offense (13:53-58). The carpenter's son. They knew him too well to receive him, "and he did not do many mighty works there, because of their unbelief."

That is the file of evidence. Now watch what Matthew does with it.

Chapter 13 is the explanation of chapters 11 and 12

Chapter 13 is the third of Matthew's five great teaching blocks (see discourse). It is not a change of subject after two chapters of conflict. It is the theological account of what you just watched.

  • The sower (13:1-9, 18-23) explains the different results. Same seed, same sower, four outcomes. The variable was never the word. John, the towns, the Pharisees, Nazareth, and the disciples all heard the same message; the soil differed. Two chapters of case studies, then the diagnosis.
  • The purpose statement (13:10-17) explains the change in method. The disciples ask why he now teaches in parables. His answer is that knowing the secrets of the kingdom has been given to some and not to others, and he quotes Isaiah 6:9-10 over the crowd. From this point on, public teaching is parable only (13:34). Concealment has become part of the message.
  • The weeds and the net (13:24-30, 36-43, 47-50) explain the mixed field. If the kingdom has truly arrived, why is the response so uneven? Because the harvest is not yet. Wheat and darnel grow together by the owner's decision, and the net brings up everything before anything is sorted. Mixture is the expected shape of the interval, not evidence that the kingdom failed.
  • The mustard seed and the leaven (13:31-33) explain the smallness. Beginnings this modest, against opposition this loud, are exactly what a seed and a lump of dough look like early. The trajectory is stated as growth and permeation.
  • The treasure and the pearl (13:44-46) explain the ones who did receive it. Not better people; people to whom something of enormous worth became visible, who then acted accordingly.

Interpretation

What holds 11:25-27 together with 11:28?

"I thank you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that you have hidden these things from the wise and understanding and revealed them to little children; yes, Father, for such was your gracious will." Matthew 11:25-26, ESV
"Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest." Matthew 11:28, ESV

Two verses apart. The first says the Father hides and reveals as he pleases and that the Son reveals him to whomever he chooses (11:27). The second throws the door open to everyone hearing. Both are the same speaker in the same breath, and Matthew records them without any apparent discomfort.

MacArthur on chapter 11 refuses to soften either side: the invitation is "an open invitation to all who hear," yet "without a sovereignly-bestowed spiritual awakening, all sinners refuse to acknowledge the depth of their spiritual poverty," and so "the truth of divine election in v. 27 is not incompatible with the free offer to all in vv. 28-30." That is the Reformed reading, and it is the reading of this course.

Do not smooth this out. The temptation runs both directions: shrink the sovereignty until the invitation is the only real thing, or shrink the invitation until it is a formality God does not mean. Scripture lays two rails and runs both the full length of the track. God is genuinely sovereign in who receives sight, and the call to come is genuinely addressed to you. Your job is not to determine which rail you are standing on before you move. Your job is to come.

Is the blindness of 13:13-15 something they did or something done to them?

Indeed, in their case the prophecy of Isaiah is fulfilled that says: "'You will indeed hear but never understand, and you will indeed see but never perceive.' For this people's heart has grown dull, and with their ears they can barely hear, and their eyes they have closed, lest they should see with their eyes and hear with their ears and understand with their heart and turn, and I would heal them." Matthew 13:14-15, ESV

Read the grammar carefully. "Their eyes they have closed" is something they did. But Jesus is quoting a commission in which God sends Isaiah precisely to make hearts dull (Isa. 6:9-10), and he cites it to explain why he now speaks in a way that conceals. Both are asserted. MacArthur names the tension exactly: Matthew "seems to suggest that their own unbelief is the cause of their spiritual blindness," Luke "emphasizes God's initiative in obscuring the truth," and "both things are true, of course."

The traditional term for this is judicial hardening: God confirming people in the direction they have already chosen, as a judgment. Note the sequence in chapters 11 and 12. The hardening language does not arrive first. It arrives after the towns shrugged and after the Pharisees called the Spirit's work satanic. And note the strange mercy in it, which MacArthur also sees: further light to people who have settled against the light would only deepen the guilt. Add 13:11 to this, "To you it has been given to know the secrets of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it has not been given," and the picture is complete. Sight is a gift, never a wage. Blindness is a verdict, never an accident.

Hold that beside the soils. The rocky ground had no root; the thorny ground loved money and the cares of the world. Nobody in chapter 13 is condemned for a decree they could not see. They are condemned for what they actually wanted.

If the field stays mixed, has the kingdom failed?

This is the fork question of the unit, and the parables answer it directly. The owner will not let the weeds be pulled early; the net comes up full of everything; the sorting waits for angels at "the end of the age" (13:39-43, 49-50). So a mixed field is not a defeat. It is the announced arrangement.

Where readers divide is on the trajectory. Premillennial and futurist readers, MacArthur among them, take the present age as a parenthesis of mixture and locate the kingdom's visible glory in a future earthly reign after Christ returns; his note on 13:43 looks ahead to "the glory of Christ's kingdom and eternal heaven." Amillennial readers expect wheat and weeds to grow side by side, both intensifying, until the end, with no era of visible dominance. The postmillennial home lens of this course reads the mustard seed and the leaven as the trajectory statement: the smallest seed becomes the largest garden plant, the leaven works until "it was all leavened," and that happens inside history before the harvest, not after it. Interestingly, MacArthur's own note on 13:33 argues hard against reading the leaven as an evil influence, insisting Jesus is "describing that kingdom itself as the pervading influence." Read at face value, that is a growth trajectory.

Argue it from the text and not the label. Nothing in these parables says the mustard plant stops growing, and nothing in them says the weeds are removed before the end. Both facts have to stand. Whatever you conclude about the timetable, the pastoral point is the same one Matthew is making after two chapters of rejection: the response you see around you is not proof that the word is weak.

Application

  • About God The King who could have answered the Beelzebul charge with fire answered it with an argument, and then offered rest to the weary. Sovereign over who sees, and gentle toward whoever comes. Both are the same person in the same three chapters.
  • About you Capernaum is the warning that should land hardest. It never mocked him. It simply saw and stayed unmoved, and its sentence is heavier than Sodom's. Familiarity with Jesus is not the same thing as receiving him, and the safest-looking response in this unit is the one Jesus treats as the most dangerous.
  • Do Today, take four minutes and write the four soils down the side of a page. Beside each, name one honest thing from your own last year: a word that never landed, a burst of enthusiasm that died under cost, a worry or a want that choked something out, and one place fruit actually showed up. Then pray 11:28 out loud with the specific weight you are carrying named in the prayer. Do not generalize it.

Church prompt
The parable of the weeds says the field stays mixed until the harvest, and the net says the same about the visible church. Ask your elders how they think about that pastorally: what does a church do about members whose lives look like darnel, and where is the line between the discipline the New Testament commands and the early uprooting Jesus forbids here? Second thing worth raising with a friend: John the Baptist asked an honest question from prison and Jesus honored him for it. Does our church make room for that kind of question, or only for the confident version of faith?

Questions welcome, always: "if God hides things from some people, how can the invitation in 11:28 be sincere?", "did John the Baptist lose his faith in prison?", "is the leaven in 13:33 good or evil, since leaven usually means sin?" Ask your teacher.

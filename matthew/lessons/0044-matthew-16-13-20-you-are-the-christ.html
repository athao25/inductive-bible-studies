Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The place is named, and Matthew rarely wastes a place name (v. 13). "The district of Caesarea Philippi," about 25 miles north of Galilee at the foot of Mount Hermon, per MacArthur on chapter 16. This is Gentile territory, a town rebuilt by Herod Philip and renamed for Caesar, sitting beside a cave-shrine to the god Pan and a temple built to Augustus. The confession that Jesus is Lord is made in the shadow of a rival god and a rival lord.
  • Two questions, and the second one turns (vv. 13, 15). First, "Who do people say that the Son of Man is?" Then, "But who do you say that I am?" The Greek pronoun is emphatic and plural: you, this group, right now. Notice also the switch from "the Son of Man" to "I am." Jesus makes the question personal in both directions.
  • Public opinion is respectful and wrong (v. 14). John the Baptist, Elijah, Jeremiah, one of the prophets. Every guess is a compliment, and every guess makes him a forerunner rather than the goal. There is no neutral flattering answer to this question.
  • Peter answers with two titles, not one (v. 16). "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God." Christ is the office, the anointed King promised through the Old Testament. "Son of the living God" is the relationship, and "the living God" is an Old Testament name for the LORD set against dead idols (Deuteronomy 5:26; Joshua 3:10; 1 Samuel 17:26). Standing in front of Pan's grotto, that adjective is doing work.
  • Jesus credits the Father, not Peter (v. 17). "Flesh and blood has not revealed this to you, but my Father who is in heaven." The blessing is real and the insight is real, and neither one originated with Peter. The confession is a gift before it is an achievement.
  • The wordplay is in the sentence itself (v. 18). "You are Peter (Petros), and on this rock (petra) I will build my church." Two related but distinct words sit side by side, and the whole interpretive argument you are about to meet lives in that gap.
  • The builder and the owner are named once and never questioned (v. 18). "I will build my church." Whatever else is contested here, the possessive pronoun is not. It is his project and his property.
  • "Church" appears here for the first time in any Gospel (v. 18). Matthew is the only Gospel that uses the word at all, here and at 18:17. Two occurrences in four Gospels, and both are in Matthew.
  • Gates, not armies (v. 18). "The gates of hell shall not prevail against it." Gates are what a city defends itself with. Prevailing or failing to prevail is what a defense does against an attack.
  • Keys, and then binding and loosing (v. 19). Two images stacked: keys of the kingdom, then a verb pair about earth and heaven. Note the tenses carefully in the ESV: "shall be bound in heaven," "shall be loosed in heaven." Earth is not instructing heaven.
  • The high point ends in a gag order (v. 20). "Then he strictly charged the disciples to tell no one that he was the Christ." Right after the greatest confession in the book, silence is commanded. Keep that oddity open; verse 21 explains it.

Interpretation

Why is this the hinge of the whole Gospel?

"He said to them, 'But who do you say that I am?' Simon Peter replied, 'You are the Christ, the Son of the living God.'" Matthew 16:15-16, ESV

Fifteen chapters have been building the evidence: a genealogy, a virgin birth, a voice from heaven, authority over disease, storm, demons, bread, and death. The crowds have watched all of it and landed on "a prophet." The disciples have watched all of it and are now asked to say out loud what it adds up to. Peter does, and the book turns on its axis. Look at how Matthew marks the turn: "From that time Jesus began to show his disciples that he must go to Jerusalem and suffer" (16:21). MacArthur is right that this phrase signals a new emphasis, from public ministry to private preparation for the cross. Everything before this verse asks who is he. Everything after it asks what did he come to do, and the answer is: die.

That is also the answer to verse 20. Why silence a true confession? Because the word Christ in that generation's mouth meant a military liberator who would throw out Rome, and a crowd that shouted it would have been shouting for the wrong kingdom on the wrong timetable. The title was not wrong; the public's content for the title was. Jesus withholds the announcement until the cross has defined the word. The disciples themselves do not yet understand it, which Peter proves within six verses by rebuking the Christ he just confessed.

Then feel the weight of verse 17. Peter did not reason his way here, and neither did you. "Flesh and blood has not revealed this to you, but my Father." MacArthur puts it well: this "was a confession of Peter's personal faith, made possible by a divinely-regenerated heart." If you can say Jesus is the Christ and mean it, that sentence is evidence of a work God already did in you.

What is the rock, and what are the keys?

"And I tell you, you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it. I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven, and whatever you bind on earth shall be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven." Matthew 16:18-19, ESV

First, define the word. Church translates the Greek ekklesia, "called-out assembly," the ordinary word for a citizen body summoned to meet, and the word the Greek Old Testament uses for the assembly of Israel. The study of what the church is, who belongs to it, and what authority it holds is called ecclesiology, and this passage is where every argument in that field begins. This verse and 18:17 are the only two places any Gospel uses the word, which is precisely why the two verses must be read together, as you will see below.

Second, the gates. Hell, or Hades, is pictured as a walled city, and gates are defensive equipment. A gate never charged anyone. So the promise is not merely that the church survives a siege; it is that the church is the one doing the besieging, and death's gates will not hold. MacArthur notes that "gates of hell" is a Jewish way of saying death, and that "even death, the ultimate weapon of Satan, has no power to stop the church." Take it further in the direction the image points: the church advances into territory death currently holds, and the doors give way.

Third, the keys. Verse 19 hands Peter keys and a binding-and-loosing authority, and two chapters later Jesus hands the identical authority to the local congregation: "Truly, I say to you, whatever you bind on earth shall be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven" (18:18), addressed to a church dealing with an unrepentant member. Same words, different recipient. Whatever the keys are, they are not a personal possession of one man. And the future-perfect force of the verbs matters: the church declares on earth what heaven has already settled. As MacArthur puts it, "the church's authority is not to determine these things, but to declare the judgment of heaven based on the principles of the word." Keys open the door by preaching the gospel and by receiving and disciplining members, and both actions announce a verdict rather than invent one.

Fork #3: the rock and the keys

This is the most contested sentence in the Gospel. Roughly half of Christendom rests its government on it. Learn each reading from its strongest form, and argue from the text.

The Roman Catholic reading. Peter is the rock. Jesus renames Simon in the same breath in which he names the foundation, so the play on words is an identification, not a contrast. In Aramaic, which Jesus likely spoke, one word (kepha) would serve for both, so the Greek variation is only a grammatical accident of gender. From this Rome derives Peter's primacy among the apostles, a bishop's chair at Rome as his seat, and a succession of popes holding the keys. Strongest point: verse 18 is addressed to Peter in the second person singular throughout, and it is unnatural to say "you are Peter" and then mean someone else by "this rock" in the very next clause.

Protestant reading A: the rock is the confession, or Christ as confessed. Petros means a stone; petra means bedrock. Jesus shifts words deliberately: a boulder-like truth has just come out of the mouth of a man named Pebble. The rock is what Peter said in verse 16. MacArthur takes this line, noting that the New Testament makes Christ the only foundation (1 Corinthians 3:11; Acts 4:11-12) and the head of the church (Ephesians 5:23), and that Peter himself explains the imagery later: believers are "living stones" who confess Christ, and Christ is the cornerstone (1 Peter 2:5-7).

Protestant reading B: the rock is Peter, in his representative role. Many Reformed interpreters grant that "this rock" is Peter and deny that anything Rome claims follows from it. Peter is the first confessor and a foundation-layer: he preaches at Pentecost, he opens the door to the Samaritans and then to Cornelius's household. Ephesians 2:20 says the church is "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone," which is exactly this picture, and note that a foundation is by definition laid once. Foundations do not have successors.

Why either Protestant reading refutes the papal claim. Do not let anyone tell you the Reformation hangs on option A. Grant option B entirely and the case for Rome still fails, on four textual grounds. (1) Verse 17 says the Father, not Peter, produced this confession, so nothing here is Peter's own competence to pass on. (2) Verse 18 says "I will build my church," which keeps the Architect, Builder, Owner, and Lord in one pair of hands. (3) Matthew 18:18 gives the same binding and loosing to the congregation, which is decisive: a power shared with every local church two chapters later was never a personal monopoly. (4) The text itself supplies the rebuke: within two paragraphs Jesus tells this same rock "Get behind me, Satan!" (16:23), and later Paul opposes him "to his face, because he stood condemned" (Galatians 2:11). Neither passage is written the way you write about an infallible office. Add to that the plain silence of Scripture about Rome, about succession, and about a chief apostle, and the load the doctrine has to carry is far heavier than the verse can bear.

Where the eschatologies land. This fork divides less by millennial view than the others in this course, and that is worth knowing. Our postmillennial home lens hears verse 18 as a promise with a direction: a church on the offensive against gates that will not hold, taking real ground in history, which fits the mustard tree and the leaven of chapter 13. The amillennial reading hears the same verse as an unbreakable guarantee of preservation through an age of conflict, with the decisive victory at Christ's return. MacArthur, premillennial and futurist, adds a distinctive claim of his own: that the church Jesus promises to build here "began at Pentecost" as a new entity, distinct from Israel. Test that one against the text. Jesus says he will build; he does not say he will start from nothing, and ekklesia was already Israel's word for her assembly. The Reformed reading is that God has one covenant people across both testaments, and Pentecost is the pouring out of the Spirit on that people, not the founding of a second one.

Application

  • About God He does not leave the most important question in your life to your own cleverness. He reveals his Son, and he builds his own church, and death's gates do not get a vote.
  • About you Your confession is a gift, which kills both pride and despair. And the two questions are still separate: what people say about Jesus costs nothing, and what you say costs everything.
  • Do Answer verse 15 out loud today, in your own words, in one sentence, somewhere other than a church building: in the car, at your desk, on a walk. Then name one place this week where saying it out loud would cost you something, and go there anyway.

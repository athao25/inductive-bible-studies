Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The scene is a departure (v. 1). "Jesus left the temple and was going away." He has just said "your house is left to you desolate" (23:38). He leaves, and the disciples respond by pointing out the architecture. The juxtaposition is the whole setup: he pronounces a building doomed, they admire it.
  • The prediction is total and specific (v. 2). "There will not be left here one stone upon another that will not be thrown down." Not damaged, not captured, not defiled: dismantled. And "here", of these buildings, in front of them.
  • The location changes, and it matters (v. 3). The Mount of Olives sits directly east of the temple across the Kidron Valley, the one spot with a full panoramic view of the whole complex. They are looking at the thing he just condemned while they ask about it.
  • The question is asked "privately" (v. 3). This is not public teaching to crowds. It is a private answer to a private question, which means the question shapes the answer. Whatever chapters 24-25 are, they are a reply to what these men asked.
  • The question has parts (v. 3). "Tell us, when will these things be, and what will be the sign of your coming and of the end of the age?" Count the clauses: when will these things be, and what will be the sign of two things joined by "and", your coming, and the end of the age. Whether that is one question, two, or three is the hinge of this entire unit. Mark it in your Bible.
  • Jesus' first word is not a prediction but a warning (v. 4). "See that no one leads you astray." The verb "lead astray" or "deceive" recurs in verses 4, 5, and 11. The discourse is built to protect, not to satisfy curiosity.
  • Twice he tells them what does not mean the end (vv. 6, 8). "The end is not yet"; "all these are but the beginning of the birth pains." Wars, famines, and earthquakes are explicitly disqualified as end-markers. Every generation that has read this chapter has been tempted to do exactly what verse 6 forbids.
  • The persecution is personal and named (vv. 9-10). "They will deliver you up to tribulation and put you to death, and you will be hated by all nations for my name's sake." Then betrayal from inside: "many will fall away and betray one another and hate one another." The pressure comes from outside and produces collapse on the inside.
  • Verse 12 gives a cause and an effect. "Because lawlessness will be increased, the love of many will grow cold." That is a diagnosis with a mechanism, not just a lament, and it names love, not doctrine, as what fails first.
  • Verse 13 is the survival clause, verse 14 the timetable clause. "The one who endures to the end will be saved" is the command; "and then the end will come" is the schedule, and what triggers it is not a war but a proclamation.

Interpretation

What did Jesus say would happen to the temple, and did it?

"But he answered them, “You see all these, do you not? Truly, I say to you, there will not be left here one stone upon another that will not be thrown down.”" Matthew 24:2, ESV

Start here, because this part is not disputed by anyone. Herod's temple was begun around 20 BC and was still unfinished in Jesus' day; some of its stones measured roughly forty feet long, its facing was white marble, and its eastern wall carried gold plating that threw back the morning sun for miles. It was one of the most impressive structures in the ancient world. In AD 70, roughly forty years after Jesus spoke, the Roman general Titus besieged Jerusalem and destroyed it. MacArthur's note is a good factual summary and every school of interpretation accepts it: Titus "built large wooden scaffolds around the walls of the temple buildings, piled them high with wood and other flammable items, and set them ablaze. The heat from the fires was so intense that the stones crumbled" (MacArthur on chapter 24, note on 24:2).

Hold that fixed point. Whatever you conclude about the rest of the chapter, verse 2 was fulfilled in history, in the lifetime of people standing there. That is not a theory; it is a date.

What exactly did the disciples ask, and why is it the whole battle?

"As he sat on the Mount of Olives, the disciples came to him privately, saying, “Tell us, when will these things be, and what will be the sign of your coming and of the end of the age?”" Matthew 24:3, ESV

Read that sentence slowly. Interpreters divide over three things in it, and the divisions produce nearly every disagreement in this unit.

First, how many questions is it? Some read one question asked three ways, on the assumption that the disciples thought the temple's fall, Christ's coming, and the end of the age were a single event. Some read two, "when will these things be" and "what will be the sign". Some read three distinct topics. Second, were the disciples right? They had just heard the temple sentenced and, per Luke 19:11, they still "supposed that the kingdom of God was to appear immediately". If their assumption was mistaken, part of Jesus' job in the answer is to pull apart what they had fused. Third, does Jesus answer them separately or together? Does one continuous prophecy run to 25:46, or is there a seam somewhere, most often proposed at 24:36 ("but concerning that day and hour")?

One word deserves flagging now because it carries weight later. "Coming" in verse 3 translates the Greek parousia, literally "presence" or "arrival", a word used in the ancient world for a king's royal visit to a city. It appears in Matthew only in this chapter (vv. 3, 27, 37, 39). Whether every use means the final bodily return, or whether some describe a coming in judgment such as the "coming on the clouds" language the prophets used for God judging nations (Isaiah 19:1), is precisely what lesson 0064 will argue from the text.

The three readings, laid out fairly (your map for Unit 7)

1. Futurist (MacArthur, premillennial and dispensational). The disciples asked about the far-future coming; Jesus answers in reverse order, giving signs of his coming in vv. 4-35 and the timing question from v. 36 on. AD 70 is at most a preview; the abomination of desolation (v. 15), the great tribulation, and the coming of the Son of Man (vv. 29-31) are still ahead of us. Strength: it takes the cosmic language of vv. 29-31 at full weight.

2. Partial preterist (Durbin, postmillennial, our home lens). Verses 4-35 answer the temple question and were fulfilled in the generation Jesus named (v. 34), climaxing in AD 70; the bodily second coming, resurrection, and final judgment enter at v. 36 and fill chapter 25. Strength: it takes "this generation will not pass away" (v. 34) and "you" in v. 9 in their plain sense. Note carefully: partial. Full preterism, which denies any future bodily return, is heresy, and no teacher in this course holds it.

3. Both/and, double fulfillment (White, amillennial, and many others). The AD 70 judgment is real and really predicted here, and it functions as a pattern of the final judgment, so the same words look at a near horizon and a far one at once. Strength: prophecy elsewhere often works this way, and it explains why the chapter refuses to sort cleanly.

What this course will do. We will not settle it by label. Lesson 0064 argues the fork from the text of vv. 29-35, and lesson 0069 compares all three honestly once you have read the whole discourse. Your job over the next week is to read chapters 24-25 straight through more than once and form your own view. All three positions are held by men who confess Christ's bodily return, a real final judgment, and the full authority of Scripture. This is a family disagreement, not a test of orthodoxy.

What is Jesus actually telling them to do in verses 4-14?

"And you will hear of wars and rumors of wars. See that you are not alarmed, for this must take place, but the end is not yet. For nation will rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom, and there will be famines and earthquakes in various places. All these are but the beginning of the birth pains." Matthew 24:6-8, ESV

Notice how much of this is negative instruction. Do not be led astray. Do not be alarmed. Do not treat catastrophe as a countdown. The image of "birth pains" is doing careful work: labor pains are real, they are painful, they genuinely mean something is coming, and early contractions tell you almost nothing about the hour. Anyone who reads a war or an earthquake as proof that the end is here has used this passage to do the one thing it forbids.

Then the pressure turns inward. Persecution (v. 9) produces apostasy (v. 10), false prophets exploit it (v. 11), lawlessness spreads and "the love of many will grow cold" (v. 12). Against that, one word: "the one who endures to the end will be saved" (v. 13). This is not salvation earned by stamina. Endurance is the evidence of the real thing, not the price of it, and MacArthur states it well: "God, as part of his saving work, secures our perseverance"; the warnings "are among the means God uses to secure our perseverance in the faith". On this the three lenses have no quarrel at all.

"And this gospel of the kingdom will be proclaimed throughout the whole world as a testimony to all nations, and then the end will come." Matthew 24:14, ESV

Verse 14 is a key verse for every position, and it is worth seeing how each handles it, because this is the kind of comparison you will make repeatedly in this unit. The futurist reads "the whole world" as global evangelization completed before the final tribulation ends, possibly with angelic proclamation (Revelation 14:6). The partial preterist notes that the Greek oikoumene commonly means the inhabited Roman world, that Paul could already write of the gospel "proclaimed in all creation under heaven" (Colossians 1:23) before AD 70, and reads "the end" as the end of the old covenant order that fell with the temple. The both/and reader takes the first-century sense as genuinely satisfied and the global sense as still unfolding toward the last day. Postmillennially, verse 14 is a promise with teeth: the gospel does not merely get announced, it gets a hearing among all nations, and that expectation shapes how our home lens reads Matthew's ending in 28:18-20.

Application

  • About God. He tells his people in advance what will hurt them. Jesus does not offer the disciples a date; he offers them warning, endurance, and a mission. A God who governs history closely enough to name a temple's demolition forty years out is not improvising through your decade either.
  • About you. Verse 12 is uncomfortably precise: love grows cold not because doctrine collapses but because lawlessness wears it down. Cold love rarely announces itself. It looks like still attending, still agreeing, still being right, and feeling nothing for people.
  • Do (today). Take ten minutes and read Matthew 24 straight through in one sitting, no notes, no commentary, and write one sentence answering "what do I currently think this chapter is about?" Date it. Keep it until lesson 0069, when you will read your own sentence back. That single act will make this unit yours rather than a set of positions you were handed.

Matthew 20:17-34: To Serve and to Give

~30 min · Passage: Matthew 20:17-34 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If not, stop here. Read Matthew 20:17-34 twice in your ESV. Write at least five observations: every specific thing Jesus says will happen to him in verses 18-19 and in what order, who comes to him next and what she asks, what Jesus asks her in return, how the other ten react, the two words Jesus uses for greatness in verses 26-27, exactly what verse 28 says he came to do, and what the two blind men call him. Then come back and compare.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • He takes them aside, and the direction is named twice (v. 17). "As Jesus was going up to Jerusalem, he took the twelve disciples aside, and on the way he said to them, 'See, we are going up to Jerusalem.'" This is private teaching, not crowd preaching. MacArthur notes on 20:17 that this is the start of the final journey to the cross, out of Perea and up toward the city that kills prophets.
  • The prediction is now a list, in sequence (vv. 18-19). Count them: delivered over to the chief priests and scribes; condemned to death; delivered over to the Gentiles; mocked; flogged; crucified; raised on the third day. Seven items, in the exact order they will happen in chapters 26-28. Nothing here is vague.
  • He names the method of execution for the first time (v. 19). "Crucified." The earlier predictions said "be killed" (16:21) and "they will kill him" (17:23). Now the word is out loud.
  • Two different groups do the killing (vv. 18-19). Israel's leaders condemn; Gentiles carry it out. Both hands are on it, and Matthew's Jewish readers are not allowed to file this as a Roman crime only.
  • The resurrection is in every single prediction (v. 19). "And he will be raised on the third day." He has never once announced the death without announcing the rising. Also note the passive voice: he will be raised, by the Father.
  • The very next verse is a request for promotion (vv. 20-21). "Then the mother of the sons of Zebedee came up to him with her sons, and kneeling before him she asked him for something." She wants her two boys seated at his right and left "in your kingdom." He has just described his own execution. Matthew puts the two paragraphs side by side on purpose.
  • Jesus answers the sons, not the mother (v. 22). "You do not know what you are asking" is plural in the Greek, and "Are you able to drink the cup that I am to drink?" is addressed to them. "They said to him, 'We are able.'" The mother spoke; the brothers owned it.
  • He grants half the request and refuses the other half (v. 23). "You will drink my cup" is a yes. "To sit at my right hand and at my left is not mine to grant, but it is for those for whom it has been prepared by my Father" is a no, and it puts the seating chart in the Father's hand.
  • The ten are angry, and Matthew does not flatter them (v. 24). "They were indignant at the two brothers." Not at the request, at the two who got there first. This same argument keeps recurring (18:1; Luke 22:24), right up to the Last Supper.
  • The word for the King's mission is "ransom," and it is bought with a life (v. 28). "Even as the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." The word "even as" ties it directly to the servanthood he just commanded: his cross is the pattern and the purchase.
  • Two blind men see what twelve seeing men do not (vv. 30-31). They call him "Lord" and "Son of David," a messianic title, and they say it twice, louder the second time, over a crowd telling them to shut up.
  • The same question, asked twice, answered differently (vv. 21, 32). To the mother: "What do you want?" To the blind men: "What do you want me to do for you?" One asks for thrones. The other asks for mercy.

Interpretation

Why tell them the details now?

"See, we are going up to Jerusalem. And the Son of Man will be delivered over to the chief priests and scribes, and they will condemn him to death and deliver him over to the Gentiles to be mocked and flogged and crucified, and he will be raised on the third day." Matthew 20:18-19, ESV

Line the three predictions up and watch the resolution sharpen. At 16:21: he must go to Jerusalem, suffer many things from the elders and chief priests and scribes, be killed, and on the third day be raised. At 17:22-23: he is about to be delivered into the hands of men, they will kill him, he will be raised on the third day. Here: named accusers, a formal verdict, a handover to Rome, mockery, the lash, a cross, and the third day. MacArthur puts it simply on 20:19: this was the third time, and "this time, however, he added more details." The point of the sharpening is that no one can later say he was ambushed. A man who describes his own flogging weeks in advance and keeps walking toward the city is not a victim of events. Son of Man is his own favorite title for himself, and here the Son of Man is handed over, which is exactly the language Isaiah uses of the suffering servant.

Hold on to the passive verb in the last clause. He does not say "I will rise," he says "he will be raised." The Father raises the Son. The death is something the Son gives (v. 28) and the resurrection is something the Father does, and both halves are always announced together. Whenever Scripture puts the cross in front of you it also puts the empty tomb there. Never quote one without the other.

What is wrong with the request, and what is wrong with the anger?

"Say that these two sons of mine are to sit, one at your right hand and one at your left, in your kingdom." Matthew 20:21, ESV

Read verse 20 immediately after verse 19 and let it land. He has just said the word "crucified," and the response in the room is a bid for cabinet positions. That is not an accident of arrangement; it is Matthew's argument. The disciples have heard the prediction three times and have absorbed exactly none of it.

A fair question first: Mark 10:35 has James and John make this request themselves, while Matthew has the mother. This is a difference of emphasis, not a contradiction, and the text tells you so. Verse 22 has Jesus answering the sons directly in the plural, which means they were standing right there and the request was theirs. MacArthur's note on 20:20 offers the natural reconciliation: either all three asked, or, more likely, the brothers had worked it out beforehand and put their mother forward to carry it. Matthew, writing to Jews who prized family standing, keeps the mother in the frame; Mark goes straight to the men who are accountable. Two camera angles on one scene. Learning to say that calmly, without either flattening the Gospels into one or pitting them against each other, is a durable skill.

Then the cup. In the Old Testament a cup is a portion God hands you to drink, and most often it is the cup of his wrath: "in the hand of the LORD there is a cup" (Psalm 75:8); "you have drunk from the hand of the LORD the cup of his wrath" (Isaiah 51:17); the cup passed to the nations in Jeremiah 25:15-17. So when Jesus asks "are you able to drink the cup that I am to drink," he is not asking about hardship in general. He is asking whether they can drink the wrath of God, which is precisely the cup he will beg to have removed in Gethsemane and then drink anyway (26:39). "We are able," they say, cheerfully, having no idea. His answer in verse 23 is more sobering than a refusal: yes, you will drink my cup. James was the first apostle executed (Acts 12:2); John was exiled to Patmos (Revelation 1:9). They got the suffering. The seating was never on offer.

And the ten are indignant, which is the tell. If they had understood what the brothers were asking for, they would have been horrified. They are simply annoyed that someone else asked first. MacArthur names it plainly on 20:24: "jealous displeasure, no doubt. They all would have petitioned Jesus for the exalted, favored positions, given the opportunity." Nothing in that room is spiritual. It is a promotion fight. (One note on where the request came from: MacArthur thinks they were playing off 19:28, the promise of twelve thrones, which he reads as a future earthly millennial reign. Our postmillennial home lens takes those thrones as the reign Christ inaugurated at his resurrection and is now extending through history. Either way, the correction Jesus gives here is identical, and the passage does not turn on the difference.)

What does verse 28 actually say?

"You know that the rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their great ones exercise authority over them. It shall not be so among you. But whoever would be great among you must be your servant, and whoever would be first among you must be your slave, even as the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." Matthew 20:25-28, ESV

First the contrast. Gentile rulers "lord it over" and "exercise authority over" people; both verbs carry the sense of pressing down from above. Then four words that ought to be framed over every church office door: "It shall not be so among you." Jesus does not abolish greatness. He redefines the road to it. "Whoever would be great among you must be your servant (diakonos, a table-waiter)" and "whoever would be first must be your slave (doulos, an owned man with no rights of his own)." The ladder is still there; it just points down.

Now verse 28, which is the single clearest statement of the cross in Matthew, and one of the clearest in the Bible. Take it in three pieces.

"To give his life." Not to have it taken. He is the actor. This is the same voluntariness that made him keep walking toward Jerusalem in verse 17.

"As a ransom." The Greek lytron is a commercial word: the price paid to buy a slave out of slavery or a prisoner out of captivity. It is money on a table for a person's release. So the cross is not merely a display of love or a moral example; something was actually purchased at a price, and the purchased thing is people. Be careful about one popular mistake here. The ransom is not paid to Satan, as if the devil held a legitimate claim God had to buy off. As MacArthur puts it on 20:28, "redemption does not involve a price paid to Satan. Rather, the ransom is offered to God, to satisfy his justice and wrath against sin." Our problem was never that Satan had rights over us. It was that God is just, and we are guilty, and the cup had to be drunk by someone.

"For many." Two things sit in this phrase. The preposition translated "for" (anti) means "in place of," not merely "for the benefit of." This is the word you use for exchange, one thing swapped in for another. That is what substitutionary atonement means: Christ did not merely help us, he stood where we were standing and took what was coming to us. And "many" is an echo you are meant to hear. Isaiah 53, the great suffering-servant chapter, ends: "he bore the sin of many, and makes intercession for the transgressors" (53:12), after saying "he was wounded for our transgressions" and "the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all" (53:5-6). Jesus is quietly telling the Twelve which Old Testament chapter he is about to fulfill. Notice too that "many" is a word of scope, not of stinginess; it is set against the one life given, not against "all."

Put the pieces together and the logic of the whole paragraph closes. Why must greatness look like slavery? Because the greatest one there is came as a slave and paid a slave's price for slaves. The ethic is not an arbitrary rule. It is the shape of the gospel pressed into the shape of a life.

Why does Matthew end the chapter with blind men?

"And behold, there were two blind men sitting by the roadside, and when they heard that Jesus was passing by, they cried out, 'Lord, have mercy on us, Son of David!'" Matthew 20:30, ESV

The placement is the point. For four paragraphs twelve men with working eyes have failed to see what is in front of them: a King going to die, and greatness that looks like a towel and a basin. Then Matthew shows you two men who cannot see anything and who identify him correctly on the first try. "Son of David" is a claim that this is the promised King of David's line, and they are the only people in the chapter who say it. The crowd, which can see, tells them to be quiet. They shout louder.

Then verse 32: "What do you want me to do for you?" The same question he asked the mother eleven verses earlier, and the answers could not be further apart. She wanted a throne for her sons. They want mercy: "Lord, let our eyes be opened." He gives it, "in pity touched their eyes," and the last three words of the chapter tell you what receiving mercy does: "and followed him." Followed him where? Up the road to Jerusalem, in verse 17, toward the cross. The chapter that began with a passion prediction ends with two healed beggars walking that road behind him.

One footnote for your peace of mind: Mark 10:46 and Luke 18:35 mention only one blind man, and Luke places it as Jesus approaches Jericho. MacArthur's note on 20:30 handles it well: there were two men, and Bartimaeus was the spokesman the other accounts focus on, and there were in fact two Jerichos, the ancient ruin and the inhabited city beside it, so leaving one and approaching the other is a single moment. Matthew, who often keeps details the others compress, keeps both men.

Application

  • About God He does not merely command service; he performed it, at the price of his own life, and the payment was made to his own justice. The King of the universe defines his own greatness as being spent for other people.
  • About you If you are honest, you have been in the room in verse 24: not scandalized by ambition, just annoyed that someone else got there first. And you have been in the ditch in verse 30, with nothing to offer and nothing to ask for but mercy. Only one of those two postures gets healed.
  • Do Today, do one concrete thing for someone that no one will ever know you did, that gets you nothing, and that you would normally consider beneath you: the dish, the diaper, the trash, the text to the person nobody checks on. Do not mention it afterward. That silence is the exercise.

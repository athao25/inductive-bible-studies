Lesson 0066 · Unit 7: Chapters 24-25

Matthew 25:1-13: The Ten Virgins

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 25:1-13 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 25:1-13 twice in your ESV yet, stop and do it now. Write at least five observations. Try this discipline as you read: make two columns, one for everything that is true of all ten women and one for everything that separates the five from the five. The second column is going to be much shorter than you expect, and that is the whole lesson.

Recall first

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The parable is introduced as kingdom teaching (v. 1). "Then the kingdom of heaven will be like ten virgins." This is a parable about the kingdom of heaven, not a wedding manual, and "then" ties it back to the unknown day of 24:36-51.
  • All ten are inside the circle (vv. 1-2). All ten are invited. All ten are attendants of the bridegroom, not strangers at the gate. The labels "foolish" and "wise" are Matthew's verdict on the outcome, not a description of two different guest lists.
  • All ten have lamps; only five have flasks (vv. 3-4). The foolish "took no oil with them"; the wise "took flasks of oil with their lamps." Read carefully: the foolish are not lampless. Their lamps burn at first (v. 8 says they are "going out", not that they never lit). What they lack is reserve.
  • The delay is stated as a fact, not a flaw (v. 5). "As the bridegroom was delayed." Nobody in the story is blamed for the delay, and nobody in the story is surprised that a wedding ran late; in that world they usually did.
  • All ten sleep, and nobody is scolded for it (v. 5). "They all became drowsy and slept." Wise and foolish alike. Sleeping is never mentioned again, never rebuked, never given as the reason the door shuts. Watch how many sermons hang on a detail this text refuses to condemn.
  • The cry comes at the worst hour (vv. 6-7). Midnight. "Then all those virgins rose and trimmed their lamps." All ten wake, all ten respond, all ten get ready. The response is identical. Only the resources differ.
  • The refusal is a stated impossibility (vv. 8-9). Not "you do not deserve it" but "since there will not be enough for us and for you." That reason matters enormously, and we will spend most of the lesson on it.
  • Two closures, one final (vv. 10-12). "The door was shut" is the first; "I do not know you" is the second and worse one. The parable ends with a command in verse 13, which is the only imperative in the whole passage.

Interpretation

1. What is actually happening at this wedding?

The parable is only strange if you do not know the customs, so set the scene. A first-century Jewish wedding was not a ceremony you attended at a fixed hour; it was a procession you joined. After the betrothal period, the bridegroom came in person to the bride's house, often at night, to collect her. Then the whole party escorted the couple through the streets to the groom's house, where the feast was held (days of it, sometimes a week). The attendants' job was practical: light the way. MacArthur on chapter 25 notes that the "lamps" here "were actually torches" needed for a night procession, and that the celebration began when the bridegroom arrived to fetch the bride.

Two features of that custom carry the parable. First, the arrival time was genuinely unknown; negotiations between the families over gifts and settlements notoriously ran long, and part of the fun was the suspense. Second, an attendant whose torch went out was not merely embarrassed, she was useless, and in the dark she was indistinguishable from a stranger crashing the party. So the story is not about ten women who failed to care. It is about ten women who all cared, all showed up, and all waited, five of whom planned for a delay everybody should have expected.

2. Why will the wise not share? (The heart of the parable)

"But the wise answered, saying, ‘Since there will not be enough for us and for you, go rather to the dealers and buy for yourselves.’" Matthew 25:9, ESV

This verse offends modern readers, and it should be handled honestly rather than softened. Notice first what the wise do not say. They do not say "you should have thought of that." They do not say "you are not worth it." They give a reason, and the reason is arithmetic: split five portions ten ways and you get ten torches that die halfway down the street. Sharing here does not produce two saved people; it produces ten failures. The refusal is not stinginess. It is the recognition that this particular commodity cannot be divided at midnight.

That is the point of the parable, and it is worth sitting with because it cuts against something we assume. Nearly everything else in the Christian life is shareable. You can give away money, time, food, teaching, comfort, even your reputation. But there is a category of thing that is yours or it is nobody's. Interpreters have identified the oil variously: the Holy Spirit, saving faith, genuine conversion, a life of ongoing readiness and good works. Be honest that the parable never says, and be suspicious of anyone who is certain. What the parable does say is the thing all those readings hold in common: whatever it is, it cannot be borrowed in the last five minutes from someone who has it.

Push that into real life and it is sobering. No one is saved by proximity. Not by a believing mother, not by a godly spouse, not by a good church, not by a pastor who prays well, not by growing up in the pew where the light was always on because somebody else brought oil. Those are enormous mercies and God uses them; the means of grace are real. But at midnight nobody can hand you their faith. Ezekiel 14:14 says the same thing bluntly: even Noah, Daniel, and Job "would deliver but their own lives by their righteousness." Faith is personal or it is not faith.

3. "I do not know you": the shut door and its echo

"Afterward the other virgins came also, saying, ‘Lord, lord, open to us.’ But he answered, ‘Truly, I say to you, I do not know you.’" Matthew 25:11-12, ESV

Read that beside something you met back in Matthew 7, at the end of the Sermon on the Mount. There, people cry "Lord, Lord" and list their credentials (prophecy, exorcisms, mighty works), and the verdict is "I never knew you; depart from me, you workers of lawlessness" (7:22-23). The doubled address is the same. The verb is the same. The tragedy is the same: people who expected to be inside, who used the right name for Jesus, who were doing religious things right up to the end, discover that the relationship they assumed was never there.

Note carefully what the bridegroom does not say. He does not say "you arrived late" or "your paperwork is out of order." He says he does not know them, and in biblical usage "to know" is relational, not informational (the same sense as Amos 3:2, "You only have I known of all the families of the earth"). The foolish are excluded not on a technicality but because the lamp with no oil turns out to be a picture of a profession with no life behind it. MacArthur's note on 25:1-13 puts the practical edge on it: when Christ returns "there will be no second chances for the unprepared." He reads the passage as instruction for a future generation living through a still-future tribulation; on our home postmillennial reading much of chapter 24 has already found its fulfillment in AD 70, and chapter 25 turns from that judgment to the final one. Notice, though, that the two frames do not change one syllable of the application here. Whether the door in view is the end of the age or the end of your life, it shuts, you do not know when, and no one else's oil will open it.

4. "Watch therefore": but they were all asleep

"Watch therefore, for you know neither the day nor the hour." Matthew 25:13, ESV

Here is a tension worth naming rather than smoothing over, because noticing it is good inductive work. Verse 13 says "watch", but the parable just told you that all ten slept and that the sleeping was not the problem. If the lesson were "stay awake", the wise would have failed too. So "watch" cannot mean vigilance in the sense of never closing your eyes; it means readiness in the sense of having done in advance what cannot be done at the last minute. The Greek word carries both senses, and the parable itself tells you which one is in play.

That is a great relief and a great seriousness at once. Relief, because you are allowed to sleep. You are allowed to work an ordinary job, raise children, be tired, and not spend your life scanning the horizon in a state of religious anxiety. Seriousness, because the question is not how alert you feel tonight but what you have laid in. A person who has bought oil can sleep. A person who has not cannot fix it by staying up.

Application

  • About God He is a bridegroom who is coming and who is worth waiting up for, and the delay is not neglect. But he is also a judge whose door genuinely closes. Take both halves; a Christ who is only one of them is not the Christ of this chapter.
  • About you Ask the searching question, but ask it correctly. The question is not "do I feel ready?" (feelings are exactly the thing that runs low at midnight). It is "is my faith my own?" And if that frightens you, notice where the parable sends the foolish: go and buy. The remedy is not to stare harder at yourself, it is to go to Christ, who sells without money (Isaiah 55:1). Fear that drives you to him is doing its job. Fear that leaves you circling your own heart is not.
  • Do Today, do one thing that is unmistakably yours and not borrowed. Pray out loud, alone, in your own words, for five minutes, without a book or a recording. That is not a test to pass; it is simply the difference between warming yourself at another person's fire and having one.

Church prompt
This parable is uncomfortable to raise, so raise it gently. Ask a mature believer at church how they would counsel someone who has been in church their whole life and has begun to wonder whether the faith is actually theirs. Listen for whether the answer sends the person to Christ or to introspection. Then ask yourself the harder version: is there anyone in your household whose oil you have quietly assumed is there?

Questions welcome, always: "what does the oil represent?", "if all ten slept, why does verse 13 say watch?", "can a real Christian lose the door?" Ask your teacher.

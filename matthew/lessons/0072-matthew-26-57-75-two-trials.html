Lesson 0072 · Unit 8: Chapters 26-28

Matthew 26:57-75: Two Trials

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 26:57-75 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 26:57-75 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations. Watch especially for where each scene is happening (inside the house, outside in the courtyard), for who is asking the questions in each place, and for what each man says when he is asked directly who Jesus is.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Read the shape before you read the verses

Matthew is doing something with the camera here, and if you miss it you miss the passage. He starts inside the high priest's house (v. 57). Then in verse 58 he steps outside for one sentence to plant Peter in the courtyard. Then he goes back inside for the trial (vv. 59-68). Then, when the verdict is in, he walks back out to the courtyard and stays there (vv. 69-75). Two hearings are running at the same time, in the same building, a few yards apart.

Inside, a man is asked under oath whether he is the Son of God, tells the truth, and is condemned for it. Outside, a man is asked by a kitchen servant whether he knows that man, lies to save himself, and walks away destroyed. Matthew did not have to arrange it this way. He did. Read the two scenes against each other all the way through.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Peter is placed before the trial begins (v. 58). "And Peter was following him at a distance, as far as the courtyard of the high priest, and going inside he sat with the guards to see the end." Every word of that sentence is doing work: following, but at a distance; inside the gate, but sitting with the wrong men.
  • Matthew says outright what they were looking for (v. 59). "The chief priests and the whole council were seeking false testimony against Jesus that they might put him to death." Not testimony. False testimony. The verdict was fixed before the first witness spoke, and the narrator tells you so in plain words.
  • The evidence fails on its own terms (v. 60). "But they found none, though many false witnesses came forward." Liars were plentiful and still could not produce a case, because Old Testament law required two witnesses to agree (Deuteronomy 19:15) and theirs did not.
  • The charge they finally land on is a twisted quotation (v. 61). Two men say, "This man said, 'I am able to destroy the temple of God, and to rebuild it in three days.'" John 2:19 records what he actually said: "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up," and John adds, "he was speaking about the temple of his body." A command to them becomes a boast by him.
  • Jesus does not defend himself (vv. 62-63). The high priest stands up and demands an answer; "but Jesus remained silent." He has answered every question in this Gospel for twenty-five chapters. Here he says nothing.
  • The silence breaks only under an oath about his identity (v. 63). "I adjure you by the living God, tell us if you are the Christ, the Son of God." He will not argue about temple sayings. He will not leave that question unanswered.
  • The answer that convicts him is a claim, not a crime (vv. 64-66). "You have said so. But I tell you, from now on you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven." The robes tear, the word blasphemy is spoken, and the council says, "He deserves death." Nothing else in the trial stuck. This did.
  • The court becomes a mob (vv. 67-68). "Then they spit in his face and struck him. And some slapped him, saying, 'Prophesy to us, you Christ! Who is it that struck you?'" The supreme court of Israel, having just rendered judgment, spits.
  • The accusers outside are nobodies (vv. 69, 71, 73). A servant girl. Another servant girl. Then some bystanders who notice his accent. No soldier draws a sword on Peter. Domestic staff and a northern vowel are enough.
  • The denials escalate in three clean steps (vv. 70, 72, 74). First a dodge, "I do not know what you mean." Then a denial "with an oath." Then "he began to invoke a curse on himself and to swear, 'I do not know the man.'" Each round he commits more of himself to the lie.
  • The chapter ends on a sound and a man walking out (vv. 74-75). "And immediately the rooster crowed. And Peter remembered the saying of Jesus... And he went out and wept bitterly." Matthew gives no comment, no verdict on Peter. Just the weeping.

Interpretation

Was this a trial at all?

Stay with what the text itself gives you. Matthew tells you the council was "seeking false testimony" (v. 59), that is, hunting for a conviction and then shopping for grounds. He tells you the evidence collapsed (v. 60). He tells you the presiding judge stood up and personally cross-examined the defendant (vv. 62-63), which is not the job of a judge. He tells you it was still dark, since the rooster has not crowed until verse 74, and 27:1 says the council reconvened "when morning came" to render the official verdict. And he shows you the judges assaulting the prisoner in the courtroom (v. 67).

The Sanhedrin (the seventy-one-member supreme council of Israel, presided over by the high priest) had its own rules, and MacArthur on chapter 26 lists what this hearing broke: "Such a hearing was illegal on several counts: criminal trials were not to be held at night; and trials in capital cases could only be held at the temple and only in public." He also notes something the text quietly implies: "the men who were trying him were the same ones who had conspired against him." That is the whole problem in one line. Caiaphas is prosecutor, judge, and co-conspirator.

Do not miss the irony Matthew is building. The most religiously scrupulous body in the world, meeting to defend God's honor against blasphemy, breaks its own law at every step in order to kill God's Son. Religion without a new heart does not restrain sin; it dresses it.

Why is he silent, and then why does he speak?

"But Jesus remained silent. And the high priest said to him, 'I adjure you by the living God, tell us if you are the Christ, the Son of God.'" Matthew 26:63, ESV

The silence is not sullenness and it is not strategy. Isaiah had written it down seven hundred years earlier: "He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth; like a lamb that is led to the slaughter, and like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth" (Isaiah 53:7). Jesus has spent this whole Gospel quoting and fulfilling the prophets. He is now doing it with his mouth shut.

So why answer at all? Because of what Caiaphas does next. "I adjure you by the living God" is a formal oath placed on the defendant; it puts him under legal obligation before God to reply. MacArthur's note is right that "Jesus' answer implies acceptance of the oath." And notice what the question is. It is not about temples or witnesses or sedition. It is the question this entire book has been walking toward: are you the Christ, the Son of God? Peter answered that question at Caesarea Philippi and was blessed for it (16:16). Now Jesus answers it himself, under oath, in front of the men who will kill him for it.

Sit with the arithmetic of that. He is not condemned for anything he did. No charge of fraud, violence, or theft survives the evening. He is condemned for telling the truth about who he is. If he had said "no," or said nothing, the case dies with the witnesses. He dies because he would not lie about his identity. Hold that thought exactly nine verses, because a man in the courtyard is about to face a far easier version of the same question.

What is he claiming in verse 64, and why does the robe tear?

"Jesus said to him, 'You have said so. But I tell you, from now on you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven.'" Matthew 26:64, ESV

Two Old Testament texts are welded together here, and every man in that room knew both by heart. MacArthur's note is one sentence and exactly right: "The imagery was taken from Ps. 110:1 and Dan. 7:13."

Psalm 110:1 is the enthronement text: "The LORD says to my Lord: 'Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool.'" Jesus had used this psalm on these same men three days earlier to ask how David's son could also be David's Lord (22:41-46), and they could not answer him. Now he tells them who takes that seat.

Daniel 7:13 is the coronation vision: "with the clouds of heaven there came one like a son of man, and he came to the Ancient of Days and was presented before him," and he receives "dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him" (Daniel 7:14). Notice the direction of travel in Daniel: the Son of Man comes on the clouds to God, to be enthroned, not down to earth.

Put them together and the claim is enormous. The prisoner in the chains says: I am the human figure of Daniel 7 who receives the everlasting kingdom, and I am the one David called Lord, and you are looking at your judge. "Right hand of Power" is a reverent Jewish way of saying "right hand of God" without pronouncing the name, so he is claiming the throne beside God himself. That is why the robes tear. Caiaphas is not confused about what he heard. He heard a man claim deity and dominion, and if it is not true it is the worst blasphemy ever uttered in that chamber. The tearing was the high priest's own dramatic gesture; MacArthur notes it was actually forbidden to him by Leviticus 10:6 and 21:10, with a later rabbinic exception carved out for hearing blasphemy, and that Caiaphas's grief "was as phony as the charge."

Now the two words that carry the freight: "from now on." Jesus does not say "someday you will see." He says the seeing starts here. Something about his enthronement begins at the cross and the empty tomb, and these very men will watch it happen to them.

"From now on you will see": three readings, fairly stated
Christians who take every word of this seriously still land differently on what Caiaphas will see. MacArthur (premillennial, futurist) reads "coming on the clouds" primarily as the visible second advent, so Caiaphas sees it at the final resurrection and judgment; the "from now on" points to the whole era from the resurrection onward that culminates there. Amillennial readers (James White among them) take Daniel 7 as the ascension enthronement, real and present now, with one visible return at the end. Postmillennial readers, this course's home lens, take the same ascension enthronement and press "from now on" hard: Caiaphas's own generation saw the reign begin in the resurrection, Pentecost, the gospel spreading through the empire, and the fall of the temple in AD 70 that ended his order for good, while the bodily return remains ahead. Three notes before you choose. First, all three confess a real bodily second coming; none of them is soft on that. Second, Daniel's cloud-rider is moving toward the throne, which is genuine weight for reading verse 64 as enthronement. Third, and most importantly, all three agree on the sentence that mattered in that room: the man they were condemning was telling them he would shortly be seated on the throne of God. That is the claim they killed him for, and it is true.

Why does Matthew tell you about Peter here, of all places?

Because the contrast is the sermon. Line the two scenes up:

  • Inside: the high priest of Israel asks, under oath, "are you the Christ?" Outside: a servant girl says, "You also were with Jesus the Galilean."
  • Inside: the truthful answer costs him his life. Outside: a truthful answer costs Peter, at worst, an uncomfortable night.
  • Inside: "You have said so." Outside: "I do not know the man."

And watch the escalation outside, because this is how it always goes. The first denial is a dodge that keeps deniability: "I do not know what you mean" (v. 70). The second adds an oath, dragging God's name in to guarantee the lie (v. 72). The third "invokes a curse on himself and swears," which as MacArthur explains means Peter "pronounced a curse of death on himself at God's hand if his words were untrue" (v. 74). In roughly an hour, the man who swore "I will never fall away" (26:33) and who drew a sword in the garden goes from evasion to calling down damnation on his own head. Sin does not stay the size you first agreed to. Each lie needs a bigger lie to hold it up.

And look who does this to him. Not Rome. Not the Sanhedrin. A girl carrying dishes, and then another one, and then some men standing around a fire who noticed he sounded like he was from up north. Peter walked past armed men to get into that courtyard. He is undone by the kitchen. Courage that survives the dramatic threat can die instantly in the ordinary room, which is worth knowing about yourself before it happens rather than after.

Then: "And immediately the rooster crowed." No sermon, no lightning. A bird. And the sound of it drops the whole prophecy of verse 34 back into Peter's mind at once. Luke 22:61 adds the detail that Jesus turned and looked at him. Matthew leaves us with five words instead: "he went out and wept bitterly."

Bitter tears: godly grief and worldly grief

Do not moralize this scene and do not soften it. Peter really did it, three times, with an oath and a curse, after being warned to his face. Matthew is not excusing him.

But the passage does not end Peter's story and neither should you. Two chapters on, the risen Christ sends word specifically "to his disciples and Peter" (Mark 16:7); in John 21 Jesus asks him three times "do you love me?", one restoration for each denial, and hands him a flock; and in Acts 2 this same man stands up in Jerusalem, in public, in front of the same city, and preaches Christ crucified and risen to thousands. The man who could not tell the truth to a servant girl told it to Jerusalem. That is not Peter's resilience. That is grace.

The next lesson will show you the other man, and the difference between them is the thing to carry out of this chapter. Judas also feels the weight of what he did; he says "I have sinned"; he throws the money back; and he goes out and hangs himself. Peter weeps bitterly and goes back to the others. Paul names the two roads: "For godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret, whereas worldly grief produces death" (2 Corinthians 7:10). Both men are wrecked. One is wrecked toward Christ, one is wrecked away from him. Repentance is not measured by how much it hurts. It is measured by which direction you are facing when it hurts.

MacArthur says it as well as it can be said: "The true Peter is seen not in his denial but in his repentance. This account reminds us of not only our own weakness, but also the richness of divine grace."

Application

  • About God: he was condemned for telling the truth about himself while a friend lied about him twenty yards away, and he went to the cross for both crimes. The trial that convicts him is the trial that saves the man in the courtyard.
  • About you: you will probably not be asked at gunpoint. You will be asked at a desk, at a table, in a group chat, by someone with no power over you at all, and the tempting answer will be the small one that keeps things comfortable. Also this: if you have already given that answer, the rooster is not the end of the chapter.
  • Do: name the specific room where you go quiet about Christ, the actual place and the actual people. Write it down. Then do one small honest thing there this week: answer the question straight, say where you were Sunday, decline to laugh at the joke. And if a denial is already behind you, do the Peter thing today instead of the Judas thing: go back, tell God the truth about it out loud, and go be with his people rather than avoiding them.

Lesson 0058 · Unit 6: Chapters 19-23

Matthew 22:23-46: The Great Commandment

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 22:23-46 (ESV, read first!) · Memory verse 8 activates today

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 22:23-46 twice in your ESV yet, stop and do it now. Write at least five observations: notice the parenthesis Matthew adds in verse 23, count how many separate groups question Jesus in this chapter, notice which Old Testament book Jesus quotes to the Sadducees, and watch who asks the last question in the passage.

Recall first

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Matthew tells you the punchline first (v. 23). "Sadducees came to him, who say that there is no resurrection." The question that follows is not a question; it is a trap set by people who do not believe the premise they are asking about.
  • The scenario is built out of Moses (vv. 24-28). "Teacher, Moses said" introduces the levirate law of Deuteronomy 25:5-10, which required a man to marry his dead brother's childless widow. Seven brothers, one wife, no children: the case is engineered, not remembered.
  • Two errors, one diagnosis (v. 29). "You are wrong, because you know neither the Scriptures nor the power of God." Jesus names a doctrinal failure and a failure of imagination, and he answers both in order: power in verse 30, Scripture in verses 31-32.
  • A comparison with a stated limit (v. 30). The resurrected are "like angels in heaven", and the sentence says exactly what that likeness covers: they "neither marry nor are given in marriage". Nothing else about angels is being imported.
  • The proof text is from the Pentateuch (vv. 31-32). Of everything in the Old Testament that speaks of resurrection, Jesus reaches for Exodus 3:6, the burning bush. That choice is deliberate, and the reason is in the Sadducees themselves.
  • The crowd reacts, the opponents regroup (vv. 33-34). The crowd is "astonished"; the Pharisees hear "that he had silenced the Sadducees" and immediately gather. A rival party's defeat produces no joy, only a new attack.
  • Two commandments, two books (vv. 37-39). Deuteronomy 6:5 and Leviticus 19:18, quoted back to back, with the joint at "And a second is like it". Jesus was asked for one commandment and gave two on purpose.
  • He asks the last question (vv. 41-45). Four groups have tested him in this chapter. Then he turns and tests them, from Psalm 110:1, and the passage ends with silence: "nor from that day did anyone dare to ask him any more questions" (v. 46).

Interpretation

1. Why does Jesus argue from a verb tense in Exodus?

Everything in this exchange is explained by who the Sadducees were. They were the priestly, aristocratic, temple-running party, and they held two convictions that matter here: they denied the resurrection (and angels and spirits, Acts 23:8), and they treated only the five books of Moses as fully authoritative. So an argument from Daniel 12:2 or Isaiah 26:19 would have bounced off them. Jesus goes into the one section of Scripture they cannot refuse.

"And as for the resurrection of the dead, have you not read what was said to you by God: ‘I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob’? He is not God of the dead, but of the living." Matthew 22:31-32, ESV

The argument hangs on a present tense. At the bush, centuries after the patriarchs were buried, God does not say "I was" their God; he says "I am". MacArthur on chapter 22 puts it exactly: the argument "was based on the emphatic present tense 'I am' of Ex. 3:6", and it "utterly silenced the Sadducees". Think about what that assumes. A covenant relationship is not a contract that expires at a graveside. If God still binds his own name to Abraham, then Abraham still is, and a man who still is will one day be raised body and all. Notice also what Jesus did not do: he did not scold them for asking, and he did not answer the riddle on its own terms. He went underneath it to the premise.

2. "Neither marry nor are given in marriage": what is actually being denied?

Take this one slowly, because it worries people, and it should be handled with care rather than a shrug. Read what the sentence says and refuse to say more.

"For in the resurrection they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven." Matthew 22:30, ESV

Three things are in the text. First, the verbs are about the institution: nobody marries and nobody is given in marriage there. Marriage is a covenant "till death us do part" by its own terms (Romans 7:2-3), so on the far side of death the institution has finished its work. Second, the comparison to angels is given one specific respect, and only one: deathless creatures who do not propagate have no need of the arrangement. MacArthur notes the extra sting, that the Sadducees denied angels too, so the illustration corrects a second error while answering the first. Third, and this is what the text does not say: it never says that the people you loved are erased, forgotten, or strangers to you. Recognition and love are not abolished; a legal and procreative institution is fulfilled and set down. If your fear is "will I still know my wife", the passage does not answer that fear by saying no. It answers a different question, and the answer to yours is that love in the presence of Christ is not going to be thinner than it is now. What Jesus condemns is a view of eternity so small that it can only be imagined as this life extended.

3. Two commandments, and one question they could not answer

The lawyer (a scribe specializing in the law) asks which commandment is "the great" one, a live rabbinic debate over the hundreds of commands in the Torah. Jesus answers, then adds something he was not asked for.

"And he said to him, “You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind. This is the great and first commandment. And a second is like it: You shall love your neighbor as yourself. On these two commandments depend all the Law and the Prophets.”" Matthew 22:37-40, ESV

Verse 37 is Deuteronomy 6:5, the Shema, the confession Israel recited daily. Verse 39 is Leviticus 19:18, buried in a chapter of case laws about wages and gossip and field edges. Jesus lifts it out and welds it to the Shema. "A second is like it" is the hinge of the whole answer: it refuses to let you have the vertical without the horizontal. Love of neighbor is not a lesser application of religion; it is the same kind of thing as love of God, and 1 John 4:20 later says the quiet part out loud. Note too that the heart, soul, and mind are not three tanks to fill separately; MacArthur is right that the terms "underscore the completeness of the kind of love that is called for". Then verse 40 hands you a reading key: all the Law and the Prophets depend on these, the way a door hangs on hinges. Every command in the Old Testament is an instance of one of the two. That is how you read Leviticus without drowning.

Now Jesus takes his turn. He asks whose son the Christ is; they give the standard, correct answer, "The son of David" (see Son of David). Then he quotes Psalm 110:1: "The Lord said to my Lord, Sit at my right hand, until I put your enemies under your feet." If David, writing in the Spirit, calls this descendant "Lord", how can he simply be David's son? They will not answer, but the answer is the thesis of this whole Gospel: the Messiah is David's son and David's Lord at once, truly human and truly divine. Matthew has been arguing this since 1:1, where the book opens "the son of David" and then 1:23 names him "God with us". Verse 46 closes the door: no one dared ask him anything else. The next words out of his mouth are chapter 23, the woes. When questioning stops, indictment begins.

One side note, since Psalm 110 is a favorite battleground. "Until I put your enemies under your feet" describes the reign of the seated King, and the timing of that subjugation is where the schools differ. The postmillennial reading (our home lens) takes 1 Corinthians 15:25 straight, that Christ reigns now and progressively subdues his enemies through the gospel before he returns. The amillennial reading agrees the reign is present but expects no visible triumph in history. MacArthur's premillennial frame places the subduing largely in a future earthly reign after the return. Jesus does not settle that here, and neither will we today; his point is simply that David's Lord is enthroned now, at the right hand, which every side affirms.

Application

  • About God He introduces himself at the bush as the God of dead men, and by doing so tells you they are not dead. "He is not God of the dead, but of the living" is the safest sentence in the Bible to stand on at a funeral.
  • About you You can hold a view of eternity too small, and you can hold a view of obedience too fragmented. Jesus corrects both in one paragraph: the resurrection is bigger than your imagination, and the whole law is simpler than your anxiety.
  • Do Take the two hinges and hang one real thing on each today. For the first, spend five minutes praying with the mind included: think about something true of God and love him for it. For the second, pick one neighbor (literal or otherwise) and do for them one thing you would want done for you. Today, not this week.

Church prompt
Ask someone at church this week what they expect to be true of relationships in the resurrection, and listen before you correct. Most people have either never thought about it or have quietly grieved it. Then ask an older believer a second question: which of the two commandments does our congregation find harder, and why? The answers tell you more about a church than its statement of faith does.

Questions welcome, always: "will I know my family in the resurrection?", "why did the Sadducees only accept the books of Moses?", "if all the Law hangs on love, do the other commands still matter?" Ask your teacher.

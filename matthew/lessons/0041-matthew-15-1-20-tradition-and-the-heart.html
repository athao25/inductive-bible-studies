Matthew 15:1-20: Tradition and the Heart

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The delegation comes from Jerusalem (v. 1). "Then Pharisees and scribes came to Jesus from Jerusalem." Not the local Galilean opposition; the capital has sent representatives roughly ninety miles north. This is an official inquiry.
  • The charge names tradition, not law (v. 2). "Why do your disciples break the tradition of the elders?" They do not say "the commandment of God" or "the law of Moses". They say tradition, and Jesus will make that word do all the work.
  • Jesus answers a question with a question (v. 3). "And why do you break the commandment of God for the sake of your tradition?" He never defends the disciples' hands. He shifts the trial to the accusers.
  • God speaks and then "you say" (vv. 4-5). The structure is a contrast of speakers: "For God commanded... But you say". Two authorities are set side by side, and the second one contradicts the first.
  • The verdict on tradition is total (v. 6). "So for the sake of your tradition you have made void the word of God." Not obscured, not overbalanced. Void.
  • Isaiah is applied directly to the men standing there (vv. 7-9). "You hypocrites! Well did Isaiah prophesy of you." The prophecy was originally spoken to Isaiah's own generation, and Jesus lands it on this one.
  • He turns from the officials to the crowd (v. 10). "And he called the people to him and said to them, 'Hear and understand.'" The teaching that follows is deliberately given in public, over the heads of the delegation.
  • In and out are the whole axis (v. 11). "It is not what goes into the mouth that defiles a person, but what comes out of the mouth; this defiles a person." One organ, two directions, opposite verdicts.
  • The disciples worry about offense; Jesus does not (vv. 12-14). "Do you know that the Pharisees were offended?" His reply is about uprooting and abandonment, not repair: "Let them alone; they are blind guides."
  • The list in verse 19 is ordered, not random. "Evil thoughts, murder, adultery, sexual immorality, theft, false witness, slander." Thoughts first, then a sequence that walks down the second half of the Ten Commandments.
  • The closing line returns to the original charge (v. 20). "But to eat with unwashed hands does not defile anyone." He finally answers verse 2, twenty verses later, in nine words.

Interpretation

What exactly were the disciples accused of?

Then Pharisees and scribes came to Jesus from Jerusalem and said, "Why do your disciples break the tradition of the elders? For they do not wash their hands when they eat." Matthew 15:1-2, ESV

Get the issue right or the whole passage misfires. This is not about germs. The washing in view is a ceremonial rinse, a ritual pouring of water over the hands to remove the defilement a man might have picked up in the market from contact with Gentiles, sinners, or unclean things. Cleanliness in the hygienic sense is not the question anywhere in the chapter.

Nor is it about the Bible. MacArthur on chapter 15 is precise: the tradition of the elders "was a body of extrabiblical law that had existed only in oral form and only since the time of the Babylonian captivity", and "the law of Moses contained no commandment about washing one's hands before eating", the single exception being priests eating holy offerings (Leviticus 22:6-7). So the accusation is that the disciples broke a human rule, invented centuries after Moses, and the Pharisees present it with the gravity of a violated commandment. That equation, oral tradition treated as binding as Scripture, is the disease Jesus is about to name.

Notice what he does not do. He does not say the disciples had in fact washed. He does not plead extenuating circumstances. He does not concede the rule and beg leniency. He counterattacks: "And why do you break the commandment of God for the sake of your tradition?" (v. 3). The men who arrived as prosecutors are, in one sentence, the defendants, and the standard is no longer their tradition but God's command.

How did the Corban rule cancel the fifth commandment?

"For God commanded, 'Honor your father and your mother,' and, 'Whoever reviles father or mother must surely die.' But you say, 'If anyone tells his father or his mother, "What you would have gained from me is given to God," he need not honor his father.' So for the sake of your tradition you have made void the word of God." Matthew 15:4-6, ESV

The mechanism is worth walking through slowly, because it is the exhibit for the whole indictment. Under the tradition, a man could pronounce a formula over property or money declaring it dedicated to God. Mark, telling the same scene, preserves the Hebrew word itself: "Corban" (Mark 7:11), meaning "given to God". Once pronounced, the vow was treated as irrevocable, and the man could then tell his aging parents that the resources they needed were no longer his to give. In practice he often kept using the property himself until the day it was actually handed over. The vow cost him nothing and excused him from everything.

So a tradition manufactured a pious-sounding way to break the fifth commandment. Honoring father and mother in the Old Testament is not a greeting-card sentiment; Exodus 21:17 attaches a death penalty to cursing them, and in a world without pensions, "honor" plainly includes material support for the old. The tradition let a man look more devoted than the law required while leaving his mother hungry. MacArthur's note puts the responsibility where Jesus puts it: "The rabbis had approved this exception to the commandments of Moses and thus in effect nullified God's law."

Weigh the verdict in verse 6: "you have made void the word of God." Human tradition is not necessarily evil; every church has customs, and some are useful servants. The moment a custom acquires the authority to overrule Scripture, it stops being a servant and starts being a rival, and Jesus does not grade it on a curve. This is the practical edge of the Reformation's sola Scriptura: Scripture alone binds the conscience, and any tradition that contradicts it is void, not the other way round.

Why does Jesus quote Isaiah, and why is he unmoved by their offense?

"You hypocrites! Well did Isaiah prophesy of you, when he said: 'This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me; in vain do they worship me, teaching as doctrines the commandments of men.'" Matthew 15:7-9, ESV

Isaiah 29:13 was spoken about Judah in Isaiah's own day, roughly seven hundred years earlier, and Jesus says it "prophesied of you". That is a deliberate move. The same disease has a fresh host. And the word he uses first, hypocrite (a Greek term for a stage actor wearing a mask), is not an insult thrown in temper; it is the exact diagnosis Isaiah's line contains. Lips near, heart far. The mouth doing religion while the affections are somewhere else entirely. Notice also the last clause: their worship is "in vain" not because it is careless but because its content is human, "teaching as doctrines the commandments of men". Meticulous worship of the wrong authority is still empty.

Then the disciples come with what sounds like reasonable pastoral concern: "Do you know that the Pharisees were offended when they heard this saying?" (v. 12). Jesus' answer is startling.

He answered, "Every plant that my heavenly Father has not planted will be rooted up. Let them alone; they are blind guides. And if the blind lead the blind, both will fall into a pit." Matthew 15:13-14, ESV

He does not soften the sentence, clarify the misunderstanding, or send a delegation to smooth it over. He says in effect: let them be offended. The planting image says that what God did not plant will not stand, however impressive its foliage looks now, which is a quiet claim of sovereignty over the whole religious establishment of Israel. "Let them alone" is heavier still. MacArthur reads it as "a form of God's wrath", signifying abandonment, the same act Paul describes when God "gave them up" in Romans 1. There is a point at which the merciful thing has already been said and refused.

Hold that next to what Jesus is not saying. He is not indifferent to Pharisees as people; Nicodemus is a Pharisee, and so is Saul of Tarsus. He is refusing to let the offense of blind guides set the agenda for the truth. Note too that he only says this after teaching them plainly, twice.

What actually defiles a person?

"But what comes out of the mouth proceeds from the heart, and this defiles a person. For out of the heart come evil thoughts, murder, adultery, sexual immorality, theft, false witness, slander." Matthew 15:18-19, ESV

The argument in verses 17-18 is almost crude in its simplicity: food goes into the stomach and out of the body, touching nothing that matters morally. Words go the other direction, and they come from somewhere. The mouth is a drain in one direction and a spring in the other, and only the spring tells you about the source. "Heart" here is the Bible's word for the whole inner person, the seat of thinking, willing, and loving, not the emotions alone.

Run the list in verse 19 against the Ten Commandments and the order jumps out. Murder is the sixth, adultery and sexual immorality the seventh, theft the eighth, false witness the ninth, slander lands on the ninth and tenth. That is the second table of the law, the commandments governing our dealings with people, walked through in sequence. And in front of the whole list stands "evil thoughts", which is exactly the Sermon on the Mount's argument (5:21-30): the outward act was never the whole commandment. The men who invented Corban to keep the fifth commandment on paper are being told that the second table is broken in the heart before a hand ever moves.

One authorial observation worth making. Mark, telling this same episode for a largely Gentile readership, adds an editorial line of his own: "Thus he declared all foods clean" (Mark 7:19). Matthew, writing to Jews, stops at "to eat with unwashed hands does not defile anyone" (v. 20) and lets the implication stand unstated. Both are true and neither is a contradiction; Matthew is simply more reserved on a point that would have been explosive for his readers, and he leaves the ceremonial food laws to be resolved where the New Testament resolves them (Acts 10:9-16; Romans 14:14; Colossians 2:16-17). MacArthur draws the same distinction carefully: ceremonial defilement "could be dealt with through ceremonial means", but moral defilement "corrupts a person's soul". Comparing the two Gospels here is not a way of correcting Matthew; it is a way of hearing what he chose to say and what he chose to leave implied.

MacArthur adds a humbling footnote: Peter, who asks for the explanation in verse 15, still found this hard years later, needing a rooftop vision in Acts 10:14 before he would eat what God called clean. Understanding a doctrine and having it reach the reflexes are two different works of grace.

Application

  • About God He is not impressed by devotion that costs nothing and wounds someone. He reads the source, not the surface, and his own word is the standard by which every human rule about him is measured, including the rules we like.
  • About you Everyone has a Corban. It is the spiritual-sounding reason we give for a duty we would rather not perform: the ministry commitment that keeps us from an aging parent, the busyness that is really avoidance, the principle invoked exactly when it is convenient. Ask what came out of your mouth this week and where it came from, because verse 18 says it came from the real you.
  • Do Two things today. First, call or visit a parent or an elderly relative, and if there is a concrete need you have been rationalizing away, meet part of it this week. Second, write down one rule you keep as though it were Scripture (a preference about music, schedule, dress, or method) and go check whether the Bible actually commands it. Keep it if you like, but hold it as preference, not law.

Church prompt
Ask your small group this week: which of our practices are commanded in Scripture, and which are simply how we have always done it? The point is not to throw the second category out, since order and custom are good, but to be honest about which is which. Then ask an elder the harder version: has our church ever let a custom quietly overrule a clear command, and how would we know if it had?

Questions welcome, always: "is all tradition bad, then?", "why does Mark say 'all foods clean' and Matthew does not?", "if the Pharisees were sincere, how can they be hypocrites?" Ask your teacher.

Lesson 0022 · Unit 3: Chapters 8-10

Matthew 8:1-17: Authority Over Disease

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 8:1-17 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If not, stop here. Read Matthew 8:1-17 twice in your ESV and write at least five observations. Watch for three separate people who get healed and what makes each one an unlikely candidate. Note who Jesus touches and who he never comes near. Note the two speeches he gives (vv. 4 and 10-12) and how differently they land. Then come back and compare.

A turn in the road

Chapters 5-7 were one long block of teaching, and they ended with the crowd astonished that Jesus taught "as one who had authority" (7:29). Matthew now spends chapters 8-9 proving the claim with deeds. The King has spoken; now the King acts. Same word, authority, carries across the seam: it is the last note of the Sermon and the hinge of the centurion's argument in 8:9. Matthew is not changing the subject, he is finishing an argument.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Three healings, three people no respectable rabbi would want (vv. 2, 5, 14). A leper, a Gentile officer of the occupying Roman army, and a woman. Matthew could have led Jesus' ministry with a synagogue ruler. He leads with the outside edge of Israel's social map, and then in v. 16 opens it to "all who were sick."
  • Jesus touches the untouchable (v. 3). "Jesus stretched out his hand and touched him." Under Leviticus 13-14 a leper was ceremonially unclean, lived outside the camp, and made unclean whatever he touched. Every reader expects defilement to travel from the leper to Jesus. It travels the other way. Nothing in the text says Jesus was defiled; the man is cleansed.
  • The leper's request is precise (v. 2). "Lord, if you will, you can make me clean." He does not say if you can. He grants the power and asks only about the will. Jesus answers in exactly the same two parts: "I will; be clean" (v. 3).
  • The word for the leper is "clean," not "healed" (vv. 2-3). Leprosy in Israel was a ritual problem as much as a medical one. It barred a man from the camp and the sanctuary. So the vocabulary is priestly, which is why v. 4 sends him to a priest.
  • The centurion is healed at a distance and never meets Jesus in the house (vv. 8, 13). Jesus offers to come. The centurion refuses the visit and asks for a word. He is the only person in the passage Jesus does not touch, and his servant is healed "at that very moment."
  • Jesus marvels (v. 10). Matthew records Jesus marveling at faith exactly once. The verb is startling on its lips, and the comparison is deliberately painful: "with no one in Israel have I found such faith."
  • The servant is healed, and then the woman gets up and serves (vv. 13, 15). Peter's mother-in-law "rose and began to serve him." Not convalescence, immediate function. Matthew keeps his healings undramatic and complete.
  • The whole section is capped by a Scripture citation (v. 17). This is Matthew's tenth or so fulfillment formula, and the first drawn from Isaiah 53, the Servant Song about a substitute who suffers for others. Matthew has just told you how to read the healings.

Interpretation

Why does Jesus touch the leper, and why send him to the priest?

And Jesus stretched out his hand and touched him, saying, "I will; be clean." And immediately his leprosy was cleansed. And Jesus said to him, "See that you say nothing to anyone, but go, show yourself to the priest and offer the gift that Moses commanded, for a proof to them." Matthew 8:3-4, ESV

He did not have to touch him. He heals a servant from across town with a sentence, so distance was never the obstacle. The touch is a deliberate sermon. In the Mosaic order, uncleanness was contagious and holiness was not; contact with the defiled made you defiled. In Jesus, the flow reverses. He is the one point in the world where holiness is the stronger force. Hold that thought, because it is the same logic as the cross: he takes what is ours and we receive what is his.

Then the priest. Leviticus 14 prescribes an elaborate ritual for a cleansed leper, beginning, as MacArthur on chapter 8 notes, with "A sacrifice of two birds, one of which was killed and the other set free (Lev. 14:4-7)." He also glosses "a proof to them" simply as "I.e., the priests." That is the point of the errand. Israel's priesthood had a standing procedure for a healed leper that they had almost certainly never had occasion to perform. Jesus sends the man straight into the temple system to hand its officials a case they cannot explain and cannot deny. It is not secrecy for its own sake; it is testimony delivered to the exact people who must render a verdict on him.

Why the silence order, then? MacArthur's note on 8:4 is practical: "Publicity over such miracles might hinder Christ's mission and divert public attention from his message." Mark 1:45 says that is precisely what happened when the man talked. A crowd hunting a healer is not the same thing as a nation receiving a King, and Jesus consistently refuses to be reduced to the former.

Why does a Roman officer understand Jesus better than Israel does?

"For I too am a man under authority, with soldiers under me. And I say to one, 'Go,' and he goes, and to another, 'Come,' and he comes, and to my servant, 'Do this,' and he does it." Matthew 8:9, ESV

Read the argument carefully, because it is better than it first sounds. The centurion does not say "I have authority, so I understand power." He says "I am a man under authority." He knows that his hundred men obey him because Rome stands behind him; his word carries the empire's weight without the emperor being present. So, he reasons, Jesus' word must carry heaven's weight without Jesus being present. Disease is simply a soldier in a chain of command that terminates in God. That is a theology of providence arrived at by a man whose job was logistics, and it is more exact than anything the scribes had said.

MacArthur adds a detail that explains the man's opening line: "Jewish tradition held that a person who entered a Gentile's house was ceremonially defiled (cf. John 18:28). The centurion, undoubtedly familiar with this law, felt unworthy of having Jesus suffer such an inconvenience." So his humility is informed, not vague. He knows exactly what he is and does not argue about it. Note the pairing with vv. 1-4: the leper is unclean by disease, the centurion by birth, and neither uncleanness slows Jesus down for a second.

"I tell you, many will come from east and west and recline at table with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven, while the sons of the kingdom will be thrown into the outer darkness. In that place there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth." Matthew 8:11-12, ESV

This is the first hard word of its kind in Matthew and it is aimed at the home crowd. "Sons of the kingdom" means, in MacArthur's phrase, "The Hebrew nation, physical heirs of Abraham." The rabbis pictured a messianic banquet for Jews only; Jesus keeps the banquet, keeps Abraham, and swaps the guest list. Descent guarantees nothing. Faith like this Gentile's is what seats you at the table. This is not Matthew abandoning Israel, it is Matthew insisting that the promises to Israel were always going to reach "east and west" (Isaiah 49:12; Malachi 1:11), and that the way in is trust, not pedigree. Everything Jesus said in 7:21-23 about people who say "Lord, Lord" and are not known is now given a national edge.

Does Isaiah 53:4 promise physical healing to every believer?

That evening they brought to him many who were oppressed by demons, and he cast out the spirits with a word and healed all who were sick. This was to fulfill what was spoken by the prophet Isaiah: "He took our illnesses and bore our diseases." Matthew 8:16-17, ESV

This verse gets used to argue that physical healing is purchased in the atonement and therefore available now, on demand, to anyone with sufficient faith. The argument deserves a straight answer rather than a sneer, because it starts from something true: Matthew really does connect the healings to Isaiah 53, the chapter about a substitute wounded for our transgressions. Sickness is not a neutral fact of biology. It is part of the wreckage of the fall, and Christ's death is aimed at the whole wreckage, not a slice of it. Anyone who says healing has nothing to do with the atonement is reading past v. 17.

But look at where Matthew places the quotation. He attaches it to an evening in Capernaum, before the cross, when nothing had yet been paid. So he is not saying "because he died, you can be healed." He is saying these healings are what this Servant is like, and they are the leading edge of what he came to carry away. The bearing is real and it is comprehensive; the timing is the question. MacArthur states the balance well: "Christ bore both the guilt and the curse of sin (cf. Gal. 3:13). Both physical healing and ultimate victory over death are guaranteed by Christ's atoning work, but these will not be fully realized until the very end (1 Cor. 15:26)." That is right, and it is not a dodge, because Paul argues exactly this way: the body is still dying while the Spirit is already given, and creation groans for a redemption of bodies that is still future (Romans 8:11, 23).

Then let the Gospel test it. This same Matthew reports John the Baptist executed in prison, Jesus himself scourged and killed, and the disciples promised floggings and death (10:17-22). The New Testament shows Paul leaving Trophimus sick at Miletus, telling Timothy to take wine for his stomach, and being refused three times when he begged for his own thorn to be removed. If guaranteed present healing were the deal, the apostles did not know it. What Scripture guarantees is that the curse is defeated and will be abolished, that the King has authority over disease right now and still exercises it as he pleases, and that the last enemy destroyed is death itself, not a sinus infection this week.

Two pastoral consequences follow, and they cut both ways. First, pray boldly for the sick; the leper's instinct was sound, and Jesus said "I will." Second, never tell a suffering believer that his body is proof of his unbelief. The text will not carry it, and the cross forbids it. The postmillennial hope this course reads from expects the gospel's effects to spread widely and visibly through history, including real mercy to real bodies; it does not expect a sick-free church before the resurrection, and it never makes healing a wage paid for faith.

Application

  • About God His holiness is stronger than your defilement. Everything you assume would repel him is precisely what he reaches out and touches. And his authority does not require proximity, permission, or the right pedigree in the person asking.
  • About you Like the leper, you probably doubt the will more than the power. You believe God could; you are not sure he would, not for you. Verse 3 is the answer to that specific fear, and the cross is the louder version of it.
  • Do Today, contact one person whose situation makes people keep their distance: chronic illness, a psychiatric ward, addiction, prison, a divorce nobody knows how to talk about. Call, visit, or text, and stay past the point of comfort. Then pray for that person out loud, in the words of v. 2: "Lord, if you will, you can."

Primary source
In your local MacArthur notes copy, read the notes on 8:2, 8:4, 8:8, and 8:17 (4 min). The 8:4 note explains the two-bird sacrifice of Leviticus 14 and identifies "them" as the priests, which is the key to the whole errand. The 8:8 note supplies the ceremonial background behind the centurion's "not worthy." The 8:17 note is the one to read twice: it is his statement on healing and the atonement, and it points you to his fuller note at Isaiah 53:4-5, which is worth turning to in your study Bible. For preaching, search the GTY Matthew archive for his messages on the centurion; he is excellent on the chain-of-command argument in v. 9.
Church prompt
Ask this week how your church actually prays for the sick. Does anyone pray with the leper's boldness, or has "if it be your will" become a way of expecting nothing? And on the other side, does anyone in the congregation carry quiet shame for not being healed? Both errors live in the same room. If your church has a prayer list, ask an elder for one name on it you could visit rather than only read.

Questions welcome, always: "if healing is in the atonement, why are Christians still sick?", "was Jesus made unclean when he touched the leper?", "who exactly are the 'sons of the kingdom' thrown out in v. 12?" Ask your teacher.

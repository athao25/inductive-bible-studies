Lesson 0008 · Unit 1: Chapters 1-4

Matthew 3:13-17: The Father's Voice

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 3:13-17 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
Five verses only. If you have not read them, stop and read Matthew 3:13-17 twice, slowly, and write at least five observations. Who travels, and from where? Who objects, and why? Who speaks last? Count how many times someone or something comes down in this scene. Today we go deep rather than wide; the payoff depends on you having looked hard at a very short text.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Jesus initiates (v. 13). He "came from Galilee to the Jordan to John, to be baptized by him." Nobody fetches him. It is roughly a seventy-mile walk south, deliberately taken, for the express purpose stated in the verse.
  • John resists (v. 14). "John would have prevented him." The Greek verb is imperfect: he kept trying to stop it. His reason is a reversal: "I need to be baptized by you, and do you come to me?" The baptizer wants to change places with the baptized.
  • Jesus gives one reason, and it is plural (v. 15). "It is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness." Not "for me." John has a part in this too. Then, quietly, "he consented."
  • The sequence in v. 16 is tight. Baptized, up from the water, heavens opened, Spirit descending, resting on him. Four things happen in one breath, joined by "and behold," Matthew's flag for "look at this."
  • Three Persons, one moment (vv. 16-17). The Son stands in the water, the Spirit of God descends like a dove, a voice from heaven speaks. Three distinct actors, each doing something the others are not.
  • The Father speaks in the third person (v. 17). "This is my beloved Son," not "you are." At the Jordan the announcement is made to the bystanders. (Mark and Luke record "You are"; both are true of one utterance, and Matthew's angle is public testimony.)
  • Nothing is said about performance. The Father is "well pleased" before Jesus has preached a sermon, healed anyone, or called a disciple. The approval precedes the ministry; it does not reward it.
  • Direction matters. Everything in this scene comes down: the Son down to the Jordan, the Spirit down from the opened heaven, the voice down from above. The King's coronation is a descent.

Interpretation

Why would the sinless one submit to a baptism of repentance?

This is John's own question, and it is the right one. John's baptism, as you saw last lesson, was a public confession of sin and a turning from it (3:6). Jesus has nothing to confess. MacArthur on chapter 3 puts John's objection exactly: "John's baptism symbolized repentance, and John saw this as inappropriate for the One he knew was the spotless Lamb of God." Jesus does not deny the problem. He answers it:

But Jesus answered him, "Let it be so now, for thus it is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness." Then he consented. Matthew 3:15, ESV

Work that answer from three angles, all of them in the text or its background.

1. Identification. He steps into the sinner's line. He takes the sinner's place in the water before he takes the sinner's place on the cross, and the second explains the first: "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Corinthians 5:21). Isaiah had said the Servant would "bear their iniquities" and so "make many to be accounted righteous" (Isaiah 53:11). The Jordan is where that bearing goes public. He is not repenting; he is joining himself to the repentant.

2. Inauguration. "All righteousness" includes obedience to everything God has appointed, and God appointed John's baptism (see 21:25, where Jesus asks whether John's baptism was "from heaven or from man"). Priests in Israel were washed and anointed before they served (Exodus 29:4-7). Here the King is washed, anointed with the Spirit, and publicly commissioned by the Father, all before his first sermon in 4:17.

3. A picture of what is coming. Jesus later calls his death a baptism: "I have a baptism to be baptized with, and how great is my distress until it is accomplished!" (Luke 12:50). Going down into the water and coming up out of it is the shape of the whole story, death and resurrection, acted out at the start. This is why Christian baptism means what it means now: union with Christ "in his death, burial, and resurrection" (Romans 6:3-5).

What exactly happens in verses 16-17?

And when Jesus was baptized, immediately he went up from the water, and behold, the heavens were opened to him, and he saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and coming to rest on him; and behold, a voice from heaven said, "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased." Matthew 3:16-17, ESV

This is the clearest scene in the Gospels for what the church later called the Trinity: one God who exists eternally as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, three Persons distinguished from one another but not three Gods. The doctrine is not read into the text here; the text hands it over. The Son is in the water, the Spirit descends onto him, the Father speaks from heaven, and no one is talking to himself. MacArthur's note is exactly right: "Here all three Persons of the Trinity are clearly delineated."

Two details reward attention. The Spirit comes "like a dove," a visible, gentle, unmistakable descent; the Spirit who hovered over the waters at creation (Genesis 1:2) now rests on the man in the Jordan, and the anointing is what makes him the Christ, which is simply the Greek for Messiah, "anointed one." And "the heavens were opened": the barrier between God and earth is pulled back. Matthew's book began by naming him "God with us" (1:23). Here you watch heaven agree.

Why these particular words from the Father?

The sentence is short, and every phrase is a quotation. "My beloved Son" reaches for the royal enthronement psalm: "You are my Son; today I have begotten you" (Psalm 2:7), the psalm where God installs his King on Zion and gives him the nations. "With whom I am well pleased" reaches for the Servant: "Behold my servant, whom I uphold, my chosen, in whom my soul delights; I have put my Spirit upon him" (Isaiah 42:1). MacArthur notes the same pairing, "prophecies that would have been well known to those with messianic expectations."

Hold those two together, because first-century Israel largely did not. King and Servant were kept in separate drawers: one for the conquering son of David, one for the suffering figure of Isaiah 53. In a single sentence over Jesus' head, the Father staples them together. The King reigns by serving; the Servant suffers as King. That is the whole plot of Matthew, announced before Jesus opens his mouth, and it is why chapter 27 can call a crucifixion a coronation.

One further note on where the kingdom stands. MacArthur, reading as a futurist premillennialist, treats the kingdom as spiritual now and "established in a literal earthly kingdom" only after Christ returns. Our home postmillennial reading takes Psalm 2 more directly: the Son installed here is given the nations as his heritage, that installation is made effective in the resurrection ("all authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me," 28:18), and his reign advances through history as the gospel goes out, not only after it. Both readings confess the same enthroned Christ; they differ on the timetable of his visible triumph. The text at hand does not settle it, and the lesson does not need it settled. What the Jordan settles is who he is.

Application

  • About God God is not a solitary ruler who needed a world for company. Before there was anyone to save, there was a Father delighting in a Son in the fellowship of the Spirit. The love that reaches you at the Jordan is older than creation.
  • About you The Father says "well pleased" over a Son who has not yet done anything publicly. If you are in Christ, that verdict is the one you live under, spoken over you before your obedience and not because of it. Your work today flows from approval; it does not fish for it.
  • Do Write out Matthew 3:17 on a card and put it where you will see it before work tomorrow. Then, once today, name one thing you were doing to earn approval (from God, a parent, a boss, a spouse) and do it instead as someone already approved. If you are baptized, thank God specifically for what your baptism pictures; if you are not and you belong to Christ, ask your pastor this week about it.

Church prompt
This passage sits underneath the baptismal formula your church uses, "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19). Ask an elder or your pastor this week how your church understands what baptism signifies, and whether they see the Jordan scene behind it. A second question worth asking: how do they explain the Trinity to a child?

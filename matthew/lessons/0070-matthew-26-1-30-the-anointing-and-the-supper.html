Lesson 0070 · Unit 8: Chapters 26-28

Matthew 26:1-30: The Anointing and the Supper

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 26:1-30 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 26:1-30 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations. Watch especially for how many separate scenes Matthew stacks into thirty verses, for who is planning what, and for every time money is mentioned.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

A note on the pace

Something changes here. Matthew has moved through thirty years and three years of ministry in twenty-five chapters. He now gives three full chapters, roughly one-tenth of the book, to about three days. When a narrator slows down that hard, he is telling you where the weight is. Everything before this has been walking toward the cross; from here the camera almost stops moving.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The formula closes the fifth sermon (v. 1). "When Jesus had finished all these sayings" is the same sentence that ended the other four teaching blocks (7:28, 11:1, 13:53, 19:1), but this time with "all." The discourses are over. There is no sixth sermon; there is a cross.
  • Jesus announces it before anyone plots it (v. 2). "You know that after two days the Passover is coming, and the Son of Man will be delivered up to be crucified." He names the timing, the manner, and the feast. Only then does Matthew show us the council meeting (vv. 3-5).
  • The leaders want the opposite timing (v. 5). "Not during the feast, lest there be an uproar among the people." Their plan and God's plan collide on exactly this point, and Jesus already told you which one holds.
  • The flask is broken over him at a leper's house (vv. 6-7). The setting is Bethany, the host is "Simon the leper," and the perfume is "an alabaster flask of very expensive ointment," poured on his head as he reclines.
  • The objection sounds pious (vv. 8-9). The disciples are "indignant" and speak of the poor. Nobody in the room says the woman has done something evil; they say she has done something wasteful.
  • Jesus calls it beautiful, then calls it burial (vv. 10-12). "She has done a beautiful thing to me," and "she has done it to prepare me for burial." He accepts worship directed to himself and reads her act as a funeral preparation.
  • Money frames the scene on both sides (vv. 9, 15). The ointment "could have been sold for a large sum," and then Judas asks, "What will you give me if I deliver him over to you?" Extravagance toward Christ on one side, a price on his head on the other, one verse apart.
  • The betrayer is named at the table (vv. 21-25). Eleven disciples say "Is it I, Lord?" Judas says "Is it I, Rabbi?" Matthew records the difference deliberately.
  • The verbs of the Supper repeat an earlier pattern (v. 26). Jesus took, blessed, broke, and gave. Those four verbs in that order are exactly what he did with the loaves in 14:19 and 15:36.
  • It ends with singing and walking out (v. 30). "And when they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives." He sings on the way to Gethsemane.

Interpretation

Who actually decided when Jesus would die?

"You know that after two days the Passover is coming, and the Son of Man will be delivered up to be crucified." Matthew 26:2, ESV

Set that beside the council's decision three verses later: "Not during the feast, lest there be an uproar among the people." The most powerful men in Jerusalem sit down, weigh the politics, and resolve to wait. They are overruled inside a week. MacArthur on chapter 26 puts it plainly: "The Jewish leaders, who had been eager to kill him for so long, decided to postpone their plot until a more politically opportune time. But they could not; God's chosen time had come."

Why did the timing matter so much that God overrode the plan of the men who thought they were running it? Because the Passover lamb had been pointing at this for fifteen centuries. Israel's founding rescue turned on a lamb slain, its blood on the doorposts, and death passing over the house (Exodus 12). That pattern, an earlier person or event shaped in advance to fit a later fulfillment, is called typology, and Passover is its clearest case. The true Lamb had to die at Passover, not the week after, so that no one could miss what his death was. Note carefully how Matthew tells it: the leaders are free, the betrayer is guilty, and God's purpose still lands on the exact day.

How can pouring out a year's wages be the right thing to do?

"Why do you trouble the woman? For she has done a beautiful thing to me. For you always have the poor with you, but you will not always have me." Matthew 26:10-11, ESV

The disciples' objection is not stupid. The flask was worth around three hundred denarii, close to a year's wages for a laborer, and Jesus himself had just spent a whole discourse saying that feeding the hungry and clothing the naked is the evidence of a living faith (25:35-40). So the tension is real: does this scene cancel the last one?

No, and notice how Jesus answers. He does not say caring for the poor is unimportant; he says there is one thing more important, and it is worship offered to him. Think about how that sentence would sound from anyone else. If your pastor said "the poor can wait, spend it on me," he would be a fraud. Jesus says it and it is right, which is one more quiet claim to deity in a Gospel full of them. MacArthur is exactly right here: "there is a higher priority than any other earthly ministry, and that is worship rendered to him. This would be an utter blasphemy for anyone less than God."

Then he interprets her act beyond what she likely intended: "she has done it to prepare me for burial" (v. 12). She may only have known that he was worth everything she had. He knows he will be dead by Friday and that his body will go into the tomb in a rush, with the women's spices arriving too late (28:1). Her worship, offered in ignorance of its full meaning, is the only anointing his body receives.

"Truly, I say to you, wherever this gospel is proclaimed in the whole world, what she has done will also be told in memory of her." Matthew 26:13, ESV

Stop and notice what just happened. You are reading this lesson because that promise is being kept. Two thousand years, every continent, and her act is still being told, exactly as he said, in a book that also records that the men in the room called it a waste.

Thirty pieces of silver: why that number?

Matthew is precise about the price (v. 15), and two Old Testament passages stand behind it. In Exodus 21:32, thirty shekels of silver is the compensation owed to an owner when an ox gores a slave to death: the legal value of a slave's life. In Zechariah 11:12-13, the rejected shepherd of the LORD is weighed out "thirty pieces of silver," which he is told to throw "to the potter" in the house of the LORD, a text Matthew will quote outright in the next chapter (27:9-10). Judas prices the Son of God at the going rate for a dead slave, and in doing so walks straight into a prophecy he is not consulting.

Then there is the address at the table. Around the room the question comes back "Is it I, Lord?" (v. 22). Judas asks "Is it I, Rabbi?" (v. 25). Rabbi is a respectful word; it means teacher. It is also the one word in the room that concedes nothing. Judas has spent three years with Jesus and can still address him as a competent instructor rather than as his Lord. That is worth sitting with, because it is a perfectly available way to relate to Christ today: admiring, informed, close to the table, and not submitted.

What is Jesus giving them with the bread and the cup?

"Now as they were eating, Jesus took bread, and after blessing it broke it and gave it to the disciples, and said, 'Take, eat; this is my body.' And he took a cup, and when he had given thanks he gave it to them, saying, 'Drink of it, all of you, for this is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins.'" Matthew 26:26-28, ESV

Three things are happening at once here, and it is worth taking them one at a time.

First, the setting. This is a Passover meal (vv. 17-19). Every element on that table was already a sermon about a lamb and a rescue. Jesus picks up the bread of that ancient meal and says, in effect, this has always been about me. He does not add a new ceremony beside Passover; he takes Passover in his hands and fills it.

Second, the verbs. Took, blessed, broke, gave. You have seen that sequence twice before in this Gospel, on a hillside with five loaves and again with seven (14:19; 15:36). Matthew has been training your ear. What Jesus did with bread for hungry crowds he now does with himself, and the feeding miracles turn out to have been rehearsals.

Third, the covenant. A covenant is a binding relationship God establishes and seals, and in the Old Testament it is ratified with blood. At Sinai, Moses threw blood on the people and said, "Behold the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you" (Exodus 24:8). Jesus deliberately quotes that line and changes one thing: the blood is his own. Jeremiah 31:31-34 had promised a "new covenant" in which sins are forgiven and remembered no more, and here the promise is being signed. And "poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins" reaches back to Isaiah 53:11-12, where the suffering servant "bore the sin of many." Jesus is not surprised by the cross; he is explaining it in advance, in the language of the prophets, the night before it happens.

"This is my body": where Christians differ, and why we can differ kindly
Believers who love Christ and trust Scripture have disagreed for centuries about how Christ is present in the Supper. Rome teaches that the bread and wine become his body and blood (transubstantiation). Lutherans hold that he is bodily present in, with, and under the elements. Some Baptists and others treat the Supper mainly as a memorial that stirs faith by remembering. The Reformed position, which is this course's home reading, sits between the last two: the bread and wine remain bread and wine, and Christ is truly and really present, not physically in the elements but by his Spirit to the faith of those who eat. We feed on Christ, not on his substance in the loaf. Two textual notes support this without settling every question: the disciples were looking at Jesus, whole and unbroken, when he said "this is my body," and Scripture regularly speaks this way of signs (Genesis 41:26, "the seven cows are seven years"). Hold your position with conviction and your brothers with charity. This is a table, and Christ set it.
"I tell you I will not drink again of this fruit of the vine until that day when I drink it new with you in my Father's kingdom." Matthew 26:29, ESV

He ends the meal with a vow and an appointment. The Supper looks backward to a death that has not yet happened and forward to a feast that has not yet come; every time the church eats it, it stands between those two. On what "my Father's kingdom" names here, MacArthur reads it as the earthly millennial reign; this course's postmillennial home reading takes it as the consummated kingdom, the marriage supper of the Lamb toward which Christ's present reign is already moving (Isaiah 25:6-9; Revelation 19:9). Do not let the disagreement eat the sentence. Both readings agree on the thing that should get you through this week: he intends to drink it with you.

And then verse 30: "when they had sung a hymn." At Passover the singing was the Hallel, Psalms 113-118. So the last words on his lips before Gethsemane were almost certainly words like "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" and "I shall not die, but I shall live" (Psalm 118:22, 17). He walked into the dark singing Scripture.

Application

  • About God: he is never the victim of anyone's schedule. The council sets a date, the betrayer sets a price, and the Lamb still dies at Passover, exactly on time, exactly as announced two days earlier.
  • About you: there are two ways to be near Jesus in this chapter. One woman spends everything on him and cannot explain why; one disciple calls him Teacher and prices him at thirty shekels. Proximity is not devotion, and calculation is not love.
  • Do: spend something on Christ today that you cannot justify on a spreadsheet. Give away a sum that makes you slightly uncomfortable, or take the hour you had budgeted for something useful and use it to worship and pray with nothing to show for it. If you take the Lord's Supper this week, go to it having read Matthew 26:26-28 first.

Lesson 0065 · Unit 7: Chapters 24-25

Matthew 24:36-51: That Day and Hour

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 24:36-51 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 24:36-51 twice in your ESV yet, stop and do it now. Write at least five observations. Watch specifically for the little word that opens verse 36, list exactly what the people in Noah's day were doing, count how many times Jesus says some form of "you do not know", and notice the first thing the wicked servant says before he does anything wrong.

Recall first

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The paragraph opens with a contrast (v. 36). "But concerning that day and hour no one knows." Everything from verse 4 to verse 35 ran on things you could see coming: the abomination, the flight from Judea, the fig tree in leaf. Verse 36 turns and says the opposite about "that day and hour". A seam has opened in the discourse.
  • The ignorance is ranked, and the ranking is shocking (v. 36). Not humans, not "the angels of heaven", "nor the Son, but the Father only". Jesus places himself inside the list of those who do not know.
  • The Noah comparison is stated twice, framing the illustration (vv. 37, 39). "So will be the coming of the Son of Man" opens it and closes it. Whatever the flood teaches, Jesus says his coming will be like it.
  • The activities listed are innocent ones (v. 38). "Eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage." Not violence, not idolatry, not the corruption Genesis 6 describes elsewhere. Breakfast and weddings.
  • The failure is named exactly (v. 39). "And they were unaware until the flood came and swept them all away." The verb of judgment is swept away, and it lands on people mid-sentence in ordinary life.
  • Two pairs, split down the middle (vv. 40-41). Two men in a field, two women at a mill. Same work, same place, same day; "one will be taken and one left". The line runs through a workplace, not between nations.
  • The commands finally arrive (vv. 42, 44). "Therefore, stay awake" and "you also must be ready", each attached to the same reason: "you do not know". This is the first sustained imperative in the whole chapter.
  • The wicked servant's sin starts as a sentence (vv. 48-49). He "says to himself, 'My master is delayed,'" and only then "begins to beat his fellow servants and eats and drinks with drunkards." A belief about timing comes first; the cruelty follows from it.

Interpretation

1. What changes at verse 36?

Feel the jolt before you explain it. Verse 34 said "this generation will not pass away until all these things take place", and every event before it came with a warning label: when you see the abomination, flee; when the branch is tender, you know summer is near. Then verse 36.

"But concerning that day and hour no one knows, not even the angels of heaven, nor the Son, but the Father only." Matthew 24:36, ESV

Signs, then no signs. Soon, then unknown. That reversal is the backbone of the partial-preterist reading that is our home lens (lesson 0064 argued it, so we will not re-argue it here): the discourse answers the disciples' two questions in order, so verses 4-35 address the destruction of the temple that fell in AD 70 within that generation, and verse 36 pivots to the second question, the still-future bodily return of Christ, about which nothing can be dated. On this reading the change of subject is what the change of vocabulary is telling you.

Be fair to the other side. Futurist readers, including MacArthur on chapter 24, see one continuous future sequence here and take verse 36 as narrowing the timing of the same event: the signs tell you the season, and the day and hour within that season stay hidden. His note on verse 36 goes straight to application without a break in the storyline, saying the disciples "wanted to fix the precise time, but this was not for them to know", and that "Christ's emphasis instead is on faithfulness, watchfulness, stewardship, expectancy, and preparedness". Both readings can account for the words. What neither reading can do is skip past what verses 37-51 actually ask of you, and on that, remarkably, the schools agree almost completely.

2. "Nor the Son": how can Jesus not know?

Do not hurry past this or soften it into "he knew but would not say". The text says he did not know, and the historic orthodox answer meets it head on rather than dodging it.

The confession of the church (Chalcedon, AD 451) is that the one person Jesus Christ has two natures, fully divine and fully human, united without confusion and without separation. As God, nothing is hidden from him; John 21:17 has Peter say "Lord, you know everything", and Matthew's own Gospel repeatedly shows Jesus knowing thoughts nobody spoke. As man, he had a real human mind that grew: Luke 2:52 says he "increased in wisdom and in stature". So the answer is that the Son, in his incarnate state and according to his human nature, did not know that day, while nothing whatever is hidden from him as God.

That is not a crack in his deity; it is the shape of the incarnation. Philippians 2:7 says he "emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant", which is not the subtraction of divine attributes but the addition of a genuine human life lived under real limits, including hunger, tiredness, and a finite human knowledge submitted to the Father. If you want the pastoral edge: the Lord who tells you to wait without knowing the date once waited without knowing it himself. He is not asking you to endure a condition he found beneath him.

3. Noah's flood, and the surprise in "one will be taken"

Ask what the flood is illustrating, and let verse 39 answer.

"For as in those days before the flood they were eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage, until the day when Noah entered the ark, and they were unaware until the flood came and swept them all away, so will be the coming of the Son of Man." Matthew 24:38-39, ESV

Notice what is not on the list. Genesis 6:5 certainly describes a wicked generation, but Jesus does not cite it. He names four activities that are all lawful, three of them commanded somewhere in Scripture. MacArthur reads it the same way, that the emphasis is "not so much on the extreme wickedness of Noah's day", but on "the people's preoccupation with mundane matters of everyday life" when judgment fell suddenly. The indictment is in one clause: "they were unaware". They were not caught doing something monstrous; they were caught doing nothing in particular, with a boat-shaped warning sitting in plain view.

Now the pairs. Two in a field, two at a mill, "one will be taken and one left". Readers genuinely differ on which position is the good one. Popular treatments almost always assume that being taken is being rescued and being left is being abandoned. But look at what governs the sentence: the flood in verse 39 "swept them all away", and verses 40-41 begin with "Then", pinning them to that image. In Noah's day the ones removed were the ones judged, and the one left was the one in the ark. On that reading, to be taken is to be swept off, and to be left is to survive the day standing. This is not a partisan point. MacArthur, whose framework strongly favors a pretribulation rapture elsewhere, writes on these verses that "one will be taken" means "taken in judgment (cf. v. 39) just as in Noah's day", and adds that this is "clearly not a reference to the catching away of believers described in 1 Thess. 4:16-17". When the futurist and the preterist agree against the popular reading, the popular reading is usually the one importing something. Hold it with an open hand, because able readers do take "taken" as gathered to Christ (24:31 uses different language for exactly that), but do not let a novel or a film do your exegesis.

4. The wicked servant, or why your eschatology shows up in how you treat people

Four short pictures follow, and they are the discourse's own application of everything before them. Verse 42: stay awake. Verses 43-44: if the master of the house had known the hour of the thief, he would have stayed awake, so be ready at an hour you do not expect. Verses 45-47: the faithful and wise servant is defined not by predicting but by feeding, "to give them their food at the proper time", and he is found "so doing" when the master comes. Then the negative.

"But if that wicked servant says to himself, ‘My master is delayed,’ and begins to beat his fellow servants and eats and drinks with drunkards, the master of that servant will come on a day when he does not expect him and at an hour he does not know." Matthew 24:48-50, ESV

Trace the order carefully, because this is the point of the whole chapter. His first move is not violence. It is a conclusion about timing: my master is delayed. From that single belief flow two things, cruelty toward the people under his care and self-indulgence with the master's stores. He does not stop believing in the master; he stops living as though the master were coming. That is what a bad eschatology does. It never stays theoretical. It reorganizes the way you treat the person nearest you.

So the doctrine of the end is intensely practical, and Jesus makes it so on purpose. He was asked for a timetable and gave a job description. Whether you land postmillennial, amillennial, or premillennial, this passage tests you the same way: is the household fed, at the proper time, by you? The faithful servant and the wicked servant hold the same office, do the same hours, and answer to the same master. The difference between them is what each one believes about how long, and what that belief let him do to his fellow servants.

Application

  • About God The Father keeps a date he has told no one, not the angels and not the incarnate Son, and that withholding is a mercy: a known date would produce last-minute religion instead of faithful lives. He wants servants, not deadline-keepers.
  • About you Your working view of "how long" is already shaping your conduct, whether or not you have ever named it. Nobody in this passage falls because of exotic sin. They fall by being unaware, and by assuming there is time.
  • Do Take the faithful servant's job literally today: find one person in your actual household or church whose care has been quietly deferred, and give them their food at the proper time. A meal, a call you have postponed, a bill paid, a promise kept. Do it before you sleep tonight.

Primary source
In your local MacArthur notes on chapter 24, read the notes on 24:36, 24:37, and 24:40-41 in one sitting. The note on 24:40-41 is the one to weigh hardest: watch a committed futurist rule out the rapture reading of these verses on contextual grounds, and ask yourself what that shows about arguing from the text rather than from a system. Then read 24:45-51 and notice that he treats the wicked servant as an unbeliever, and ask whether the parable's own logic requires that. For preaching, his Matthew sermon archive is at gty.org.
Church prompt
Ask two or three people at church what they think "one will be taken and one left" means, then ask where they first learned it. Most will say a book, a song, or a film rather than a Bible study, and that is worth noticing kindly rather than scoring points about. Then ask the better question, the one this passage actually presses: if our congregation genuinely expected the Master this year, which neglected people would get fed first?

Questions welcome, always: "if Jesus is God, how could he not know the day?", "does 'taken' mean rescued or judged?", "if no one can know the time, why do so many people keep setting dates?" Ask your teacher.

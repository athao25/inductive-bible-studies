Matthew 16:21-28: Take Up Your Cross

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 16:21-28 (ESV, read first!) · Memory verse 7 activates today

Did you read it?
If not, stop here. Read Matthew 16:21-28 twice in your ESV and write at least five observations. Two things to track: the four things Jesus says will happen to him in verse 21, in order, including the last one; and every time the words "life" or "soul" appear in verses 25-26. Also note who is speaking to whom in each verse, because the audience shifts twice in eight verses.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • "From that time Jesus began" (v. 21). Matthew has used this exact hinge once before, at 4:17: "From that time Jesus began to preach, saying, 'Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.'" Two turns, two announcements: the kingdom, and now the cross. Nothing else in the book is marked this way.
  • The word "must" (v. 21). Not "will," not "may have to." He must go, suffer, be killed. That single word turns the crucifixion from a tragic accident into a divine necessity, something owed to a plan already in place.
  • The resurrection is in the very first prediction (v. 21). "And on the third day be raised." It was never withheld and never added later. Whatever the disciples heard, they were told the ending from the start.
  • The opponents are named precisely (v. 21). "The elders and chief priests and scribes," which is the Sanhedrin, Israel's own ruling council. The King is killed by his own nation's leadership, not by Rome alone.
  • Peter takes Jesus aside (v. 22). The verb is deliberate and physical. He does not object in front of the others; he pulls his Master off to the side to correct him privately. It is the posture of a superior handling a subordinate's bad idea.
  • The reversal is immediate (vv. 17, 23). Six verses earlier: "Blessed are you, Simon Bar-Jonah!" Now: "Get behind me, Satan!" Same man, same conversation, no one else in between. This is the most jarring turn in the Gospel.
  • "A hindrance to me" (v. 23). The Greek word is skandalon, a stumbling block or trap-stick, the thing set in a path to make a man fall. Jesus is not calling Peter annoying; he is calling him an obstacle in the road to Jerusalem.
  • Jesus gives a diagnosis, not just a verdict (v. 23). "For you are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man." The problem is named as a set of the mind, a default orientation, not a single bad sentence.
  • The audience widens at verse 24. The rebuke was to Peter; "then Jesus told his disciples" applies the same principle to all of them. What was said to one becomes the terms for everyone.
  • Three imperatives, one order (v. 24). Deny himself, take up his cross, follow me. The following comes last, and it comes after both hands are already full.
  • "Life" and "soul" translate the same word (vv. 25-26). Greek psuche. Verse 25 uses it of the life you spend; verse 26 uses it of the soul you can forfeit. The pun is the argument.
  • Two rhetorical questions with no stated answer (v. 26). What will it profit? What shall a man give in return? Jesus leaves the blanks empty because both answers are "nothing."
  • Verse 27 and verse 28 are joined by "truly, I say to you." Verse 27 speaks of the Son of Man coming in glory to repay; verse 28 promises that some standing there will see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom before they die. Whatever verse 28 means, Matthew placed it immediately before 17:1.

Interpretation

How does the man just called blessed become "Satan" in six verses?

And Peter took him aside and began to rebuke him, saying, "Far be it from you, Lord! This shall never happen to you." But he turned and said to Peter, "Get behind me, Satan! You are a hindrance to me. For you are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man." Matthew 16:22-23, ESV

Start with what Peter did not get wrong. He does not withdraw his confession. He still says "Lord." He is not doubting that Jesus is the Messiah; he is telling the Messiah what a Messiah is allowed to do. That is the whole error, and it is worth sitting with, because it is not the error of an unbeliever. It is the error of a man whose theology is right in its subject and wrong in its content, who received a true confession from the Father (16:17) and then filled it out from the neighborhood: a Christ who conquers, not a Christ who is executed by the Sanhedrin.

Notice how quickly received truth curdles into assumed authority. Six verses after being told that flesh and blood did not reveal this to him, Peter pulls Jesus aside to correct him. MacArthur on chapter 16 puts the force of the rebuke plainly: "The harshness of this rebuke contrasts sharply with Christ's words of commendation in vv. 17-19. Jesus suggested that Peter was being a mouthpiece for Satan." That is precise. Jesus does not say Peter is Satan; he addresses the voice speaking through him, and it is the same voice that offered him the kingdoms of the world with no cross attached (4:8-10), where Jesus answered with the same two words, "Be gone, Satan." A cross-free Messiah was the devil's proposal in the wilderness, and here it comes out of the mouth of the church's foremost apostle.

The diagnosis is the part to carry home: "you are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man." Both categories here are religious. Peter's mind was full of God-talk. It was simply calibrated to what human beings find fitting: power without loss, victory without death, a kingdom that costs the King nothing. Every generation of Christians has a version of this, and it never announces itself as unbelief. It announces itself as protecting Jesus' reputation.

What is Jesus actually asking when he says "take up his cross"?

Then Jesus told his disciples, "If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me. For whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake will find it." Matthew 16:24-25, ESV

We have worn this phrase smooth. His hearers had not. A cross was an instrument of public execution, and Galileans had watched condemned men carry the crossbeam through their own streets to the place where they would die. A man carrying a cross was a man with one destination and no way back. So "take up his cross" is not a metaphor for a difficult marriage or a chronic illness, however real those are. It means walking out of your own life under sentence, having already agreed with the verdict.

That is why "deny himself" comes first. The word is the same one used later of Peter denying Jesus (26:34-35): to disown, to say "I do not know him." Discipleship begins by saying that about yourself, so that self is no longer the one whose interests decide the route. And the order matters: follow me comes third, not first. You do not follow Christ and then, later, hand over control. The handing over is how the following starts.

Then comes the paradox, which is stated twice with the same word: whoever wants to save his psuche loses it, whoever loses his psuche for Jesus' sake finds it. Not "loses it and gets a consolation prize." Finds it. This is not a call to self-hatred but to a better arithmetic, and verse 26 shows the math: the whole world on one side, one soul on the other, and the world loses. Notice what that assumes about a single human life. MacArthur's note on verse 26 asks what a person could possibly use to buy back a lost soul at the judgment, and answers in one word: "Nothing." If nothing in the created order is worth one soul, then your soul is worth more than everything you will ever be tempted to trade it for. That is the most extravagant valuation of a human being in ancient literature, and it comes with the price of the cross attached to it.

What does verse 28 mean, and why is it placed here?

"Truly, I say to you, there are some standing here who will not taste death until they see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom." Matthew 16:28, ESV

This is a hard verse, and honest readers land in different places. Four readings are seriously held. (1) The transfiguration, six days later in 17:1-8, where three of the men standing there see the Son of Man in unveiled royal glory. (2) The resurrection and ascension, where the Son of Man visibly enters his reign. (3) Pentecost, where the kingdom's power arrives and the church is founded. (4) The judgment on Jerusalem in AD 70, when the temple fell and the old order ended, a coming in judgment rather than in bodily appearance; this is the partial-preterist reading that fits our home postmillennial lens and that we will work through carefully at the Olivet Discourse.

Argue it from the context, not from the label. Three things push hard toward the transfiguration. First, placement: Matthew writes 16:28 and then immediately writes "and after six days," an unusually precise time note for him, which reads like a deliberate seam between promise and fulfillment. Second, all three Synoptic Gospels put this saying immediately before the transfiguration (Mark 9:1-8; Luke 9:27-36), which is a strong sign the early church heard them as connected. Third, the word "some": only three of the Twelve go up the mountain, which fits "some standing here" better than an event the whole group witnessed. MacArthur takes this view and adds that the word for "kingdom" can carry the sense of "royal splendor," so the promise is that some of them will see the King in his splendor, which is exactly what 17:2 describes.

What about AD 70? It is a real and defensible reading of "coming" language elsewhere in Matthew, and we are not going to wave it off; chapter 24 is where that case is genuinely made, and it will be made there from the text. But here the objection is simply the seam. If Matthew meant a fulfillment forty years out, the six-days note in the next verse is a strange thing to write. Note also that this is one place where our home lens and MacArthur's futurist framework land in the same spot for the same contextual reason, which is a useful reminder: eschatological systems do not decide individual verses. Context does, verse by verse.

Whichever reading you take, do not miss why the promise is here at all. Jesus has just told them he will be killed and told them they must carry crosses too. Verse 27 answers the obvious question, is it worth it, with a repayment at his coming in glory. Verse 28 then guarantees that some of them will get a preview before they die. He does not ask his disciples to walk toward death on a rumor.

Application

  • About God He planned the cross; he did not permit it as damage control. The "must" of verse 21 means Golgotha was decided before Peter ever objected to it, and the resurrection was named in the same breath as the death. He is also a God who tells his people the cost in advance and then shows three of them the glory ahead of time.
  • About you Your most dangerous errors will not be doubts about who Christ is. They will be confident assumptions about what he should do, and they will feel like loyalty, as Peter's did. Ask where your instincts about the Christian life are shaped by "the things of man": comfort, reputation, the reasonable expectation that following God should make life go better.
  • Do Today, write out one specific thing you are currently protecting that Jesus has not promised to protect (a plan, a comfort, a relationship, a standing with certain people). Under it write Matthew 16:25 in full. Then pray one sentence handing that specific thing over by name, not in general. Second, say verse 24 out loud slowly three times and pause at the third verb: the following is what the first two are for.

Church prompt
Peter's error was made in public, corrected in public, and recorded permanently in Scripture by a church that still called him an apostle. Two things to raise at church. Ask a pastor or elder how a congregation can tell the difference between a wrong idea that needs teaching and a "things of man" mindset that needs rebuking, since Jesus does both in this chapter within six verses. And ask an older believer what "take up your cross" has actually cost them in a concrete decision. Most churches quote the verse often and hear its price rarely.

Questions welcome, always: "did Jesus really call Peter the devil?", "if the cross was a divine must, were the chief priests still guilty?", "how do I know whether something is a cross to carry or just a hard season?" Ask your teacher.

Matthew 11:1-19: Are You the One?

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 11:1-19 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
A new unit starts today. Chapters 8-10 showed you the King's power and his sent messengers; chapters 11-13 turn to the question of how people respond to him, and the first responder is the last man you would expect. Stop and read Matthew 11:1-19 twice before another line here. Write at least five observations. One hint: notice where each speaker is standing. John speaks from a prison, Jesus speaks to a crowd, and "this generation" speaks from a marketplace. Mark who says what about whom.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Verse 1 closes a discourse. "When Jesus had finished instructing his twelve disciples, he went on from there to teach and preach in their cities." That is the same formula you met at 7:28; the Mission Discourse of chapter 10 is now complete, and this is the second of Matthew's five closings.
  • John is in prison, and Matthew says so first (v. 2). Before the question is asked, the setting is given: "when John heard in prison about the deeds of the Christ". He is not watching from the riverbank. He hears reports secondhand from a cell.
  • The question is blunt (v. 3). "Are you the one who is to come, or shall we look for another?" Not "explain yourself" but a real either/or, with a second option in it. This is the man who said at the Jordan, "Behold, the Lamb of God" (John 1:29).
  • Jesus answers with a list, not an argument (vv. 4-5). "Go and tell John what you hear and see: the blind receive their sight and the lame walk, lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear, and the dead are raised up, and the poor have good news preached to them." Every item is an event, and every item is a line out of Isaiah.
  • A beatitude with an edge closes the reply (v. 6). "And blessed is the one who is not offended by me." The blessing implies the possibility. Jesus does not deny that he is a stumbling block; he calls the man who gets past the stumbling blessed.
  • Jesus praises John only after the messengers leave (v. 7). "As they went away, Jesus began to speak to the crowds concerning John." He does not defend John's reputation to John's face, and he does not let the crowd hear a word of criticism.
  • Three questions, escalating (vv. 7-9). A reed? Soft clothing? A prophet? "Yes, I tell you, and more than a prophet." Then the verdict in verse 11: no one greater has arisen, "yet the one who is least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he".
  • The complaints in vv. 18-19 are opposite. John "came neither eating nor drinking" and got "he has a demon"; the Son of Man "came eating and drinking" and got "a glutton and a drunkard". The same generation made both charges, which means neither charge was really about food.

Interpretation

1. Was John's question really doubt, and how does Jesus treat it?

"Now when John heard in prison about the deeds of the Christ, he sent word by his disciples and said to him, “Are you the one who is to come, or shall we look for another?”" Matthew 11:2-3, ESV

Take the question at face value before you soften it. Go back to what John preached: "Even now the axe is laid to the root of the trees" (3:10), "His winnowing fork is in his hand, and he will clear his threshing floor" (3:12). Fire, axe, judgment, now. Then look at what John is actually hearing about from his cell: lepers touched, a centurion commended, dinner with tax collectors, no axe anywhere, and John himself rotting in Herod's dungeon for preaching righteousness. Nothing in the reports matched the sermon. A man who has staked his life on an announcement, and is losing his life for it, asks whether he read the announcement right. That is not a shameful thing; it is what faith under pressure sounds like.

MacArthur on chapter 11 puts it carefully: John "was understandably confused by the turn of events", and "it would be wrong to interpret this as a wavering of his faith". That is fair as far as it goes, and Jesus' own verdict in verses 7-11 protects John from any charge of collapse. But notice what the text does not do: it does not excuse the question, minimize it, or tell us John was only asking for his disciples' benefit. Matthew lets it stand. If the greatest man born of women can send that question, you are allowed to bring yours.

And watch the answer. No rebuke, no lecture on faith, no explanation of the delay. Jesus sends back a catalogue of works, and every phrase is borrowed:

"Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf unstopped; then shall the lame man leap like a deer, and the tongue of the mute sing for joy." Isaiah 35:5-6, ESV

Add Isaiah 61:1, "to bring good news to the poor", and you have Jesus' entire reply. The message is essentially: compare what you are hearing to what the prophets said the Messiah would do. John is not asked to trust a feeling; he is handed evidence and pointed back to a text he already knows. That is fulfillment used as pastoral care.

One more thing worth noticing, carefully. Isaiah 61:1 also promises "the opening of the prison to those who are bound", and that is the one clause Jesus does not send back. The Messiah's works are running exactly as prophesied, and the prisoner stays in prison. Hence verse 6: "blessed is the one who is not offended by me". The Greek behind "offended" is skandalizo, to be tripped up. Jesus names the stumbling block honestly instead of removing it. John was beheaded not long after (14:1-12). The answer he got was true, and it did not get him out.

2. How can John be the greatest and also the least?

"Truly, I say to you, among those born of women there has arisen no one greater than John the Baptist. Yet the one who is least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he." Matthew 11:11, ESV

First the praise, and read it as the crowd would. "A reed shaken by the wind" is what actually grew along the Jordan where they had gone out to hear him, and it is a picture of a man who bends whichever way opinion blows. Not John. "Soft clothing" belongs "in kings' houses", a pointed line given that John is at this moment inside a king's house, in chains, precisely because he would not flatter it. He is more than a prophet because the prophets pointed forward to the King and John pointed at him: "Behold, I send my messenger before your face" (v. 10, quoting Malachi 3:1). A prophet foretold; John introduced.

So why is the least in the kingdom of heaven greater? Not because ordinary Christians are better men than John, and not because John is shut out. The comparison is about position in redemptive history, not personal worth. MacArthur states it well: John "actually saw with his eyes and personally participated in the fulfillment of what they only prophesied", yet "all believers after the cross are greater still, because they participate in the full understanding and experience of something John merely foresaw in shadowy form". John stood at the doorway. Anyone inside the room has more than the man at the door, and it is nothing he earned. If you have read the end of this Gospel, you know something John died not knowing.

3. In what sense is John "Elijah who is to come"?

"For all the Prophets and the Law prophesied until John, and if you are willing to accept it, he is Elijah who is to come. He who has ears to hear, let him hear." Matthew 11:13-15, ESV

The Old Testament ends with a promise: "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and awesome day of the LORD comes" (Malachi 4:5). First-century Jews expected that literally, and they knew Elijah had never died (2 Kings 2:11). So Jesus' claim is enormous, and he hedges it with a strange clause: "if you are willing to accept it".

Three things keep this straight. First, John is not Elijah returned. Asked directly, "Are you Elijah?", John answered "I am not" (John 1:21). Second, the angel had already given the right category: John would go "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17), which is a description of office and message, not of personal identity. Third, Jesus says it flatly later, without the conditional: "Elijah has already come, and they did not recognize him" (17:12). So the prophecy landed on John.

What, then, is the "if"? Here the lenses diverge, so weigh them by the text. MacArthur on chapter 11 writes, "If they had believed, John would have been the fulfillment of the Elijah prophecies", and cross-references Revelation 11. That reading is driven by his premillennial and futurist frame: because Israel refused the kingdom offer, the Elijah prophecy is held open for a literal future forerunner before a still-future coming. The postmillennial reading this course works from (and the amillennial reading with it) takes 17:12 as the decider: Elijah has come, full stop, and the conditional in 11:14 is about the hearers, not about the prophecy. "If you are willing to accept it" is the same move as "he who has ears to hear, let him hear" in the very next line: the fact is settled, your reception of it is not. Malachi's forerunner arrived, most of the generation missed him, and missing him is the theme of the next three verses.

4. Why children in a marketplace?

"For John came neither eating nor drinking, and they say, ‘He has a demon.’ The Son of Man came eating and drinking, and they say, ‘Look at him! A glutton and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners!’ Yet wisdom is justified by her deeds." Matthew 11:18-19, ESV

The picture is petulant children who will not play either game: "We played the flute for you, and you did not dance; we sang a dirge, and you did not mourn." Wedding music, funeral music, and this crowd sits out both. John came with the dirge, an ascetic in camel's hair eating locusts, and was written off as demon-possessed. The Son of Man came with the flute, at table with tax collectors, and was written off as a drunk. Line the two charges up and they cancel: no one can be too austere and too free at once. Which means the objection was never about style. It was about not wanting either messenger, and the reasons were assembled afterward.

"Yet wisdom is justified by her deeds" is the verdict. God's wisdom does not have to win the argument in the marketplace; it is vindicated by what it actually accomplishes. Which is exactly the answer Jesus gave John twelve verses earlier: look at the deeds.

Application

  • About God He does not scold honest doubt, and he does not always relieve the circumstance that caused it. He gives John evidence and a beatitude, then lets him die in prison, and still calls him the greatest born of women. The kindness and the severity are in the same paragraph.
  • About you Some of your questions come from a gap between what you expected God to do and what he is doing. That gap is real, and the fix is not to pretend the expectation was fine. It is to check the expectation against what Scripture actually promised.
  • Do Write down, in one sentence, the thing you half-expected God to have done by now that he has not done. Under it, write the verse you thought promised it, and read that verse in context today. If it does not say what you assumed, cross the sentence out. If it does, take it to prayer as a request, not a grievance.

Primary source
In your local MacArthur notes, read the notes on 11:3, 11:11, and 11:14 in that order. On 11:3, weigh his line that John "wondered if he had misunderstood Jesus' agenda" against the text's own silence about John's motive. On 11:11, his greatest-and-least explanation is the clearest short statement of it you will find. On 11:14, watch the futurist frame surface in one clause, "If they had believed, John would have been the fulfillment", and then test it against Matthew 17:12. Church prompt
Most churches are better at celebrating certainty than at handling doubt, and the quiet ones with questions usually assume they are the only ones. This week, tell one trusted person at church the actual question you have been carrying, in John's plain form rather than a tidied version. Then ask them what they did with theirs. Jesus' reply to a doubter was evidence and a blessing, not a reprimand; a church can do that too.

Questions welcome, always: "if John already called Jesus the Lamb of God, how could he ask this?", "does 'the violent take it by force' in verse 12 mean something good or something bad?", "why does Jesus quote Isaiah instead of just saying yes?" Ask your teacher.

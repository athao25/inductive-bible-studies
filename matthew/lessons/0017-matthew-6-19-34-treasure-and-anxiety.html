Matthew 6:19-34: Treasure and Anxiety

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 6:19-34 (ESV, read first!) · Memory verse 4 activates today

Did you read it?
If not, stop here. Read Matthew 6:19-34 twice in your ESV and write at least five observations. Watch for the connecting words especially: circle every "for", "therefore", and "but" you find. Note where the paragraph about money ends and the paragraph about worry begins, and ask whether Jesus thinks they are two subjects or one.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Two treasuries, one command repeated (vv. 19-20). "Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth" then "lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven." Same verb, same phrase "for yourselves," opposite address. Jesus does not forbid saving; he redirects the account.
  • The reason is decay, not evil (vv. 19-20). Moth, rust, thieves. Earthly treasure is condemned for being perishable and stealable, not for being material. Heaven's vault simply has better security.
  • The hinge of the whole section (v. 21). "For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also." Note the direction: treasure first, heart second. Your affections follow your investments, not the other way around.
  • A sudden shift to eyesight (vv. 22-23). "The eye is the lamp of the body." A strange interruption between money and money, unless the eye language is itself about money. It is; see the Interpretation section.
  • Slavery language, not employment language (v. 24). "No one can serve two masters." The verb is the one for a bond-slave, and the four reactions are hate, love, devoted, despise. These are heart words. Nobody uses them about a payroll.
  • Money is given a name and a throne (v. 24). "You cannot serve God and money." The Aramaic word behind it, mammon (wealth, property, that which one trusts), stands here as a personal rival to God, a competing lord rather than a neutral tool.
  • "Therefore" starts the anxiety section (v. 25). Jesus does not change subjects. Worry is presented as the direct consequence of the master problem just described.
  • Three anxiety commands, three arguments (vv. 25, 31, 34). "Do not be anxious" lands three times, each followed by a reason: the birds (v. 26), the lilies (vv. 28-30), and the Father's knowledge (v. 32). The refrain is a structure, not a repetition.
  • The comparisons are all lesser to greater (vv. 26, 30). "Are you not of more value than they?" and "will he not much more clothe you." Jesus argues from what God already does for cheap things to what he certainly does for valuable ones.
  • The command is not "do not plan" (v. 25). It is "do not be anxious." Birds gather food, they simply do not gather into barns for the fear of tomorrow. And v. 34 does not say tomorrow has no trouble; it says today's is enough.

Interpretation

Why does an eye exam sit between the treasure chest and the two masters?

"The eye is the lamp of the body. So, if your eye is healthy, your whole body will be full of light, but if your eye is bad, your whole body will be full of darkness. If then the light in you is darkness, how great is the darkness!" Matthew 6:22-23, ESV

Read in English this looks like a detour into optics. In Jesus' idiom it is not. The word translated "healthy" is haplous, meaning single, simple, undivided, and in Jewish usage a "single eye" was a settled expression for generosity; the same root shows up for generous giving in Romans 12:8 and 2 Corinthians 8:2 and 9:11. Its opposite, the "evil eye," was the standard Hebrew phrase for stinginess and grudging envy (Deuteronomy 15:9; Proverbs 23:6; 28:22). So Jesus is asking a money question in a body metaphor: is your eye open-handed or grasping?

MacArthur on chapter 6 takes the verses as "an argument from the lesser to the greater," where a diseased eye leaves the body dark, and how much worse when "the darkness actually emanates from within." That is right about the logic and right about the target, the Pharisees' "superficial earthly religion that left their hearts dark." Add the idiom and the placement makes sense: v. 21 says the heart follows the treasure, vv. 22-23 say a grasping eye darkens the whole person, v. 24 says the grasping has become a master. Three steps down one staircase.

Is verse 24 about budgeting or about worship?

"No one can serve two masters, for either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve God and money." Matthew 6:24, ESV

Worship. Notice that Jesus does not say you should not, or that it is difficult; he says you cannot. A slave in that world had no second employer, no evenings free. And by naming mammon opposite God, Jesus treats wealth as a personality with claims on you: it promises security, status, and a future, which are exactly the things God promises. MacArthur's note on v. 24 keeps it plain, "money. Earthly, material treasures," and his note on v. 20 draws the practical conclusion: do not amass earthly wealth, and do use financial assets "for purposes that are heavenly and eternal."

This is why a spreadsheet cannot fix the problem in this passage. You can be poor and serve mammon by resentment and fear, and you can be wealthy and serve God by open-handed stewardship. The diagnostic question is not how much you have but which one you would obey if they gave conflicting orders this week.

Why does the anxiety section follow the money section?

"Therefore I tell you, do not be anxious about your life, what you will eat or what you will drink, nor about your body, what you will put on. Is not life more than food, and the body more than clothing?" Matthew 6:25, ESV

Because of that "therefore." Most readers treat 6:25-34 as a standalone comfort passage for hard days, and then wonder why the comfort will not stick. Jesus files it as the second half of a single argument: anxiety about provision is the emotional signature of serving the wrong master. If mammon is your lord, worry is simply what it feels like to work for an employer who cannot guarantee anything.

Then he argues from the smaller to the greater, twice. Birds "neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns, and yet your heavenly Father feeds them. Are you not of more value than they?" (v. 26). Lilies "neither toil nor spin," yet outshine Solomon at the height of his wealth, and God dresses them knowing the grass will be fuel by tomorrow (vv. 28-30). Both arguments end in the same place, and it is not positive thinking. The ground of freedom from worry is the Father's valuation of you. Worry is therefore a doctrinal error before it is a feeling, which is why the rebuke in v. 30 is "O you of little faith," a phrase MacArthur rightly flags as Jesus' recurring word to weak disciples (compare 8:26; 14:31; 16:8).

Verse 32 sharpens it to the point of offense: "For the Gentiles seek after all these things, and your heavenly Father knows that you need them all." Gentile (anyone not of Israel) is a covenant category here, and MacArthur defines it as "those outside the people of promise and outside the blessing of God." Anxiety about provision is functionally pagan, because it lives as though nobody fatherly is running the world. That is a hard sentence, and it is meant to be. Then comes the alternative, and it is a reordering rather than a suppression: "But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you" (v. 33). Not seek only, and not seek and receive nothing else. Seek first. On the kingdom here, MacArthur notes that it "refers to the sphere of salvation," so seeking first is seeking God's saving reign and the righteous life that belongs to it. Verse 34 then shrinks the horizon to a size you can carry: "Sufficient for the day is its own trouble." God gives grace in daily rations, the way he gave manna.

Application

  • About God He is a Father who feeds birds he did not adopt and clothes grass he will burn tomorrow. He knows what you need before you list it (v. 32). Your provision is not a negotiation, it is a household arrangement.
  • About you Your bank statement and your calendar are a map of your heart, because treasure comes first and the heart follows (v. 21). And your worry is not merely a temperament; it is telling you which lord you reported to this week.
  • Do Two things today. First, take the one thing you have worried about most this week and write it in a single sentence, then write next to it what it assumes about your Father, and pray it back to him honestly. Second, give something away today, an actual amount of money or a real possession, chosen large enough that you feel it. A single eye is trained by using it.

For fuller treatment, his Matthew sermons on 6:19-34 are in the GTY Matthew archive; look for the messages on treasure in heaven, the two masters, and the cure for anxiety.
Church prompt
Money and worry are the two things Christians are least likely to say out loud in a church building. Break that this week: ask one trusted believer in your congregation how they decide what to give and what to keep, and tell them one financial fear you carry. Also worth raising with an elder or deacon: who in our church is currently in genuine material need, and what would it look like for me to be part of the Father's answer to their anxiety?

Questions welcome, always: "does verse 20 forbid savings accounts and retirement?", "what does 'all these things will be added' promise, exactly, and to whom?", "if worry is sin, what do I do with anxiety that has a medical cause?" Ask your teacher.

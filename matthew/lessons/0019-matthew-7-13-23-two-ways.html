Matthew 7:13-23: Two Ways

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 7:13-23 (ESV, read first!)

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Everything comes in twos (vv. 13-23). Two gates, two ways, two destinations, two crowds (vv. 13-14); two trees and two kinds of fruit (vv. 17-20); two groups on the last day (vv. 21-23). Next lesson adds two builders. The Sermon does not trail off, it narrows to a fork in the road.
  • The first verb is a command (v. 13). "Enter by the narrow gate." Not consider, not admire. Jesus has preached for three chapters and now demands a move. Nobody drifts through a narrow gate; the wide one requires no decision at all.
  • The wide way is described first and by its crowd (v. 13). Wide gate, easy way, "those who enter by it are many," and it "leads to destruction." Ease and company are the two things that make it attractive, and neither is evidence that it is right.
  • The narrow way is hard and its people are "few" (v. 14). The word for the way is pressed, constricted. Note that the few are described as those who "find it," which implies searching.
  • The danger is disguise, not distance (v. 15). False prophets "come to you," inside the flock, "in sheep's clothing but inwardly are ravenous wolves." The threat is not out there in the pagan world; it walks in wearing the right clothes.
  • The test is repeated as a bracket (vv. 16, 20). "You will recognize them by their fruits" opens and closes the paragraph. Between the brackets: thornbushes do not yield grapes, and a healthy tree "cannot" bear bad fruit (v. 18). Nature, not effort, decides the fruit.
  • "Lord, Lord" is said twice, by two different groups (vv. 21, 22). Saying it is not the problem. Verse 21 gives the true test in one line: "the one who does the will of my Father who is in heaven."
  • The rejected have an impressive resume (v. 22). Prophecy, exorcism, "many mighty works," all "in your name." These are not nominal churchgoers; they are the ministry-active. And the verdict against them is not "you did not try" but "I never knew you" (v. 23).
  • Jesus is the one on the bench (vv. 22-23). They plead to him, "on that day," and he pronounces the sentence. Watch who is doing the judging at the end of the world.

Interpretation

If the way is hard and the few find it, is the kingdom doomed to be small?

"Enter by the narrow gate. For the gate is wide and the way is easy that leads to destruction, and those who enter by it are many. For the gate is narrow and the way is hard that leads to life, and those who find it are few." Matthew 7:13-14, ESV

Take the sober note seriously first. Jesus is standing in front of a large, enthusiastic crowd (7:28) and tells them that popularity is no proof of safety. MacArthur on chapter 7 is right that both gates claim to lead to God: "The narrow gate is by faith, only through Christ, constricted and precise... The wide gate includes all religions of works and self-righteousness, with no single way." That is the point of the image. Wide means room for every opinion; narrow means one Christ, one door, one way.

Now the discipline: this is a sermon addressed to a crowd of hearers on a hillside, not a statistical forecast about the total population of history. Jesus is telling these people, right now, that most of the religious traffic around them is headed the wrong way. If you turn v. 14 into a proof text that the redeemed will always be a small minority in every age, you have to answer for the mustard seed that becomes the largest of garden plants and the leaven that works through the whole lump (13:31-33), for a kingdom given to "all nations" (28:19), and for the multitude no one can number (Revelation 7:9). Our postmillennial home lens (see postmillennialism) expects the gospel to succeed broadly in history; premillennial and amillennial readers expect less. But none of that is what verse 14 is measuring. Narrow describes the door, not the final headcount. The gate stays exactly as narrow when the crowds pouring through it are enormous.

Why fruit, and not doctrine or eloquence, as the test for a false teacher?

"Beware of false prophets, who come to you in sheep's clothing but inwardly are ravenous wolves. You will recognize them by their fruits." Matthew 7:15-16, ESV

Because the whole problem is that they look right. A wolf that came in growling would need no warning label. MacArthur adds a sharp note on v. 15: they "deceive not by disguising themselves as sheep, but by impersonating true shepherds," since wool was the working shepherd's own garb. So the counterfeit is not a stranger in the pew, it is a man in the pulpit. Appearance is disqualified as evidence precisely because appearance is the disguise, and so is charisma, and so is a crowd.

Fruit works as a test for one reason: fruit takes time. You cannot fake a decade. Jesus' agricultural picture is not "check his statement of faith," it is "watch the tree through a few seasons." That makes discernment patient work rather than a snap verdict, and it fits what you learned last lesson about judging: 7:1 forbids the censorious, self-appointed judge, while 7:15-20 commands careful, evidence-weighing discernment. What is the fruit? Both the character the Sermon has been describing (5:3-12, 5:43-48) and the doctrine taught, since a corrupt message eventually shows up in a corrupt life. Note also v. 18: a healthy tree cannot bear bad fruit. Jesus is not describing behavior that can be managed but a nature that will out.

How can someone prophesy in Jesus' name and still hear "I never knew you"?

"On that day many will say to me, 'Lord, Lord, did we not prophesy in your name, and cast out demons in your name, and do many mighty works in your name?' And then will I declare to them, 'I never knew you; depart from me, you workers of lawlessness.'" Matthew 7:22-23, ESV

These are the most frightening verses in the Sermon, so read them precisely rather than emotionally. Notice what these people have: the right title for Jesus, supernatural ministry, and volume ("many mighty works"). Notice what the text never says they have: the will of the Father done (v. 21), and any relationship with Christ. MacArthur puts the resume itself under suspicion: "their whole confidence was in these works, further proof that these works, spectacular as they might have appeared, could not have been authentic." The word Jesus uses for them, "lawlessness," is the same category 1 John 3:4 gives to all sin. Their ministry was a career; their lives were unrepentant.

The verdict is relational, and that is the mercy hidden inside the terror. "I never knew you" is not "your numbers came up short." Salvation was never a threshold of works to clear; it is being known by Christ, which is why nobody can accidentally fall below the line. So if this passage has just put a knot in your stomach, do not leave it free-floating. Answer it the way Scripture does. First with the gospel: Christ died for sinners and receives everyone who comes to him, and "whoever comes to me I will never cast out" (John 6:37). The people in v. 22 never came to him at all; they came to their own resume. Second with the tests 1 John supplies for exactly this anxiety: do you believe Jesus is the Christ come in the flesh (1 John 5:1), do you love the brothers (3:14), do you keep his commandments even while you keep repenting when you fail (2:3-6). Repentance that keeps returning to Christ is itself evidence of life; the wolves in v. 22 never repented of anything, they only performed. Fear that drives you to Christ is doing its job. Fear that drives you into paralysis is not from him.

One more thing, easy to miss. Jesus casts himself as the judge on the last day, receiving the pleas of the nations and deciding eternal destinies with a sentence. No prophet in Israel ever spoke like that. It sits alongside "but I say to you" (5:22) and the forgiving of sins yet to come (9:2) as an implicit claim to be God, made without ever announcing itself.

Application

  • About God He is honest with us. A God indifferent to our destination would not describe the wide road at all, much less warn us while the crowd is still cheering. And he is the one who both sets the narrow gate and stands at the end of the way as judge.
  • About you The frightening thing about v. 22 is that activity is not evidence. You can serve, speak, give, and organize and still not be known. The question is not "have I done enough for Christ?" but "does Christ know me, and am I doing the Father's will?"
  • Do Two things today, both small. Take ten minutes and read 1 John 2:3-6, 3:14, and 5:11-13 slowly, then write one sentence naming where your assurance actually rests. Then pick one command of the Father you know you are avoiding, something specific enough to name in five words, and obey it before you sleep.

Church prompt
Verse 15 says the wolves come dressed as shepherds, which means a congregation needs more than one pair of eyes. Ask an elder this week how your church evaluates the teaching it takes in, from the pulpit and from books, podcasts, and conferences members bring home. Then ask the harder half of it: what does our church do for someone who says out loud, "I am not sure Christ knows me"? Verses 21-23 land on real people in real pews, and they need a pastor, not a lecture.

Questions welcome, always: "does 'few find it' mean most people are lost forever?", "how do I tell fruit from a good performance?", "can a truly saved person doubt their salvation?" Ask your teacher.

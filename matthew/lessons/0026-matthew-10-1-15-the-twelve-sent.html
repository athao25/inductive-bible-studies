Matthew 10:1-15: The Twelve Sent

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 10:1-15 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If not, stop here. Read Matthew 10:1-15 twice in your ESV and write at least five observations. Two things to track as you read: how many times the number twelve appears, and every extra word attached to a name in the list (whose brother, whose son, what job, what party, what he later did). Those attached words are not filler; Matthew chose each one.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • A new block of teaching starts here (v. 1). Chapters 8 and 9 were a run of miracle scenes; from 10:5 to 11:1 Jesus talks almost without interruption. This is the second of the five great blocks of teaching Matthew built his book around, the Mission Discourse, and like the others it closes with the formula "when Jesus had finished" (11:1). See the five listed on the book map.
  • The authority is handed over, not shared out (v. 1). "Gave them authority over unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to heal every disease and every affliction." Compare that sentence with what you have just watched Jesus himself do in chapters 8 and 9. It is the same list, in the same order. The works of the King become the works of the sent ones.
  • The vocabulary changes mid-sentence (vv. 1-2). Verse 1 calls them "his twelve disciples"; verse 2 calls them "the twelve apostles." Nothing happened in between except the sending. Disciple means learner; apostle means one sent with authority to represent the sender.
  • Twelve is stated three times in three verses (vv. 1, 2, 5). Matthew will not let the number pass unnoticed. Israel had twelve tribes. A Jewish reader counts along.
  • The names come in pairs (vv. 2-4). "Simon... and Andrew his brother; James... and John his brother; Philip and Bartholomew; Thomas and Matthew..." Six pairs, not a loose list, which fits Mark 6:7, where they go out two by two.
  • Matthew labels himself and nobody else labels him (v. 3). "Thomas and Matthew the tax collector." The parallel lists in Mark 3:18, Luke 6:15, and Acts 1:13 name Matthew without the job title. Only his own Gospel keeps it attached.
  • The list contains two political opposites (vv. 3-4). A tax collector, who worked for Rome, and "Simon the Zealot," whose party existed to throw Rome out, in one band of twelve men. Outside of Jesus these two do not share a road, let alone a mission.
  • The last name always carries its ending (v. 4). "Judas Iscariot, who betrayed him." Every apostle list in the New Testament ends there and says so, so the reader is never allowed to forget how the story turns.
  • The mission is fenced (vv. 5-6). "Go nowhere among the Gentiles and enter no town of the Samaritans, but go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel." A prohibition, then a redirection. Note that a fence has an inside as well as an outside.
  • The sermon is a quotation (v. 7). "The kingdom of heaven is at hand" is word for word what John the Baptist preached (3:2) and what Jesus preached (4:17). The messengers add nothing and subtract nothing.
  • Free in, free out (v. 8). "You received without paying; give without pay." Yet two verses later, "the laborer deserves his food." Not a contradiction, but hold both.
  • The response of a town is weighed (vv. 13-15). Peace comes on a worthy house or returns to the sender; dust is shaken off a refusing one; and Sodom fares better on the day of judgment than that town. The verdict scales with the light received.

Interpretation

Why twelve, and why does Matthew keep saying it?

Jesus had more than twelve followers, and he could have sent seven or seventy (he does send seventy-two later, Luke 10:1). He picked twelve and Matthew reports the number three times in five verses, because in Israel twelve is never merely a quantity. Twelve tribes came out of Egypt, twelve stones were set up at the Jordan, twelve loaves sat on the table in the tabernacle. To appoint twelve men and send them to "the lost sheep of the house of Israel" is to make a claim: the King is not founding a club alongside Israel, he is constituting Israel again around himself. The old nation is not being ignored; it is being reconstituted, from the inside, under its rightful King. Revelation 21:12-14 finishes the picture, with the twelve tribes on the gates of the city and the twelve apostles on its foundations, one people.

Then look at who the twelve are. MacArthur on chapter 10 observes that the lists in the Gospels and Acts always run in the same three groups of four, with Peter always first and "Judas Iscariot is always named last." Inside that fixed frame, Matthew makes two edits worth noticing. He writes "Matthew the tax collector" about himself, the only list that does, keeping in public the label he had every reason to drop, two chapters after Jesus called him out of that tax booth (9:9). And he puts that man in the same sentence as "Simon the Zealot," whom MacArthur identifies with "a group determined to overthrow Roman domination in Israel." A Zealot's political program had room for killing collaborators like Matthew. Fishermen fill the rest. The renewed Israel begins as a band of natural enemies who now have one Lord, which tells you something permanent about what the kingdom does to men before it ever asks them to do anything.

Does "go nowhere among the Gentiles" shut the door on the nations?

"These twelve Jesus sent out, instructing them, 'Go nowhere among the Gentiles and enter no town of the Samaritans, but go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.'" Matthew 10:5-6, ESV

This sentence startles readers, and it should, because Matthew's own Gospel opened with Gentile magi worshiping the child (2:1-12) and had Jesus marvel at a Roman centurion's faith two chapters ago (8:10). The restriction is not about worth, and it is not permanent. It is about sequence. God made his promises to Abraham and David, and the King goes first to the people who hold the promises, which is exactly what Paul later calls the gospel's order: "to the Jew first and also to the Greek" (Romans 1:16). MacArthur puts it the same way, that Christ was not forbidding contact with a Gentile met on the road, but that "they were to take the message first to the covenant people, in the regions nearby." Covenant people here means Israel, the nation God bound himself to by promise (see covenant in the glossary).

And Matthew makes sure the fence comes down before his book ends. The last paragraph of the Gospel reverses this instruction in the plainest terms: "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations" (28:19). Same verb, opposite scope. So read 10:5-6 as the first movement of one mission, not as a wall. Israel first, then the nations, and by Acts 1:8 the sequence is spelled out as a route: Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, the end of the earth. The Samaritan towns closed here are evangelized in Acts 8. Nothing was cancelled; the clock had not yet struck.

Why the strict packing list, and how does "give without pay" fit with "the laborer deserves his food"?

"Heal the sick, raise the dead, cleanse lepers, cast out demons. You received without paying; give without pay. Acquire no gold or silver or copper for your belts, no bag for your journey, or two tunics or sandals or a staff, for the laborer deserves his food." Matthew 10:8-10, ESV

Take the two halves in order. "You received without paying; give without pay" guards the message. These men had just been handed power to heal and to raise the dead, and MacArthur notes bluntly that "if they sold these gifts for money, they could have made quite a fortune," which "would have obscured the message of grace Christ sent them to preach." Grace that charges admission is no longer recognizable as grace. That principle has no expiration date, and it is the standing verdict on every ministry that meters the gospel.

The packing list guards the messengers. No money, no bag, no change of clothes, no spare sandals or staff, which means no cushion, no plan B, and no way to look like a traveling philosopher on tour. They will eat if God moves someone to feed them. That is the point of the reason attached: "the laborer deserves his food." So the two lines are not in tension. Selling ministry is forbidden; receiving support is expected, and it is called wages rather than charity. Paul quotes this same principle for church elders in 1 Timothy 5:18. Note also that the specific restrictions here were for this mission and this moment; on the night before the cross Jesus tells the same men, "let the one who has no sword sell his cloak and buy one" (Luke 22:36), and MacArthur is right that the strictness in chapter 10 was "unique for this mission," designed to teach these apostles to trust God's supply through God's people.

The last instructions govern arrival and departure. They are to find a worthy house and greet it, and the greeting is not small talk: "peace" is the Hebrew shalom, wholeness and blessing, and Jesus speaks of it as a thing that lands or comes back, "let your peace come upon it, but if it is not worthy, let your peace return to you" (v. 13). Then the exit. Shaking dust off the feet was a gesture Jews used when leaving Gentile territory, refusing to carry pagan soil into the holy land. Now Jesus aims it at Israelite towns that will not receive his messengers, which is a shocking reversal: a Jewish town that refuses the King is treated as pagan ground. Verse 15 completes the logic, that it will be "more bearable on the day of judgment for the land of Sodom and Gomorrah than for that town." Sodom had no preacher. These towns are hearing the King's own message from the King's own apostles, so their guilt is greater. Judgment tracks the light you were given, and few people in history have been given as much light as a person with an English Bible on the shelf.

Application

  • About God He is a King who delegates his own authority to ordinary men (v. 1) and who builds his renewed people out of a tax collector and a Zealot in the same band. He also keeps an order in history, going to Israel first and then the nations, which means his patience is a strategy and not an oversight.
  • About you Matthew never dropped the label "the tax collector," because in his hands it had stopped being shame and become evidence of grace. Whatever your version of that label is, the gospel does not require you to hide it. And notice you are not asked to invent a message: the apostles preached the same sentence John and Jesus preached, and so do you.
  • Do Two things today. First, name the one person in your church or family you would least naturally be allied with, and do one concrete thing for them this week: a message, a coffee, a favor. Simon the Zealot had to sit at a fire with Matthew. Second, do something free today that you could have charged for or traded for, and tell nobody, as a small drill in "you received without paying; give without pay."

Primary source
In your local MacArthur notes copy, read the notes on 10:1-2, 10:2, 10:4, 10:5, 10:8, and 10:14 together (4 min). Three payoffs: the note on 10:1-2 gives you the clean distinction between "disciple" (student) and "apostle" (qualified representative sent on a mission); the note on 10:2 shows the fixed structure of the apostle lists across all four New Testament versions; and the note on 10:14 supplies the cultural background for shaking off dust, "an expression of disdain," used when returning from Gentile regions and repeated by Paul in Acts 13:51. One place to read him critically: on 10:1 he treats the delegated power as "a preview of the power Christ will exhibit in his earthly kingdom," which is his premillennial framework doing the work. The verse itself only claims that the authority authenticated the King and his messengers; whether it previews a future thousand-year earthly reign is an argument that has to come from elsewhere. For fuller treatment, his Matthew sermons on chapter 10 are in the GTY Matthew archive; look for the series on the twelve apostles, especially the messages on Matthew and on Simon the Zealot.
Church prompt
This passage puts natural enemies on one team and makes a town's response to messengers a matter of judgment. Two things to raise at church. Ask an elder how your congregation supports the people who labor in the word, and whether the principle "the laborer deserves his food" is actually being honored. Then ask yourself, and if you can, one other member: is there a political or cultural line inside our congregation that functions like the line between a tax collector and a Zealot, and what would it look like to cross it on purpose this month?

Questions welcome, always: "if the twelve replace or renew the tribes, what happens to ethnic Israel after this?", "does 'raise the dead' in verse 8 apply to any minister today?", "how do I tell the difference between supporting a preacher and paying for the gospel?" Ask your teacher.

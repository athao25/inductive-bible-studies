Matthew 5:33-48: Love Your Enemies

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The formula runs three more times (vv. 33, 38, 43). "You have heard that it was said . . . But I say to you." Add the three from the previous lesson (vv. 21, 27, 31) and you have six. Today completes the set. Jesus is not correcting Moses; he is correcting what people had heard about Moses.
  • Four swearing formulas, all with a reason attached (vv. 34-36). Heaven, earth, Jerusalem, your own head. Each one gets a "for": God's throne, God's footstool, the city of the great King, and a head whose hairs you cannot even recolor. Notice that every escape route leads back to God.
  • The replacement is startlingly small (v. 37). "Let what you say be simply 'Yes' or 'No.'" Not a better oath, no oath. Anything extra "comes from evil."
  • "An eye for an eye" is quoted, not invented (v. 38). It is real Scripture (Ex. 21:24; Lev. 24:20; Deut. 19:21). The problem is not the text but the use people were making of it.
  • Four concrete situations, not a principle in the abstract (vv. 39-42). A slap on the right cheek, a lawsuit over a tunic, a forced mile, a request to borrow. Insult, property, liberty, money. Jesus works through the actual pressure points of daily life under occupation.
  • The right cheek is a strange detail (v. 39). For a right-handed man, striking your right cheek takes the back of his hand. That is the blow of contempt, not the blow of combat.
  • "Hate your enemy" has no Old Testament address (v. 43). Leviticus 19:18 says love your neighbor. The second half is quoted with no chapter and verse because there is none. Matthew lets the missing citation do the work.
  • The reason is the Father's own conduct (vv. 45-48). Sun and rain go to evil and good, just and unjust. Tax collectors and Gentiles love their own; the question is "what more are you doing than others?" (v. 47).

Interpretation

Does verse 34 forbid all oaths, including in a courtroom?

"But I say to you, Do not take an oath at all, either by heaven, for it is the throne of God, or by the earth, for it is his footstool, or by Jerusalem, for it is the city of the great King. And do not take an oath by your head, for you cannot make one hair white or black. Let what you say be simply 'Yes' or 'No'; anything more than this comes from evil." Matthew 5:34-37, ESV

Start with the abuse Jesus is naming. A graded oath system had grown up in which some formulas were treated as binding and others as loopholes. Swear by the temple and you could wriggle out; swear by the gold of the temple and you were stuck (Jesus dismantles that exact scheme later, in 23:16-22). The whole apparatus existed so that a man could sound solemn while keeping an exit. MacArthur on chapter 5 describes it plainly: people swore by heaven, earth, Jerusalem, or their own heads "not by God, hoping to avoid divine judgment for their lie."

Jesus closes every exit by pointing out that there is no such thing as a God-free object to swear by. Heaven is his throne, earth his footstool, Jerusalem his city, and your head is so far from being yours that you cannot change one hair's color. Since all of it is God's, every oath drags God in, and every evasive oath is simply a lie told loudly.

So is a courtroom oath sin? No, and the Bible itself is the reason. God swears by himself to Abraham (Gen. 22:16; Heb. 6:13-18). The Law commands oaths in certain cases (Num. 5:19-22; 30:2). Paul repeatedly calls God as his witness (Rom. 1:9; 2 Cor. 1:23). Jesus himself answers when the high priest puts him under oath (Matt. 26:63-64). Scripture does not contradict itself, so verse 34 cannot mean what would make God a sinner. What Jesus forbids is the everyday use of reinforcement to prop up speech that is otherwise unreliable. MacArthur puts the positive side well: "all our speech should be as if we were under an oath to tell the truth." The mark of a kingdom citizen is that nobody ever needs to ask him to swear, because his plain yes already carries the weight.

If "an eye for an eye" is Scripture, what exactly is Jesus overturning?

"You have heard that it was said, 'An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.' But I say to you, Do not resist the one who is evil. But if anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also." Matthew 5:38-39, ESV

Nothing in the Law, and everything in the misuse of it. The lex talionis, the law of like-for-like, was given to judges, and it was a ceiling rather than a floor: one eye, not a village burned; one tooth, not a family feud. In a world where an insult could escalate into generations of blood, that legislation was mercy. People had quietly moved it from the courthouse to the kitchen table, turning a limit on judicial penalty into a personal right of payback. MacArthur is careful on this point and worth quoting: the standard "was never meant to sanction acts of personal retaliation," and Jesus here "made no alteration to the true meaning of the law."

Read the four examples and you will see they are all personal, not civil. The backhanded slap is an insult to dignity, and turning the other cheek refuses to trade contempt for contempt. The tunic and cloak (v. 40) concern a lawsuit; the cloak was the outer garment a poor man slept in, and the Law protected it from seizure (Ex. 22:26-27), so Jesus is saying give up even what you could legally keep. The mile (v. 41) is the Roman soldier's legal right to conscript a civilian to carry his gear; the occupier's most galling privilege is met with a second mile freely offered. Simon of Cyrene will live that verse on the way to Golgotha (27:32).

What this does not do is abolish courts, police, or self-defense. God still hands the sword to civil authority (Rom. 13:1-4), and Paul appeals to Roman law when his rights are violated (Acts 16:37; 25:11). Jesus is not dissolving justice; he is removing your personal claim on it. You may not run your own private courtroom with yourself as plaintiff, judge, and executioner. Vengeance belongs to God (Rom. 12:19), and that is precisely what makes turning the cheek possible rather than merely heroic.

What does "be perfect" mean, and is it crushing me on purpose?

"You have heard that it was said, 'You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy.' But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, so that you may be sons of your Father who is in heaven. For he makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good, and sends rain on the just and on the unjust." Matthew 5:43-45, ESV

Notice first where "hate your enemy" came from: nowhere. It is not in Leviticus, not in Deuteronomy, not anywhere in the Old Testament, which in fact commands feeding your enemy (Prov. 25:21) and returning his stray ox (Ex. 23:4-5). It was a traditional gloss, a human footnote that had hardened into a quotation. That matters for the whole series of six: Jesus is repeatedly stripping tradition off the text, not stripping authority off the text.

Then look at the ground he gives. Not "because your enemy deserves it" and not "because it will work." The reason is family resemblance: love your enemies so "that you may be sons of your Father," because he sends sun and rain on the evil and the good alike. Theologians call this common grace, the genuine, non-saving goodness God shows to everyone without exception, and MacArthur names it in the same terms while rightly distinguishing it from God's everlasting love for the elect. The daily weather is an ethics lesson. Every sunrise falls on people who curse the God who sent it, and he sends it anyway.

"You therefore must be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect." Matthew 5:48, ESV

The word behind "perfect" (teleios) means complete, finished, whole, having reached its intended end. Given the context, the completeness in view is completeness in love: the Father's love has no edge where it stops, no category of person it excludes, and yours is not to have one either. Loving only those who love you is not a small love, it is an unfinished one, the kind tax collectors already manage (v. 46).

And "therefore" ties this verse back through the whole section to 5:20, "unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven." That is the demand this passage has been unfolding all along, and here is the honest ending: nobody keeps it. Not one reader of this lesson has an unbroken record of plain speech, refused retaliation, and prayer for people who hate him. MacArthur says the standard is unattainable and God will not lower it "without compromising his own perfection," and he is right, and the next sentence is the one that matters most: "The marvelous truth of the gospel is that Christ has met this standard on our behalf." The Sermon on the Mount is not a ladder handed to climbers. It drives you to Christ first, and only then, from inside his righteousness, does it drive you to effort. Read verse 48 any other way and it will either crush you or make you a liar.

Application

  • About God He is a Father whose goodness runs past the boundary of who deserves it. He gave sun and rain to his enemies for centuries, then gave his Son. Common grace is the small daily version of the cross.
  • About you Two diagnostics. First: does anyone in your life need extra assurance to believe you, and if so, why? Second: name the person you would exclude if enemy love had an exception clause. That name is where your love is still unfinished.
  • Do Today, pray by name, out loud, for one person who has genuinely wronged you, asking God to bless them and not merely to fix them. Then find one sentence you have been softening or over-selling this week and say it plainly, yes or no, with no reinforcement attached.

Church prompt
Enemy love gets tested first inside the church, where offenses are close-range. This week ask a pastor or elder one question: when someone in the congregation has been wronged by another member, what does our church actually counsel them to do? Compare the answer to verses 39-42 and 44. If there is an old grievance you are carrying against someone in your own congregation, this is the week to go to them yourself.

Matthew 5:13-16: Salt and Light

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Both sentences are indicative, not imperative (vv. 13, 14). "You are the salt of the earth." "You are the light of the world." Jesus does not say become, try to be, or you ought to be. He describes what his disciples already are before he asks anything of them.
  • "You" is emphatic and plural (vv. 13-14). In the Greek the pronoun is added when it did not have to be, which throws weight on it: you, and nobody else. And it is plural throughout. This is a congregation being described, not a lone hero.
  • The audience carries over from 5:1-12. No new speech marker, no new crowd. The people who are salt and light are the same people just called poor in spirit, mourning, meek, hungry for righteousness, merciful, pure, peacemaking, and persecuted. The character of vv. 3-12 is the flavor and the brightness of vv. 13-16.
  • Two different spheres (vv. 13, 14). Salt is "of the earth," light is "of the world." Not the synagogue, not Israel only. The scope is as wide as it can be.
  • The only command in the paragraph is at the end (v. 16). "Let your light shine before others." Three verses of identity, then one imperative. Notice the order and do not reverse it.
  • Useless salt has one destiny (v. 13). "No longer good for anything except to be thrown out and trampled under people's feet." Not repurposed, not diluted, not restored. The question "how shall its saltiness be restored?" is rhetorical and the answer is: it cannot be.
  • Two images of impossibility (vv. 14-15). "A city set on a hill cannot be hidden." "Nor do people light a lamp and put it under a basket." Hiding is not merely wrong here, it is absurd. Nobody does that with lamps, and nobody manages it with cities.
  • The works are seen, the glory goes elsewhere (v. 16). "That they may see your good works and give glory to your Father who is in heaven." The visibility is the point, and the destination of the credit is the point, and they are different addresses. This is also the first time in Matthew that Jesus calls God "your Father." He will do it fifteen more times before chapter 7 ends.

Interpretation

How can salt stop being salty, and what is Jesus actually warning about?

"You are the salt of the earth, but if salt has lost its taste, how shall its saltiness be restored? It is no longer good for anything except to be thrown out and trampled under people's feet." Matthew 5:13, ESV

Chemically, sodium chloride cannot become unsalty. MacArthur on chapter 5 gives the historical answer: "Pure salt cannot lose its flavor or effectiveness, but the salt that is common in the Dead Sea area is contaminated with gypsum and other minerals and may have a flat taste or be ineffective as a preservative. Such mineral salts were useful for little more than keeping footpaths free of vegetation." That last detail is worth pausing on, because it explains the odd ending of the verse: contaminated salt was literally scattered on paths and walked on. Jesus is not inventing a picture, he is describing what people did with a bad batch.

So the danger is not chemistry, it is contamination. Salt did three jobs in the ancient world. It preserved meat in a world with no refrigeration, holding back rot. It seasoned, making bland food worth eating. And it was a covenant sign: "You shall not let the salt of the covenant with your God be missing from your grain offering" (Leviticus 2:13), and Numbers 18:19 calls God's grant to Aaron "a covenant of salt forever." Salt meant permanence and loyalty. Put the three together and the disciples' job is clear: they slow the decay of the society they live in, they make the truth taste like something, and they belong to God by a bond that does not spoil.

What is being warned against, then, is a church so blended into the world that nothing distinguishes it. Not a church that failed at influence, but one that lost its difference. Notice what Jesus does not say: he does not say the world will hate you and win. He says the mixture will make you good for nothing. The threat to the church has always been dilution, not opposition.

If 5:16 says let them see, why does 6:1 forbid being seen?

"In the same way, let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father who is in heaven." Matthew 5:16, ESV
"Beware of practicing your righteousness before other people in order to be seen by them, for then you will have no reward from your Father who is in heaven." Matthew 6:1, ESV

Same speaker, same sermon, roughly twenty verses apart. This is one of the sharpest apparent contradictions in the Gospels, and it dissolves the moment you look at the purpose clauses rather than the actions. Both verses assume the works are visible; neither one tells you to hide. The difference is the ending. In 5:16 the sentence terminates in "glory to your Father." In 6:1 it terminates in "to be seen by them." Same deed, same audience, opposite destination for the credit.

Which means the test is not whether anyone notices, it is what you want when they do. If a watching neighbor's response is "what a good man," the salt has already gone flat. If the response is "what kind of God does he have," the lamp is on the stand. MacArthur's note on 5:16 puts it economically: "A godly life gives convincing testimony of the saving power of God. That brings him glory." He points to 1 Peter 2:12, where Peter says almost exactly this, that the Gentiles who slander you may "see your good deeds and glorify God on the day of visitation."

There is real pastoral relief here. Christians often tie themselves in knots trying to do good invisibly, as though secrecy were the virtue. It is not. Secrecy is impossible for a city on a hill, and it is silly for a lamp. The command is to shine. The discipline is over the motive, and that is a matter you settle in prayer before the deed, not by hiding the deed afterward.

Why does identity come before the command?

This is the structural point of the whole passage, and it is worth naming as a rule you will use for the rest of the Sermon. Jesus first describes (vv. 3-12, and now "you are"), then he commands (v. 16). He does not say "shine, and then you will be light." He says you are light, so shine. In the Reformed reading of Scripture this is the standard shape of biblical ethics: God declares what he has made his people to be, and the commands follow as the outworking of that gift. Paul does the same thing constantly, most bluntly in Ephesians 5:8, "at one time you were darkness, but now you are light in the Lord. Walk as children of light."

Reverse the order and you have moralism: earn the status by the performance. Keep the order and you have the gospel: receive the status, then live like it is true. Notice too that the light is derivative. Jesus will later say "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12), and Matthew has already applied Isaiah's dawning light to Christ himself (4:16). Disciples are light the way the moon is bright. Which is exactly why the glory in v. 16 has to travel past them to the Father.

One more thread worth holding: this passage assumes the church is meant to be publicly visible and to have an actual effect on the earth, not just to survive until the rescue. That instinct runs with the postmillennial expectation this course reads from, that the kingdom leavens the world (13:33), and it does not depend on it. Premillennial, amillennial, and postmillennial readers all have to obey v. 16 in the same house on the same street this week.

Application

  • About God He gives before he demands. The Father who is named twice in these four verses is the one who made you salt and light in the first place, and he is the one the credit belongs to. Grace precedes duty in the very grammar of the sentence.
  • About you The question is never "am I influential?" but "am I still distinct?" Look for the places where your speech, your money, your weekends, and your anger are indistinguishable from your neighbor's. That is where the gypsum got in.
  • Do Today, do one good work you would normally do privately, and do it where it will be seen: pay for the person behind you, help a coworker publicly, take a meal to a neighbor in daylight. Before you do it, pray one sentence: "Father, let them see this and think of you, not me." Then, when someone thanks you, name Christ in a single unforced sentence.

Church prompt
Salt and light are plural. Ask this week at church: where is our congregation visibly present in this town, and where have we quietly gone flat? A useful, non-accusing way in is to ask an elder what our church is known for by people who do not attend. Then ask yourself the harder half: if that reputation is thin, which of my hours would have to move for it to change?

Questions welcome, always: "can a real Christian actually lose his saltiness?", "does v. 16 mean Christians should push for public influence?", "how do I tell the difference between shining and showing off?" Ask your teacher.

Matthew 1:18-25: Immanuel

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 1:18-25 (ESV, read first!) · Memory verse 1 is active and sits inside today's text

Did you read it?
Stop here if you have not. Read Matthew 1:18-25 in your ESV twice, slowly, and write at least five observations before you go on. Watch for: who does the acting in each sentence, how many times the Holy Spirit is named, what Joseph is called, what two names are given to the child, and where the narrator steps in to speak to you directly.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The Spirit is named twice, before any explanation (vv. 18, 20). Matthew says "from the Holy Spirit" as a flat statement of fact in v. 18, then has the angel repeat it in v. 20. The narrator tells you the answer before Joseph gets it.
  • Joseph is called her "husband" while they are only betrothed (vv. 18-19). The same paragraph says they had not "come together" and that he planned to "divorce her". Betrothal is already a legal marriage bond here.
  • Joseph is described, not praised, in one compact clause (v. 19). "Being a just man and unwilling to put her to shame": one word about his standing before God, one about his intention toward her, and they are joined, not opposed.
  • Joseph speaks no word in the entire passage (vv. 18-25). He considers, he is addressed, he wakes, he obeys, he names. Every line of dialogue belongs to the angel or to the prophet.
  • The angel addresses him as "Joseph, son of David" (v. 20). That title is the hinge to the genealogy you read last time; the legal line runs through this man's obedience.
  • Two names are given, and they do different jobs (vv. 21, 23). "Jesus" is a command about what he will do ("he will save his people from their sins"); "Immanuel" is a statement about who he is ("God with us"). Note also who calls him what: Joseph calls him Jesus (v. 25), "they" call him Immanuel.
  • Verses 22-23 change speaker (v. 22). Matthew steps out of the story to comment: "All this took place to fulfill what the Lord had spoken by the prophet." Notice the Lord is the speaker and the prophet is the mouth.
  • The obedience is reported in blunt sequence (vv. 24-25). Woke, did, took, knew her not, called. No feelings are recorded. Matthew measures Joseph by what he did the next morning.

Interpretation

Why does a betrothed man need a divorce?

In first-century Jewish practice, betrothal was not an engagement in the modern sense. It was a binding covenant, normally lasting about a year, in which the couple were already legally husband and wife though they did not yet live together or have relations. Only death or a formal divorce could end it. MacArthur on chapter 1 puts it plainly at v. 18: "Jewish betrothal was as binding as modern marriage. A divorce was necessary to terminate the betrothal (v. 19) and the betrothed couple were regarded legally as husband and wife (v. 19)". That is why Matthew can call Joseph her "husband" in v. 19 and still say in v. 18 that they had not come together. If you miss this, the passage reads like a broken engagement; read rightly, it is a man facing what he believes is adultery inside a marriage.

What kind of "just man" plans a quiet divorce?

And her husband Joseph, being a just man and unwilling to put her to shame, resolved to divorce her quietly. Matthew 1:19, ESV

The word translated "just" (or "righteous") describes a man who trusts God and takes God's law seriously. The law he took seriously was severe: Deuteronomy 22:23-24 prescribes stoning for a betrothed woman found unfaithful, and a public accusation would at minimum have destroyed her. Yet Matthew joins "just" to "unwilling to put her to shame" with a simple "and", not a "but". Justice and mercy are not two men fighting inside Joseph; they are one disposition. He will not pretend nothing happened (he plans to divorce), and he will not use the law as a weapon (he plans to do it quietly). MacArthur's note is exactly right here: "Joseph's righteousness meant he was also merciful."

Then God interrupts. The angel comes in a dream, the first of five dreams in Matthew (1:20; 2:12, 13, 19, 22), all of them clustered around the King's arrival and all of them producing immediate obedience. Notice what the revelation does not do: it does not excuse Joseph's plan as wrong-headed, it gives him information he could not have had. "Do not fear to take Mary as your wife, for that which is conceived in her is from the Holy Spirit." The conception is not a scandal to be managed but a work of God to be received.

Does Isaiah 7:14 really say "virgin"?

Verse 22 is the first appearance of Matthew's fulfillment formula, a pattern you will meet more than a dozen times in this Gospel: "All this took place to fulfill what the Lord had spoken by the prophet." Learn to spot it; it is one of Matthew's main arguments that Jesus is the promised Messiah.

"Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and they shall call his name Immanuel" (which means, God with us). Matthew 1:23, ESV

Here is the honest difficulty, and you should know it before someone hands it to you as a gotcha. Isaiah 7:14 in Hebrew uses almah, a word meaning a young woman of marriageable age. Hebrew has a narrower word, bethulah, that more strictly means virgin. Skeptics argue Isaiah only promised a young woman's ordinary child and that Christians upgraded it. Two things answer that. First, almah is never used in the Old Testament of a married or unchaste woman, and a merely ordinary birth is a strange candidate for the "sign" God offers in Isaiah 7:11-14. Second, and decisively for Matthew: he is quoting the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Old Testament made by Jewish scholars roughly two centuries before Christ, and it renders almah with parthenos, the unambiguous Greek word for virgin. Jews reading their own Scriptures, long before there was any Christian claim to defend, understood it that way. As MacArthur puts it, "Matthew, writing under the Spirit's inspiration, ends all doubt about the meaning of the word in Isa. 7:14." And Matthew has already removed the ambiguity from his own side of the ledger: v. 18 and v. 20 say "from the Holy Spirit", and v. 25 says Joseph "knew her not until she had given birth to a son."

Do not miss the second name. Isaiah's sign child was called Immanuel, "God with us", in the days when Assyria threatened to swallow Judah. Matthew says the full weight of that name lands on this child. And he does not drop it: the last words of the book are "behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age" (28:20). Immanuel opens Matthew and Immanuel closes it. When you reach chapter 28, you will feel the bracket snap shut.

Application

  • About God He does not save from a distance. The Son does not send help, he comes; the name is not "God for us" or "God above us" but "God with us", conceived by the Spirit in a real womb in a real town. And he comes to deal with the deepest problem you have: not Rome, not circumstances, but "their sins" (v. 21).
  • About you If Jesus saves his people from their sins, then your sins are the thing he came for, not the thing that disqualifies you. Also test yourself by Joseph: the righteous man in this story is the one whose obedience cost him his reputation and who refused to shame someone he had every legal right to expose.
  • Do Today, take the one person you currently have a legitimate grievance against, and choose the quietest righteous option available to you. Do not publicize, do not vent to a third party, do not build the case in your head. Then pray Matthew 1:21 back to God naming one specific sin of your own that you want him to save you from.

Church prompt
The virgin conception is one of the few doctrines the historic creeds spell out by name ("conceived by the Holy Spirit, born of the virgin Mary"). Ask someone at church this week why the church bothered to put it in the creed at all: what would actually change if Jesus had an ordinary human father? Bring back the answer and test it against verses 20-21.

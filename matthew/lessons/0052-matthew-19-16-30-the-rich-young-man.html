Matthew 19:16-30: The Rich Young Man

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 19:16-30 (ESV, read first!)

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The question is built out of doing (v. 16). "Teacher, what good deed must I do to have eternal life?" Every load-bearing word is about performance: a deed, a good one, done by him, in order to have. He is not asking how to be forgiven. He is asking what item is still missing from a transaction he assumes is already mostly complete.
  • Jesus does not answer the question; he questions the questioner (v. 17). "Why do you ask me about what is good? There is only one who is good." Before a single word about commandments, Jesus puts the category "good" where it belongs, with God alone. Two things are being pressed at once: the man's confidence that he can produce good, and his casual use of "Teacher" for someone he has not identified.
  • Jesus then answers on the man's own terms (v. 17). "If you would enter life, keep the commandments." That is law, not gospel, and it is deliberate. A man who wants to be paid is told the wage rate. Watch whether he flinches.
  • "Which ones?" (v. 18). The follow-up is itself an answer. He treats the commandments as a menu, a list from which the relevant items can be selected and checked off.
  • Jesus lists only the second table, and omits coveting (vv. 18-19). Murder, adultery, theft, false witness, honor your father and mother, plus Leviticus 19:18, "You shall love your neighbor as yourself." All of these govern how a man treats his neighbor. Nothing about God, no other gods, no idols, no name, no Sabbath. And the one command that would have caught him instantly, "you shall not covet", is left out. The omission is the loudest thing in the list.
  • He claims a clean record (v. 20). "All these I have kept. What do I still lack?" Notice the second sentence undercuts the first. He is sure he has kept them and equally sure something is missing, which means his own conscience is not as satisfied as his answer.
  • The instruction is specific, personal, and total (v. 21). "Go, sell what you possess and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me." Four imperatives: go, sell, give, come. The last one is the point of the other three.
  • The narrator tells you the cause of the sorrow (v. 22). "When the young man heard this he went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions." Matthew rarely explains motives; here he does. The word "for" is doing all the work. Read the sentence carefully and the ownership runs backward: it is not that he had great possessions, it is that his great possessions had him. He is the only person in this Gospel who is invited to follow Jesus and refuses.
  • Jesus generalizes immediately, and twice (vv. 23-24). "Only with difficulty will a rich person enter the kingdom of heaven", then, sharper, "it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God." The second saying is not a softening of the first.
  • The disciples are "greatly astonished" (v. 25). Not saddened for the man, astonished at the doctrine. Their question, "Who then can be saved?", only makes sense if they assumed the wealthy were the best candidates. Their scale of likelihood has just been inverted.
  • Jesus' answer moves the whole subject off the human side (v. 26). "With man this is impossible, but with God all things are possible." He does not reduce the difficulty. He grants the impossibility and names who overcomes it.
  • Peter does the arithmetic out loud (v. 27). "See, we have left everything and followed you. What then will we have?" He has noticed that the Twelve passed the test the young man failed, and he wants the balance sheet. Jesus does not rebuke the question; he answers it generously (vv. 28-29).
  • The unit ends on a reversal (v. 30). "But many who are first will be last, and the last first." That sentence points forward, not backward. Hold it; the next lesson's parable exists to explain it.

Interpretation

Why does Jesus answer a works question with more law?

And behold, a man came up to him, saying, "Teacher, what good deed must I do to have eternal life?" And he said to him, "Why do you ask me about what is good? There is only one who is good. If you would enter life, keep the commandments." Matthew 19:16-17, ESV

Because the question is diseased, and the fastest way to show a man that is to take his question seriously. If Jesus had opened with grace, the man would have filed it as one more good deed to perform. So Jesus meets him on his own ground: you want a wage, here is the standard. This is the classic use of the law that the Reformers called its schoolmaster function, driving a self-confident person to the end of his own resources (Galatians 3:24, Romans 3:20). MacArthur states the strategy exactly in his note on 19:17: "This, of course, is law, not gospel... Before showing him the way to life, Jesus wanted to impress on the young man both the high standard required by God and the absolute futility of seeking salvation by his own merit."

Do not skip the counter-question. "Why do you ask me about what is good? There is only one who is good." Jesus is not denying that he is good, and he is not denying his own deity; those readings both miss the pressure of the sentence. He is handing the man a fork. If only God is good, and you have just called me good teacher and asked me for the standard of goodness, then either take back the word or work out who you are talking to. The man never answers, and the conversation goes downhill from there.

Then the list. Five of the second-table commandments plus Leviticus 19:18, all of them about the neighbor, none about God, and coveting quietly left off. Jesus is not editing the law; he is setting a trap that the man walks into with both feet. "All these I have kept" is a statement no honest reader of the Ten Commandments can make, and Jesus does not argue the point out loud. He is about to prove it with one sentence instead.

Is "sell what you possess" a command for every disciple?

Jesus said to him, "If you would be perfect, go, sell what you possess and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me." Matthew 19:21, ESV

No, and the text itself rules it out. Jesus never required this of Peter, who still owned a house in Capernaum and a boat; not of Matthew, who threw a large dinner at his own expense; not of Zacchaeus, who gave half and was told salvation had come to his house; not of Joseph of Arimathea, a rich man and a disciple; not of Mary, Martha, and Lazarus, who kept a home he stayed in. A universal command would have produced universal obedience among the people who actually followed him. It did not, because it was not one.

What it was is a diagnostic, aimed with surgical precision. The man had just claimed to keep the whole second table, including love your neighbor as yourself. Very well: your neighbor is poor and you are rich, so love him as yourself and give him what you have. He would not. That single refusal exposed the second table he had boasted about, and behind it the first table Jesus never mentioned, because a man who will not part with his money for God has already told you which god he serves. The command located the idol. MacArthur's note on 19:21 puts it plainly: "Jesus was not teaching salvation by philanthropy; but he was demanding that this young man give him first place."

Two guardrails so this does not get misused in either direction. First, the passage is not a warrant for anyone to prescribe a specific sacrifice to somebody else; Jesus could read this man's heart and you cannot read your neighbor's. Second, and more urgent, the fact that the command was particular does not mean it was optional. The claim of Christ on everything you own is universal even though the liquidation order was not. Memory verse 7 already said it: deny yourself, take up your cross, follow me. What is variable is which possession God puts his finger on. That there is one, and that he has every right to it, is not variable at all.

And notice how Matthew closes the scene. Not "he refused", not "he argued". "He went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions." He knew what he was giving up, felt the loss, and walked anyway. Grief is not repentance. This is the saddest verse in the chapter.

The camel and the needle: was there a gate called the Needle's Eye?

"Again I tell you, it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God." When the disciples heard this, they were greatly astonished, saying, "Who then can be saved?" Matthew 19:24-25, ESV

You will hear that the "eye of the needle" was a small pedestrian gate in the Jerusalem wall, through which a camel could squeeze if you unloaded it and made it kneel, so the saying means salvation is difficult but manageable with effort. Say it plainly: there is no historical evidence for such a gate. No ancient source mentions one; the explanation first appears in medieval commentary and has been repeated ever since because it is comforting. Jesus is using deliberate hyperbole, pairing the largest animal his hearers regularly saw with the smallest opening they regularly used, and the pairing means one thing: impossible.

The disciples' reaction proves it. Nobody is "greatly astonished" by "this will take real effort". They cry "Who then can be saved?" because they heard the word impossible, and they heard it applied to the people they thought had the best odds. That assumption is the hinge. In their world wealth was widely read as a mark of God's blessing, and the rich could fund more almsgiving besides, so if a rich man cannot get in, nobody can. MacArthur notes the same background on 19:24: "Since wealth was deemed proof of God's approval... it was commonly thought that rich people were the most likely candidates for heaven. Jesus destroyed that notion."

And now the theological center of the whole passage:

But Jesus looked at them and said, "With man this is impossible, but with God all things are possible." Matthew 19:26, ESV

He agrees with them. That is what makes the sentence so strong. Salvation is not hard for man, it is impossible for man, and the only thing that changes the verdict is God doing what man cannot. This is why the passage that begins with "what good deed must I do" ends with a divine work. The rich young man's question had one flaw running through every word of it, the word "I", and verse 26 removes it. This is the doctrine your Reformed confessions are protecting when they insist salvation is by grace alone: not because human effort is undervalued, but because a corpse cannot cooperate in its own resurrection.

What is the "new world", and what does the hundredfold mean?

Jesus said to them, "Truly, I say to you, in the new world, when the Son of Man will sit on his glorious throne, you who have followed me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel." Matthew 19:28, ESV

The Greek behind "new world" is palingenesia, literally "regeneration" or "rebirth", used here not of a person being born again but of the whole created order being made new. Where you locate it depends on your eschatology, so here is the fork honestly. MacArthur, reading as a premillennialist, takes it as the future thousand-year earthly kingdom of Revelation 20, a distinct era after Christ returns, in which the apostles govern a restored ethnic Israel. That is a coherent reading and you should know it, but notice it is supplied from Revelation 20 rather than from anything in Matthew 19.

The postmillennial reading, which is this course's home lens, and the amillennial reading agree with each other here: palingenesia is the renewal of all things at the consummation, the new heavens and new earth of Romans 8:21 and Revelation 21, while the apostles' authority to "judge", that is, to govern, is already exercised now through their word in the church (compare 16:19, 18:18, and Paul's "the saints will judge the world" in 1 Corinthians 6:2). The "twelve tribes" are then the whole people of God, Jew and Gentile together, which is exactly how Matthew has been redefining Israel since chapter 8. On this reading the promise is not postponed to a separate age; it is inaugurated with Christ's enthronement (28:18) and completed when all things are made new.

Both readings hold the same pastoral point, so do not let the disagreement steal it. The hundredfold in verse 29 is not a formula for getting rich; it is the plain observation that whoever gives up a house or a family for Christ receives houses and families beyond counting in the people of God, plus eternal life on top. Peter asked what they would get, and the answer was: far more than you gave, and none of it earned. Then verse 30 lands: "many who are first will be last, and the last first." Peter had just done a little ranking. Jesus tells him the rankings do not survive contact with grace, and next lesson he tells a parable to prove it.

Application

  • About God. He is the only good one, and he saves people who cannot save themselves. Verse 26 is not a consolation prize after a failed attempt; it is the actual mechanism of every salvation there has ever been. Notice also that Jesus loved this man enough to tell him the one thing he did not want to hear, and let him walk.
  • About you. You almost certainly have an answer to "what good deed must I do", and you probably keep it in the same place this man did, just behind a claim to have kept the commandments. The test is not whether you would sell everything in theory. It is what your stomach does when you imagine actually losing the one thing you would not want named.
  • Do (today). Name it in writing: finish the sentence "I could follow Christ anywhere as long as I still had ______." Then do one concrete thing against it today, and do it in a way that costs you something you will notice. Give money you had earmarked for yourself, cancel a purchase and send the amount to someone in need, or tell one person what you wrote. An idol you can name and never touch is still an idol.

Church prompt
Ask your pastor or a mature believer: "How do you tell the difference between money you own and money that owns you?" Jesus never told most of his followers to liquidate, yet he told this one man to, and the difference was in the man, not the amount. Listen for whether the answer gives you a test you could actually apply to yourself this week.

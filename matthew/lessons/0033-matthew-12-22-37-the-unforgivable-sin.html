Lesson 0033 · Unit 4: Chapters 11-13

Matthew 12:22-37: The Unforgivable Sin

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 12:22-37 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If not, stop here. Read Matthew 12:22-37 twice in your ESV and write at least five observations. Watch the sequence: one healing, two reactions to it, and a long reply. Note the exact wording of the crowd's question in v. 23 and of the Pharisees' verdict in v. 24, and how they are answers to the same event. Track every "if" in vv. 26-29. Then notice that the passage ends on words, and ask why. Come back and compare.

Where this sits

Chapter 12 has been one long collision. The Sabbath grain field, the withered hand, and the Pharisees' council to destroy him (12:14) all come first, and Matthew answered them by quoting Isaiah's Servant, who will not break a bruised reed. Now the same opponents are handed the clearest miracle yet, and they render their verdict. This is the passage where the opposition stops being a debate about interpretation and becomes something else. Read it as the hinge of the chapter.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The man is blind, mute, and demon-oppressed, and all three are undone at once (v. 22). "He healed him, so that the man spoke and saw." There is no ambiguity to exploit here. A mute man now speaking in front of the crowd is a miracle you can verify by standing there.
  • Two verdicts on one event, back to back (vv. 23-24). The crowds ask "Can this be the Son of David?" The Pharisees say "It is only by Beelzebul." Matthew sets them side by side deliberately. Nobody in the passage disputes that something happened; they dispute its source.
  • The Pharisees do not deny the power, they reassign it (v. 24). Their charge concedes the exorcism. That concession is the whole basis of what Jesus says in vv. 31-32, so mark it.
  • Jesus answers thoughts, not just speech (v. 25). "Knowing their thoughts, he said to them." Matthew has already shown him doing this in 9:4. The man being accused of demonic power is quietly reading minds in the same breath.
  • The reply is a chain of conditionals (vv. 26-29). If Satan casts out Satan; if I cast out by Beelzebul; if it is by the Spirit of God. Jesus is not shouting them down, he is arguing, and each "if" closes off an exit.
  • He turns their own practice against them (v. 27). "By whom do your sons cast them out?" Jewish exorcists existed and were not accused of Satanism. The charge is therefore not a principle, it is a targeted slander, and their own people will judge it.
  • Verse 28 is a kingdom claim, and the wording is present tense. "Then the kingdom of God has come upon you." Not is near, not will come; has come upon. This is one of the most important sentences in Matthew for understanding the kingdom.
  • The strong man must be bound before the house is plundered (v. 29). Jesus casts his own ministry as a burglary of Satan's estate. The exorcisms are not skirmishes, they are the removal of goods from a house whose owner has already been tied up.
  • The passage ends on speech, and ties it to the heart (vv. 33-37). Tree and fruit, treasure and mouth, "out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks," and then judgment on "every careless word." The ending is not a change of topic. It is the diagnosis of what just happened in v. 24.

Interpretation

What is Jesus claiming in vv. 28-29?

"But if it is by the Spirit of God that I cast out demons, then the kingdom of God has come upon you. Or how can someone enter a strong man's house and plunder his goods, unless he first binds the strong man? Then indeed he may plunder his house." Matthew 12:28-29, ESV

Two things at once. First, that the arrival of the kingdom is not merely announced but visible; the evidence that God's reign has broken in is that Satan is losing property. Second, that this is possible only because the owner has been overpowered. Jesus does not describe himself as negotiating with the strong man or evading him. He describes a home invasion.

MacArthur on chapter 12 is direct about v. 28: "That was precisely true. The King was in their midst, displaying his sovereign power. He showed it by demonstrating his ability to bind Satan and his demons (v. 29)." That is well said, and it is more than his premillennial system usually lets him keep, since on that reading the kingdom in its proper sense is still future and this binding is a preview of it. The postmillennial home reading of this course takes v. 28 at face value and lets it govern: the kingdom came with the King, the strong man was bound in his ministry and decisively at the cross, and the plundering of his house has been under way ever since and will keep advancing through history. The amillennial reading agrees the binding is present and real, but expects the plundering to run alongside opposition until the end rather than to fill the earth. Notice that all three must reckon with the tense of v. 28. Whatever else is future, something arrived that day in front of witnesses.

What is the blasphemy against the Spirit, and have I committed it?

"Therefore I tell you, every sin and blasphemy will be forgiven people, but the blasphemy against the Spirit will not be forgiven. And whoever speaks a word against the Son of Man will be forgiven, but whoever speaks against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven, either in this age or in the age to come." Matthew 12:31-32, ESV

Start with the context, because the context is the definition. Jesus says this to men who watched a blind, mute, demon-oppressed man healed in front of them, could not deny it, and said the power was Satan's. They had the fullest possible evidence and rendered the exact opposite verdict. MacArthur's note on 12:31 puts it precisely: "The sin he was confronting was the Pharisees' deliberate rejection of that which they knew to be of God... they attributed to Satan a work that they knew was of God." That is the sin. It is not a category of bad language. It is a settled, informed, willful verdict that calls light darkness while the light is shining in the room.

So notice carefully what this sin is not. It is not a moment of doubt; doubt asks questions, and this is a refusal to accept the answer. It is not a curse that escaped your mouth in rage or grief or mental illness; Jesus explicitly says "whoever speaks a word against the Son of Man will be forgiven," which covers a great deal of hot speech. It is not a season of backsliding; Peter denied Christ with oaths and was restored in three chapters of John. It is not a blasphemous thought that intrudes and horrifies you. Every one of those is forgivable, and Scripture shows God forgiving each of them by name.

Now the pastoral point, and it is not a soft evasion, it is a straight inference from the text. If you are afraid you have committed this sin, that fear is very strong evidence that you have not. The Pharisees felt no fear. They were not troubled, they were confident; they went out and plotted (12:14). The heart that dreads having grieved the Spirit, that wants to be forgiven, that is reading this paragraph anxiously hoping the door is still open, is a heart being worked on by the very Spirit it is afraid of having blasphemed. Hardness does not agonize over its own hardness. As MacArthur adds on 12:32, even Saul of Tarsus, a Pharisee who persecuted the church, was forgiven, "because his unbelief stemmed from ignorance (1 Tim. 1:13)."

Why is any sin forgivable at all? Because the blood of Christ is of infinite worth, and "the blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin" (1 John 1:7). There is no sin so large it exceeds the price paid. Then why is this one unforgivable? Not because it outgrew the price, but because of what it is: a final and knowing rejection of the only one who applies that price. It is the Spirit who testifies of Christ and makes his truth known (John 15:26). To look at his manifest work, understand it, and name it satanic is to slam the one door through which forgiveness comes, from the inside. The sin is unpardonable because it is the settled refusal of pardon, not because pardon ran short.

Why does Jesus finish by talking about words?

"You brood of vipers! How can you speak good, when you are evil? For out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks... I tell you, on the day of judgment people will give account for every careless word they speak, for by your words you will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned." Matthew 12:34, 36-37, ESV

Because the accusation in v. 24 was not an incidental remark, it was a readout. Jesus has just been called demonic, and instead of defending his reputation further he explains where such a sentence comes from. A tree does not decide, case by case, what fruit to make. Speech is involuntary in the deep sense: it overflows. What comes out under pressure is a sample of what is stored inside. So "make the tree good and its fruit good" (v. 33) is not advice to try harder at speaking well; it is a demand for a new nature, which is exactly what these men will not have.

That is why "every careless word" is so heavy. MacArthur is blunt on 12:36: "There is no truer indication of a bad tree than the bad fruit of speech." The careless word is the honest one, the sentence that got past the editor. Verse 37 is not teaching salvation by vocabulary; it is teaching that words are admissible evidence, because they are the heart's own testimony about itself. The Pharisees convicted themselves in one sentence, and Jesus simply read it back to them.

Application

  • About God He is not stingy with pardon. The startling half of vv. 31-32 is not the exception, it is the rule: "every sin and blasphemy will be forgiven people." And he is stronger than the strong man; your deliverance was never a negotiation.
  • About you If this passage frightens you, take that fear as a mercy rather than a verdict. Then let the second half of the passage do its work: pay attention to what leaks out of you when you are tired, cornered, or annoyed, because that is the sample the text says to read.
  • Do Today, write down one sentence you said this week that you would not have said if you had thought first. Do not just resolve to be more careful. Ask what it says about what is stored inside, confess that to God specifically, and if the sentence landed on a person, go apologize to them by name before you sleep.

Primary source
In your local MacArthur notes copy, read the notes on 12:24, 12:28, 12:31, 12:32, and 12:36 (5 min). The 12:31 and 12:32 pair is the one to sit with: it defines the sin by the knowledge of the sinner, and the Saul of Tarsus example in 12:32 is the strongest pastoral relief in the note set. His 12:32 cross-references to Hebrews 6:4-6 and 10:29 are worth turning to in your study Bible, since those are the other two passages that scare tender consciences and they belong in the same conversation. For preaching, search the GTY Matthew archive for his messages on the unpardonable sin; he handles the assurance question at length there.
Church prompt
This is a passage to talk about out loud rather than worry about alone. Ask your pastor or an elder this week how he counsels someone who fears they have committed the unforgivable sin. Listen for whether the answer is grounded in the text of Matthew 12 or only in reassurance. And if you know someone in your congregation carrying that specific fear, tell them the second half of vv. 31-32 before you tell them anything else.

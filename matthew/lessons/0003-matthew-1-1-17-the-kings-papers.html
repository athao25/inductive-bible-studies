Lesson 0003 · Unit 1: Chapters 1-4

Matthew 1:1-17: The King's Papers

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 1:1-17 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Matthew 1:1-17 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Yes, the list of names. Read it slowly, out loud if you can, and write down at least five observations: who is named, who is skipped, which women appear and how they are described, what verse 17 counts, and what the first and last verses have in common. Come back with your list in front of you.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • A title verse, not a first entry (v. 1). "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham." Before a single father-son link, Matthew states two claims and puts David before Abraham, out of historical order. He is leading with the throne.
  • One relentless verb (vv. 2-16). "Was the father of", roughly forty times. The monotony is the point: an unbroken chain of legal descent, the kind of document you would produce in court.
  • Five women interrupt the chain (vv. 3, 5, 5, 6, 16). Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, "the wife of Uriah", and Mary. Ancient Jewish genealogies traced fathers; women are not required here, so every one of them is a deliberate insertion.
  • One woman is not given her name (v. 6). Bathsheba appears only as "the wife of Uriah", which names David's adultery and murder inside David's own royal record.
  • Only one man gets a title (v. 6). "David the king". Nobody else in the list, not Abraham, not Solomon, not Josiah, receives a royal label. The line bends toward the throne and then loses it.
  • The exile is a structural marker (vv. 11, 12, 17). "The deportation to Babylon" appears four times in seven verses. Matthew makes national catastrophe part of the King's paperwork rather than editing it out.
  • The final link changes shape (v. 16). Every other entry says "X was the father of Y". Verse 16 says Joseph was "the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born". The chain of fathers stops one step short of Jesus.
  • Verse 17 counts for you. Three sets of fourteen. Matthew tells you his own structure, which means the structure carries meaning; and if you check the Old Testament lists, names are missing.

Interpretation

Why lead with "son of David, son of Abraham"?

"The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham." Matthew 1:1, ESV

These are two covenant claims, not two compliments. God swore to Abraham that through his offspring "all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Gen 12:3), and swore to David that his son's throne would be established "forever" (2 Sam 7:12-16). Matthew opens by asserting that both oaths land on one man. Son of David is a messianic title: it says rightful king. Son of Abraham says promised blessing for the nations. Hold both, because Matthew will: the book that begins with Abraham's seed ends with "make disciples of all nations" (28:19).

MacArthur on chapter 1 notes that the Greek behind "book of the genealogy" is the exact phrase used in Genesis 5:1. If that echo is intended, Matthew is signaling a new beginning, a second Genesis, on his very first line.

Why put four scandalous women in a royal pedigree?

Tamar posed as a prostitute to trap her father-in-law Judah (Gen 38). Rahab was a Canaanite prostitute in Jericho (Josh 2). Ruth was a Moabite, from a people barred from the assembly (Ruth 1; Deut 23:3). Bathsheba is remembered here by the husband David killed. MacArthur's summary is exactly right: "Each of these women is an object lesson about the workings of divine grace." Add the pattern: three of the four are Gentiles or married into Israel from outside, and each entered the line through an irregularity that a royal publicist would have buried.

That is the argument. A dynasty advertising its purity would omit them; Matthew advertises them, because the King he is presenting saves "his people from their sins" (1:21) and will be worshiped by Gentile magi within one chapter. There may also be an apologetic edge: when Matthew reaches Mary in verse 16, a woman whose pregnancy invited slander, he has already shown four times over that God has been writing his royal line through women the neighbors gossiped about.

Fourteen, fourteen, fourteen: is Matthew counting or arguing?

"So all the generations from Abraham to David were fourteen generations, and from David to the deportation to Babylon fourteen generations, and from the deportation to Babylon to the Christ fourteen generations." Matthew 1:17, ESV

Compare 1 Chronicles 3 and you find Matthew has skipped kings: Ahaziah, Joash, and Amaziah are dropped between Joram and Uzziah (v. 8), and a generation is passed over between Josiah and Jeconiah (v. 11). This is not an error. Hebrew genealogies routinely compress, and "father of" can mean ancestor of; MacArthur calls it "a kind of genealogical shorthand" and says Matthew "seems to do this intentionally in order to make a symmetrical threefold division". The list is selective by design, so read it as a case being made, not a census being filed.

What does the shape argue? Three movements: rise to the throne, decline to the exile, and then a silent stretch of unknown names ending in Christ. The kingdom is given, lost, and restored, and the restoration is a person. Why fourteen? MacArthur is candid that "the significance of the number 14 is not clear". The common suggestion is that the Hebrew letters of David's name (D-V-D) total fourteen, so the very count spells the King's name; treat that as plausible, not proven. What is certain is the effect Matthew intends: the pattern is memorable, and it puts David at every hinge.

Does the curse on Jeconiah break the line?

"Thus says the LORD: 'Write this man down as childless, a man who shall not succeed in his days, for none of his offspring shall succeed in sitting on the throne of David and ruling again in Judah.'" Jeremiah 22:30, ESV

Jeconiah stands in verse 11, and God had barred his physical descendants from David's throne. Matthew keeps him anyway, which looks like a fatal admission until you reach verse 16, where the wording breaks: not "Joseph the father of Jesus" but "Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born". The Greek "of whom" is feminine singular; it points to Mary alone. So Joseph transmits legal title to David's throne as Jesus' adoptive father, while Jesus is not Jeconiah's biological descendant. The curse is threaded, not violated. MacArthur states it plainly: "the curse bypassed him." Luke's genealogy runs a different line from David through Nathan rather than Solomon (Luke 3:31), which many take as Mary's bloodline, the other half of the same answer.

One honest note on lenses: this passage is not one of our eschatology forks. MacArthur is premillennial and futurist, and that shapes his reading of chapters 13 and 24-25, but on chapter 1 his notes are historical and Christological, and our postmillennial home reading agrees with them line for line. Where all three teachers converge, say so; it makes the genuine disagreements later worth taking seriously.

Application

  • About God He keeps promises across forty-two generations, through adultery, prostitution, foreign blood, and a national collapse. Nothing in that list ran according to human plan, and every bit of it arrived exactly where God swore it would.
  • About you If God built his King's own family tree out of outsiders and scandal, then your history is not a disqualification from his purposes. Grace is not an exception in this line; it is the method.
  • Do Write out Matthew 1:1 by hand today, then under it write the two promises it claims (Gen 12:3 and 2 Sam 7:12-16) in your own words. Five minutes. Keep the page; you will use it again at 28:19.

The architecture of Unit 3

Read straight through, chapters 8-10 can feel like a pile of episodes. They are not. Matthew has arranged them, and once you see the frame you cannot unsee it.

  • The bracket (4:23 and 9:35). Two verses, nearly word for word identical: Jesus went "teaching in their synagogues and proclaiming the gospel of the kingdom and healing every disease and every affliction." That repeated sentence is a set of bookends. Everything between 4:23 and 9:35, the Sermon plus the miracles, is Matthew unpacking those three verbs: teaching (chapters 5-7), healing (chapters 8-9), proclaiming (the message that runs through both).
  • Three groups of miracles, roughly ten in all. Group one: the leper, the centurion's servant, Peter's mother-in-law (8:1-15). Group two: the storm, the Gadarene demoniacs, the paralytic (8:23-9:8). Group three: the ruler's daughter and the bleeding woman, two blind men, a mute demoniac (9:18-34).
  • Discipleship material wedged between the groups. After group one, two would-be followers get counted the cost (8:18-22). After group two, Matthew is called from the tax booth and the fasting question arrives (9:9-17). The seams are deliberate. Matthew keeps interrupting the power to ask what following costs.
  • Then chapter 10 turns outward. 9:36-38 gives the compassion and the harvest prayer; 10:1 hands the authority over. The unit does not end with a crowd applauding, it ends with twelve men sent out under the same power.

Now the sweep of what that authority covered. Matthew is building a list on purpose:

  • Over disease (8:1-4, 14-15; 9:20-22, 27-31). Leprosy, fever, hemorrhage, blindness. Every one instant, none of them gradual.
  • Over the unclean and the excluded (8:1-4; 9:9-13, 20-22). A leper, a tax collector, a bleeding woman: people the religious system quarantined.
  • Over distance (8:5-13). The centurion's servant is healed from across town by a word. No touch, no presence, no ritual.
  • Over nature (8:23-27). "What sort of man is this, that even winds and sea obey him?" The disciples ask the question the whole unit is designed to raise.
  • Over demons (8:28-34; 9:32-34). The Gadarene demoniacs recognize both who he is and when he will judge them.
  • Over sin (9:1-8). The paralytic hears "your sins are forgiven" first and "rise and walk" second. The walking proves the forgiving.
  • Over death (9:18-26). The last and highest exhibit. He takes a dead girl by the hand and she gets up.

And here is the structural argument. The King who spoke with authority in chapters 5-7 (7:29, "he was teaching them as one who had authority") now acts with authority in chapters 8-9. Then in chapter 10 he hands that authority to his sent ones (10:1, "he gave them authority"). Words, works, witnesses. That is the whole unit in three beats, and it is why chapter 10 is not an appendix. It is the point the miracles were driving toward.

Interpretation

Why does uncleanness keep running backwards in these chapters?

And behold, a leper came to him and knelt before him, saying, "Lord, if you will, you can make me clean." And Jesus stretched out his hand and touched him, saying, "I will; be clean." And immediately his leprosy was cleansed. Matthew 8:2-3, ESV

Under the Mosaic law, ceremonial uncleanness was contagious in one direction only. Touch a leper and you became unclean (Lev. 13-14). Touch a woman with a discharge of blood and you became unclean (Lev. 15:25-27). Touch a corpse and you were unclean seven days (Num. 19:11). Three times in this unit Jesus crosses that line, and three times the contagion runs the wrong way.

He touches the leper, and the leper comes out clean. The bleeding woman touches the fringe of his garment, and she is healed rather than him being defiled. He takes the dead girl by the hand (9:25), and she rises. In every case cleanness moves outward from him. MacArthur on chapter 9 notes that the woman's twelve-year discharge "left her permanently unclean for ceremonial reasons," meaning she "would have been shunned by all, including her own family, and excluded from both synagogue and temple." That is the woman who reached out, and Jesus called her "daughter."

This is not Jesus ignoring the law. It is the law's purpose arriving. The ceremonial code taught Israel that sin and death defile and that God is holy; it could quarantine uncleanness but never cure it. The holiness standing in front of the leper is stronger than the defilement, so contact heals instead of contaminating. Keep this in view when you reach chapter 15 and the question of what actually defiles a person. Matthew has been laying the groundwork since chapter 8.

How fast does the opposition gather, and where is it heading?

But the Pharisees said, "He casts out demons by the prince of demons." Matthew 9:34, ESV

Trace the escalation across two chapters. First it is unspoken: some scribes "said to themselves, 'This man is blaspheming'" (9:3). Then it becomes a complaint about his company, put to his disciples rather than to him: "Why does your teacher eat with tax collectors and sinners?" (9:11). Then a complaint about his practice, that his disciples do not fast (9:14). Then, finally, spoken out loud and aimed at the source of his power: he does this by Satan (9:34).

Grumbling to slander in about thirty verses. MacArthur puts the seriousness of it plainly: "The Pharisees had seen enough of Jesus' power to know it was God's power. But, in their willful unbelief, they said his was the power of Satan." That is the important thing to see. The problem was never insufficient evidence. Ten miracles in two chapters is not an evidence problem. The problem was a settled refusal, and refusal has to explain the evidence somehow, so it reaches for the worst available explanation.

Note where this is headed. The same charge returns in 12:24, and there Jesus answers it directly with the warning about blasphemy against the Spirit. Chapter 9:34 is the fuse; chapter 12 is the detonation. Matthew wants you to know that the conflict which eventually puts Jesus on a cross was already fully formed while the crowds were still marveling.

What is the delegated authority of chapter 10 for?

Jesus gives the Twelve "authority over unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to heal every disease and every affliction" (10:1), and the list of what they are to do in 10:8 matches what he has just been doing in chapters 8-9. The sending is patterned on the sender. MacArthur on chapter 10 reads this display as "a preview of the power Christ will exhibit in his earthly kingdom, when Satan will be bound (Rev. 20)," which is his futurist frame supplying the interpretation: the real kingdom power is postponed to a future millennium, and this is a sample. The postmillennial home lens of this course reads it the other way around. The binding of the strong man has already happened (12:29), the authority was actually given, and the mission that starts in 10:5 is the same mission that widens to "all nations" in 28:19 and is still running. Amillennial readers land close to that on the mission and differ on whether the earth will visibly submit before the end.

What all three agree on, and what the text itself insists on, is the direction of travel. Authority is not hoarded. He healed, then he sent people to heal; he preached the kingdom, then he sent people to preach it. Notice too the honest terms attached: sheep among wolves, floggings, households divided, a cross to carry (10:16-39). Delegated power came bundled with promised suffering. Any version of Christian mission that keeps the first and drops the second is not this chapter.

Application

  • About God Christ's holiness is not fragile. He walked into every quarantine the ancient world had, disease, demons, death, disgrace, and came out the stronger party every time. Nothing you bring him contaminates him.
  • About you The Pharisees watched ten miracles and concluded Satan did it. Unbelief is not a shortage of proof; it is a direction of the will. Ask honestly whether there is any place where you have already seen enough and are still explaining it away.
  • Do Today, pick one of the seven authorities listed above and pray it back specifically: name the disease, the fear, the sin, or the death you actually face this week, and ask the King who did that to do this. Then do the thing 9:38 commands: pray by name for one person you know who is "harassed and helpless," and pray the Lord would send a laborer. If that laborer turns out to be you, go.

Church prompt
Chapter 10 sends ordinary men out with a message and a warning. Ask someone at church this week who has actually done evangelism or missions what it cost them, not what it accomplished. Then ask your elders how the congregation supports the people it sends, since 10:10 makes support part of the arrangement. Second thing worth raising: the bleeding woman was excluded from the assembly for twelve years. Who is on the edge of our church right now, and what would it take to bring them in?

Matthew 1-28: The King and His Kingdom

~30 min · Passage: the whole Gospel of Matthew (ESV) · Final lesson of the course

You have read all twenty-eight chapters
Stop and take that in. Seventy-eight lessons ago you opened a genealogy you probably would have skipped, and you have walked from it to an empty tomb and a mountain in Galilee, verse by verse, with your own observations written down before anyone told you what to see. That is a real accomplishment and it belongs to you. Now do one more thing over the coming weeks: reread Matthew straight through, a few chapters at a sitting, with no notes and no lesson. You have the map in your head now, and the second pass is where the book stops being a series of episodes and becomes one argument. Today's lesson is the map, laid out whole.

Recall first

One last retrieval, from the two lessons that closed the book:

The whole book in one view

Matthew alternates story and sermon. Five great teaching blocks (discourses) are set into a running narrative, each closing with the same formula, "when Jesus had finished these sayings" (7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1). A prologue (1-4) and an epilogue (28:16-20) bracket the whole. Here is the course you just walked, in the eight units you walked it in:

1. The King arrives1-4Credentials, birth, forerunner, baptism, temptation, the kingdom announced
2. The Sermon on the Mount5-7Discourse 1: the righteousness of the kingdom
3. Power and mission8-10Authenticating miracles, then Discourse 2: the Twelve sent out
4. Responses to the King11-13Doubt, hostility, and Discourse 3: the kingdom parables
5. The Messiah confessed14-18Peter's confession, the cross foretold, Discourse 4: life together
6. The King in Jerusalem19-23The road that inverts everything, the entry, the confrontation, the woes
7. The Olivet Discourse24-25Discourse 5: judgment, delay, readiness, the final separation
8. Passion and commission26-28Supper, arrest, trials, cross, tomb, resurrection, all authority

Now the threads that run the length of it. These are the reason the book is one book.

1. The King

Every section argues one claim: Jesus is the King. The genealogy files his legal credentials (son of David, son of Abraham, 1:1). The fulfillment quotations give his prophetic warrant. The miracles of chapters 8-9 display his royal power over disease, storm, demons, and death. The discourses lay down his royal law. And the last paragraph states his royal claim without qualification: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" (28:18). The cruelest irony in the book is also its plainest statement of truth: the charge nailed over his head, "This is Jesus, the King of the Jews" (27:37), was written to mock him and is simply accurate. Rome posted the coronation notice without knowing it.

2. Fulfillment

Roughly a dozen times Matthew stops the story to say some version of "this took place to fulfill what the Lord had spoken by the prophet" (1:22; 2:15, 17, 23; 4:14; 8:17; 12:17; 13:35; 21:4; 27:9). That is the fulfillment formula, and on top of it he quotes or echoes the Old Testament more than sixty times. Two kinds of fulfillment are at work and you learned to tell them apart: straight prediction (Micah 5:2 names Bethlehem, and Bethlehem is where he is born) and typology, where an earlier pattern reaches its intended fullness (Israel called out of Egypt in Hosea 11:1 is not a prediction at all, yet Matthew says the Son coming out of Egypt fills it up). Isaiah supplies more of this than any other book: the virgin's son (7:14), the voice in the wilderness (40:3), Galilee of the Gentiles (9:1-2), the gentle Servant (42:1-4), the one who bore our diseases (53:4).

3. The kingdom of heaven

Matthew's signature phrase, thirty-two times, nowhere else in Scripture (see kingdom of heaven). Trace its arc across the book and you have the plot: announced at 4:17 ("Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand"), described in the Sermon on the Mount, where chapters 5-7 tell you what its citizens look like, hidden and growing in chapter 13, where it is seed in soil, a mustard shrub, leaven working through the dough, and consummated in chapter 25, where the door shuts, the accounts are settled, and the nations are separated. Announced, described, growing, consummated. That is the shape of the whole.

4. Immanuel

The book is bracketed by presence. Read the two verses side by side and you can see Matthew close his own circle:

"Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and they shall call his name Immanuel" (which means, God with us). Matthew 1:23, ESV
And behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age. Matthew 28:20, ESV

Even MacArthur on chapter 28 pauses on it: "There's a touching echo of the beginning of Matthew's Gospel here." The name given before his birth is the promise given after his resurrection, and everything between the two is the account of how God with us cost him everything.

5. Rejection and mission

No Gospel paints Israel's rejection of her King more starkly. The shadow starts early and never lifts: a king tries to kill the infant (2:16), his hometown takes offense (13:57), the leaders charge him with demonic power (12:24), the crowd that shouted Hosanna shouts crucify (21:9; 27:22), and on the cross he is forsaken even by God (27:46). But watch what Matthew pairs with the rejection at every stage. Gentile magi worship the child while Jerusalem is troubled (2:1-3). A centurion has faith unmatched in Israel (8:10). A Canaanite woman takes the crumbs and gets the whole loaf (15:27-28). The kingdom is taken from the tenants and given to "a people producing its fruits" (21:43). And the book ends with eleven Jewish men sent to "all nations" (28:19). The rejection is not the end of the mission; it is the door through which the mission goes worldwide.

Interpretation

Is Matthew one argument or a collection of stories?

One argument, and you now have the evidence for that in your own notes. The five discourses are marked by a repeated closing formula, which is authorial design, not accident. The genealogy is arranged in three sets of fourteen (1:17), which is arrangement, not chronicle. MacArthur puts the corollary honestly: Matthew "makes no attempt to follow a strict chronology," because "he is dealing with themes and broad concepts, not laying out a timeline." That is not a concession about accuracy. It is a statement about genre. Matthew is an eyewitness building a case, and the order of his material is part of the case. Whenever you wondered why an episode sat where it sat, you were asking exactly the right question.

The one fork you should keep holding honestly

You met five places where sincere Reformed Christians divide, and the largest was the Olivet Discourse. Hold the map you built there. Our home lens (postmillennial, partial preterist) reads "this generation will not pass away until all these things take place" (24:34) as the AD 70 destruction of Jerusalem, with the bodily return still ahead. MacArthur reads the whole discourse as still future and calls the other reading "a serious interpretive blunder"; James White blends near judgment with the final coming. What matters more than which one you settled on is how you got there: you read the text, you weighed the arguments, and you can state the other positions in terms their holders would accept. Keep doing that. Certainty on secondary questions is cheap; charity with an open Bible is not.

What you should carry out of this course

The stated goal of this study was never information. It was that you would be able to open a passage you have never studied and work it yourself: see what it says, determine what it means, and let it change you. You have now done that across twenty-eight chapters. You have practiced observation on genealogies, parables, miracles, sermons, controversies, prophecy, and a passion narrative. You have handled the four kinds of hard passage: the one that seems to contradict, the one that offends, the one that is disputed, and the one that is simply strange. That is not a small toolkit, and it is portable to any book of the Bible.

Be honest about it rather than sentimental. Test yourself concretely:

Self-assessment: can you work a passage unaided?
Take Matthew 12:38-42, the sign of Jonah, and give it forty minutes with a closed lesson archive and a closed study Bible. Write out, on paper: (1) ten observations, including who speaks, what is asked, and what is refused; (2) the passage's place in its context, what comes immediately before and after it and why Matthew put it there; (3) two interpretation questions the text itself raises, with your answer to each argued from the words on the page; (4) one application, specific and dated. Then open the MacArthur note on 12:39-40 and one lesson archive and check yourself. If your observations were textual, your context was right, and your interpretation held up, the course did its job. If a step felt shaky, that is not failure, it is your next month of practice. Repeat the exercise monthly on a new passage.

Application

  • About God He is the King with every credential, and he used all of them to go to a cross. The authority of 28:18 is held by hands that were nailed. You have watched him keep every promise the prophets made, absorb every rejection his people offered, and then promise to stay: "I am with you always." Nothing in your life this year is outside that authority or that presence.
  • About you You are one of the "all nations" of 28:19, reached because the mission did not stop at Israel's border. You did not earn a place at the table; you got the crumbs and were handed the loaf. And you have now heard the King's words in full, which raises the stakes of 7:24 considerably: the wise man is not the one who heard, but the one who heard and did.
  • Do Today, write one page: the five things this Gospel changed about how you see Jesus, with a verse reference under each. Then send it, or read it out loud, to one person who knows you. Date it and keep it. In a year, read it again next to a fresh page and see what has grown.
Final drill: all ten verses, start to finish
Ten verses, one per unit, activated across seventy-eight lessons. Today recite the whole slate in order, out loud, from memory, in one sitting. It takes about five minutes and it is the single best proof of what you have stored: 1:21 (the name and the mission) · 4:4 (the King's weapon) · 5:17 (fulfill, not abolish) · 6:33 (seek first) · 9:13 (mercy, not sacrifice) · 11:28-30 (come to me) · 16:24-25 (deny, take up, follow) · 22:37-39 (love God, love neighbor) · 24:35 (his words will not pass away) · 28:18-20 (all authority, all nations, always). Any verse that stalls, read it aloud five times and retest tomorrow. Then set a standing reminder: run all ten once a week for the next three months, and they are yours for life. Where to go next

There is no lesson 80. Here is what a good next season looks like, in order of what will serve you most:

  • Reread Matthew straight through, over a week or two, with no helps. Nothing else you can do will pay back as fast.
  • Read a Gospel that tells the same events differently. Mark for pace, short and urgent and relentless. Luke for the outsiders, the poor, the women, the Samaritans. John for the claims, seven signs and seven "I am" sayings and an argument about who Jesus is that runs from verse one.
  • Study Isaiah. Matthew quotes it more than any other book, and you have already met its virgin, its voice in the wilderness, its Servant, and its stricken man of sorrows through Matthew's eyes. Go see them in their own setting.
  • Or read Romans, for the doctrine underneath the story: what the cross accomplished, how a sinner is counted righteous, and what happens to Israel (chapters 9-11, the right place to settle a question Matthew 21:43 only raises).
  • Teach some of this to somebody else. Teaching is the strongest form of retention there is, stronger than rereading and stronger than any quiz in this course. Walk a friend, a child, or a small group through the five discourses, or through one parable you love. You will find out what you actually know within ten minutes, and you will know it far better afterward.
  • Keep it in the room at church. The habit of discussing what you are learning with your congregation is worth more over ten years than any curriculum. Bring a question to someone this Sunday.

Church prompt
Tell someone at church that you finished a verse-by-verse study of Matthew. Not to be impressive; to make yourself available. Then ask your pastor or a teacher one question: where in Matthew would they start someone who has never studied a book of the Bible before, and why there? Their answer will teach you something about teaching. And offer, genuinely, to walk one person through the five discourses over five weeks.

This is the end of the course, not the end of the conversation. Your teacher is still here, and still glad to take any question about any passage in this book, from chapter 1 to chapter 28: "why does Matthew put the genealogy in three fourteens?", "what did Jesus mean by the keys?", "how should I read 24:34 now that I have slept on it?", "where should I start in Isaiah?" Bring the hard ones. Ask your teacher.

Matthew 2:13-23: Out of Egypt

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 2:13-23 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Matthew 2:13-23 in your ESV, stop and read it twice now, slowly. Write at least five observations before you scroll: who speaks, how Joseph is guided each time, how many Old Testament quotations appear, and how each one is introduced. Eleven verses, three quotations. Then come back.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. No looking back:

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Three dreams, three moves (vv. 13, 19, 22). Joseph never plans; he is told. "Rise, take the child and his mother" comes twice word for word (vv. 13, 20), and the third warning turns him aside to Galilee. Joseph speaks not one syllable in the whole chapter. He only obeys, "by night" (v. 14).
  • The child is always first (vv. 13, 14, 20, 21). Matthew writes "the child and his mother", never "Mary and her son". Even the grammar keeps the King in front.
  • Three fulfillment quotations in eleven verses (vv. 15, 18, 23). Hosea 11:1, Jeremiah 31:15, and an unsourced line about a Nazarene. This is the densest cluster of the fulfillment formula anywhere in the Gospel.
  • The introductions are not identical. Verse 15 says the flight happened "to fulfill", a statement of purpose. Verse 17 shifts to "Then was fulfilled", with no purpose language attached. Matthew will not make God's purpose the subject of the sentence that reports a slaughter. Verse 23 says "prophets", plural, and quotes no one.
  • Herod's rage is calculated (v. 16). "Two years old or under, according to the time that he had ascertained from the wise men." He did the arithmetic. The mass murder of infants is a policy decision, and the text names the emotion behind it: he "became furious".
  • The route is Israel's route. Down into Egypt, preserved there, then called out. A tyrant killing Hebrew baby boys while one child escapes is the Moses story (Exodus 1-2) told again.
  • Verse 20 quietly echoes Exodus 4:19. "Those who sought the child's life are dead" repeats what God told Moses in Midian almost word for word. Matthew expects you to hear it.
  • Nazareth is a demotion, not a promotion (vv. 22-23). Fear of Archelaus pushes the family to an obscure northern town. Matthew records the retreat and calls it fulfillment.

Interpretation

1. How can Hosea 11:1 be "fulfilled" when it was never a prediction?

Open Hosea 11:1 and read it in its own context: "When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son." That is past tense, and the son is the nation. Hosea is not forecasting anything; he is remembering the exodus in order to indict Israel for the ingratitude that followed. Yet Matthew writes:

And he rose and took the child and his mother by night and departed to Egypt and remained there until the death of Herod. This was to fulfill what the Lord had spoken by the prophet, "Out of Egypt I called my son." Matthew 2:14-15, ESV

The category you need here is typology. A type is a real person, event, or institution in the Old Testament that God designed to prefigure a greater reality later; the greater reality is the antitype. The type is not a coded prediction and not an accident of resemblance. It is a pattern authored by the God who governs both ends of it. Israel's descent into Egypt, preservation, and calling out was history, and it was also a portrait waiting for its subject.

So Matthew's logic is not "Hosea predicted a trip to Egypt and Jesus took one." It is: Jesus is the true Israel. God's son by adoption failed in the wilderness; God's Son by nature will succeed there (watch for this in Matthew 4, where Jesus answers every temptation with a verse Israel disobeyed). Where the nation was called out and rebelled, the Son is called out and obeys. Everything Israel was meant to be, he is.

MacArthur on chapter 2 puts it exactly this way at 2:15: Israel's sojourn in Egypt was "a pictorial prophecy, rather than a specific verbal one", and such types "are always fulfilled in Christ". Agreed, and note how much that concession gives away. If Jesus is the true Israel, then those who are joined to him are the true Israel too, which is Paul's argument in Galatians 3:16, 29. MacArthur's system keeps Israel and the church on more separate tracks than we do; our Reformed home reading follows the typology all the way and lets the one Son gather one people. That difference will matter later in the course. It does not change a word of what verse 15 says.

2. Why quote a verse about weeping over a massacre of babies?

"A voice was heard in Ramah, weeping and loud lamentation, Rachel weeping for her children; she refused to be comforted, because they are no more." Matthew 2:18, ESV

Jeremiah 31:15 pictures Rachel, the mother of Israel buried near Bethlehem, rising to weep as her descendants are marched past Ramah into Babylonian captivity. That grief prefigures this grief. Matthew does not explain the murders, minimize them, or say God wanted them. He hands the mothers of Bethlehem a Scripture that says their sorrow is real and refuses cheap comfort.

But go on reading the chapter he quotes, because the context carries the point. Jeremiah 31:16-17 answers Rachel directly: "Keep your voice from weeping ... there is hope for your future." And Jeremiah 31:31 is the new covenant promise itself, "Behold, the days are coming ... when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel." Matthew reaches into the most hope-soaked chapter in the prophets for his lament. The child who escaped Bethlehem is the one who will say over a cup, "this is my blood of the covenant" (26:28). The weeping is quoted honestly; the chapter it comes from tells you how the weeping ends.

3. Which prophet said "he shall be called a Nazarene"?

None of them, in those words. Search the Old Testament and you will not find this sentence. Notice that Matthew, who has just named Jeremiah, here says only "the prophets", plural, and drops his usual "saying" that introduces a direct citation. He seems to be summarizing a theme rather than quoting a verse. Three honest proposals:

  • A wordplay on "branch". Isaiah 11:1 promises a shoot from the stump of Jesse, a netzer (branch) in Hebrew, which sounds like Natzeret, Nazareth. Matthew loves wordplay, and the branch texts are messianic (also Jer 23:5, Zech 6:12).
  • A theme, not a verse. "Nazarene" was a slur. "Can anything good come out of Nazareth?" (John 1:46). If Matthew means "he will be despised", the prophets said that constantly: Psalm 22:6-8, Isaiah 49:7, Isaiah 53:3. MacArthur calls this the most likely reading, and it fits the plural "prophets" best.
  • A spoken prophecy not preserved in writing. Possible, but it cannot be tested, so it explains nothing.

Say it plainly: this one is genuinely debated, and no view has won. That is not a crack in Scripture. Matthew's Jewish readers evidently caught the allusion; we are the ones reading someone else's mail nineteen centuries late. Notice what he is certainly claiming either way, because both leading options land in the same place: the King comes from a nothing town with a bad name. The credentials of chapter 1 and the worship of chapter 2 belong to a man everyone will write off by his address.

Application

  • About God He does not improvise. The exodus, the exile, and a refugee family on a night road to Egypt are one design, held by one hand. He also does not pretend that grief is fine; he quotes it.
  • About you If Jesus is the true Israel who obeyed where the nation failed, then your standing rests on his record, not the one you are anxiously trying to build. And your obedience will often look like Joseph's: no plan, no explanation, just the next instruction and a departure by night.
  • Do Read Hosea 11:1-4 and Jeremiah 31:15-17 in full today, out loud, five minutes total. Then write one sentence in your notes: what did Matthew see in each passage that I would have walked straight past?

Church prompt
The Bethlehem mothers got no rescue and no explanation, only a Scripture that named their grief. Ask someone at church this week how they have seen God comfort without explaining. It is a better question than "why does God allow suffering", because it is the question this text actually answers.

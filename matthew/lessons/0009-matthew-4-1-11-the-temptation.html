Matthew 4:1-11: The Wilderness Re-Run

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 4:1-11 (ESV, read first!) · Memory verse 2 activates today

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The Spirit does the leading (v. 1). The same Spirit who descended on him in 3:16 now leads him up into the wilderness, and the stated purpose is "to be tempted by the devil". This is not Jesus wandering off; it is an appointment.
  • Forty days and forty nights (v. 2). A loaded number in Israel's memory: Moses on Sinai without food or drink for forty days (Deut 9:9), Elijah's forty-day fast (1 Kings 19:8), and above all Israel's forty years in the wilderness (Deut 8:2).
  • "If you are the Son of God" appears twice (vv. 3, 6). The devil's opening words pick up the exact title the Father just spoke from heaven in 3:17. The attack lands directly on the last thing God said.
  • Three replies, three identical openings (vv. 4, 7, 10). "It is written", "Again it is written", "For it is written". Jesus offers no argument of his own, no display of power, and no negotiation. He quotes.
  • Satan also says "it is written" (v. 6). He quotes Psalm 91:11-12 and quotes it substantially correctly. The battle is not Scripture against no-Scripture; it is Scripture handled rightly against Scripture handled wrongly.
  • The scene escalates in setting (vv. 1, 5, 8). Wilderness, then the pinnacle of the temple in the holy city, then a very high mountain with all the kingdoms of the world in view. The stakes rise with each round.
  • Only the third reply names him and dismisses him (v. 10). "Be gone, Satan!" Jesus never debates and never asks a question; he ends the exchange when worship is demanded.
  • The angels come afterward (v. 11). "Then the devil left him, and behold, angels came and were ministering to him." Not during the hunger, not on the way down from the pinnacle. After.

Interpretation

Why the wilderness, and why forty?

Track where Jesus' three answers come from. Deuteronomy 8:3, Deuteronomy 6:16, Deuteronomy 6:13. All three sit inside the same block of Deuteronomy, and that block exists for one reason: to explain to Israel what her forty years in the wilderness were for and where she failed. Deuteronomy 8:2-3 names the purpose plainly.

"And you shall remember the whole way that the LORD your God has led you these forty years in the wilderness, that he might humble you, testing you to know what was in your heart, whether you would keep his commandments or not. And he humbled you and let you hunger and fed you with manna... that he might make you know that man does not live by bread alone, but man lives by every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD." Deuteronomy 8:2-3, ESV

Israel: led into the wilderness by God, made hungry, tested, and she grumbled for food. Jesus: led into the wilderness by the Spirit, made hungry, tested, and he answers with the very verse Israel was given and ignored. The second reply is sharper still. Deuteronomy 6:16 reads, "You shall not put the LORD your God to the test, as you tested him at Massah." Massah is the place where thirsty Israel demanded water and said, "Is the LORD among us or not?" (Ex 17:1-7). Satan is inviting Jesus to do exactly what Israel did at Massah: force God to prove himself with a spectacular rescue. The third reply, from Deuteronomy 6:13, answers the golden-calf problem: worship the LORD your God and serve him only.

This is typology, the God-designed pattern where an earlier person or event foreshadows a greater fulfillment. Matthew has already called Jesus out of Egypt as God's son (2:15, quoting Hosea 11:1). Now the son passes through water and into the wilderness for a time of testing, in the same order Israel did. Israel was forty years and failed at every point. Jesus is forty days and fails at none. He is the true Israel, doing what the nation could not, and he is doing it as our representative before he does anything else in public. MacArthur on chapter 4 puts it exactly: "Christ, like them, was led into the wilderness to be tested (cf. Deut. 8:2). Unlike them, he withstood every aspect of the test."

The Spirit led him "to be tempted": does God tempt anyone?

No. Scripture is flat about it: "Let no one say when he is tempted, 'I am being tempted by God,' for God cannot be tempted with evil, and he himself tempts no one" (James 1:13). Hold two things together. God tests, meaning he proves and refines what is really there (Deut 8:2 says so of Israel, Gen 22:1 of Abraham). Satan tempts, meaning he solicits to evil, aiming at ruin. The same event can be both, because the two agents have opposite intentions. Job is the pattern: the devil acts for destruction, and God, who never authors evil, uses the very same episode for a righteous end. MacArthur's note on 4:1 is worth keeping: "God himself is never the agent of temptation (James 1:13), but here—as in the book of Job—God uses even satanic tempting to serve his sovereign purposes."

So the Spirit's leading is not God dangling bait. It is God bringing his Son to the field where the last Adam and the true Israel would win what was lost, publicly and by the book.

The three temptations, mapped
First John 2:16 lists the world's whole arsenal: "the desires of the flesh and the desires of the eyes and the pride of life". The three rounds here line up with it. Bread for a starving body is the desires of the flesh (vv. 2-3). All the kingdoms and their glory, put in front of his eyes, are the desires of the eyes (vv. 8-9). A stunt fall from the temple pinnacle, a 450-foot drop into the Kidron Valley by ancient reckoning, caught by angels in front of the city, is the pride of life (vv. 5-6). Hebrews 4:15 says he was "tempted as we are, yet without sin". There is no category of temptation he did not meet.

Satan quotes Scripture. What exactly goes wrong?

Notice how conditional the attacks are: "If you are the Son of God." MacArthur is right that the "if" here "carries the meaning of 'since'". Satan is not expressing doubt about the identity; he is leveraging it. The proposal is that a real Son would use his power for himself, on his own schedule, apart from the Father's plan.

"If you are the Son of God, throw yourself down, for it is written, 'He will command his angels concerning you,' and 'On their hands they will bear you up, lest you strike your foot against a stone.'" Matthew 4:6, ESV

That is Psalm 91:11-12, quoted accurately enough. The corruption is in the use, not the wording: Psalm 91 promises God's care for the one who trusts him, and Satan deploys it to justify testing him. Jesus does not counter with a better proof text; he supplies the missing context, because Scripture does not contradict Scripture. Deuteronomy 6:16 rules out the whole enterprise. Take the lesson home: knowing Bible verses and handling the Bible rightly are two different skills. The devil has the first one. Only context and the whole counsel of Scripture supply the second.

Then the offer: "All these I will give you, if you will fall down and worship me" (v. 9). Was it a real offer? In a limited and borrowed sense, yes. Scripture calls Satan "the ruler of this world" (John 12:31) and "the god of this world" (2 Cor 4:4), and 1 John 5:19 says the whole world lies in his power. But that authority is usurped, delegated, and on a lease God can end. And it was already ending in that generation: in John 12:31 Jesus says in the same breath, "now will the ruler of this world be cast out". Matthew 12:29 has Jesus binding the strong man and plundering his house. The offer was a shortcut around the cross to a throne Jesus would receive anyway, and receive far more fully: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" (28:18). Our postmillennial home lens reads that authority as being actively exercised now, the strong man already bound and his house being progressively plundered as the gospel goes to the nations. MacArthur's futurist frame pushes the decisive dethroning to a future kingdom age. Both readings agree on today's point exactly: the crown Satan offered was not his to give, and the King refused a throne without a cross.

Which makes verse 11 the quiet knockout. The devil leaves, "and behold, angels came and were ministering to him." The very psalm Satan twisted is fulfilled, but in God's way and God's timing, without a stunt and without a demand. What Jesus refused to seize, the Father gave.

Application

  • About God He is sovereign even over the tempter's work. He never solicits you to evil, yet nothing that reaches you, including assault from the enemy, escapes his purpose. And he provides in his own way and timing what we are constantly tempted to grab early.
  • About you Your temptations arrive on Jesus' three fronts still: appetite, spectacle, and shortcut. And you will be tempted at exactly the point where God has most recently spoken to you, as Jesus was, straight after the voice from heaven.
  • Do Pick the one temptation you met this week. Find one specific verse that answers it, not a vague comforting idea, and write the verse on a card you will see tomorrow. Say it out loud once now. That is the whole method Jesus used three times.

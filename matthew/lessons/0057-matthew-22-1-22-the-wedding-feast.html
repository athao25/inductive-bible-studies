Matthew 22:1-22: The Wedding Feast and Caesar's Coin

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 22:1-22 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If not, stop here. Read Matthew 22:1-22 twice in your ESV and write at least five observations. Two things to track: in the parable, list every separate refusal and notice that they are not all the same kind of refusal; and in the tax question, write down exactly who is asking, because Matthew names two groups in verse 16 who did not belong together.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • "Again Jesus spoke to them in parables" (v. 1). This is the third parable in a row aimed at the same audience, the chief priests and elders who challenged his authority in 21:23. Two sons, wicked tenants, now a wedding feast. The series is one argument, and each installment is more severe than the last.
  • The king is throwing it for his son (v. 2). Not a banquet, a wedding banquet, and not for a friend but for the king's own son. That detail sets the level of insult. Refusing this invitation is a personal slight against the father and a snub of the son.
  • Two waves of servants, and increasing detail (vv. 3-4). The first group is simply refused: "they would not come." The second group carries a menu: "my oxen and my fat calves have been slaughtered, and everything is ready." The food is already cooked. The king's patience keeps extending after he has been ignored once.
  • Two different refusals sit side by side (vv. 5-6). "They paid no attention and went off, one to his farm, another to his business," while "the rest seized his servants, treated them shamefully, and killed them." Indifference and violence. Matthew does not treat the farm and the business as the lesser sin; both groups end up outside.
  • The king's response is military (v. 7). "He sent his troops and destroyed those murderers and burned their city." Troops and arson, in a story about a wedding. Mark the jolt; it is intentional.
  • The invitation goes to whoever is standing there (vv. 9-10). "Go therefore to the main roads" (older translations say "highways"), and the servants "gathered all whom they found, both bad and good." Three words worth circling. The moral quality of the guests is not the entrance requirement.
  • The king inspects (v. 11). "When the king came in to look at the guests." A hall filled by open invitation still gets examined by the host. One man has no wedding garment.
  • He is addressed as "Friend" and has nothing to say (v. 12). The king does not accuse; he asks a question. "And he was speechless." Matthew records no defense at all, which is itself the verdict.
  • The sentence uses Jesus' standard hell language (v. 13). "Outer darkness," "weeping and gnashing of teeth." He has used that pair before at 8:12, 13:42, and 13:50, so this is not a story-ending flourish.
  • Verse 14 is the summary line, not a new topic. "For many are called, but few are chosen." The "for" ties it to the scene just told.
  • The plot in verse 15 is premeditated (vv. 15-16). "The Pharisees went and plotted how to entangle him in his words," and then sent their disciples "along with the Herodians," a political party backing the Roman-installed Herod dynasty. The Pharisees despised Roman rule; the Herodians lived off it.
  • The question opens with flattery (v. 16). "Teacher, we know that you are true and teach the way of God truthfully, and you do not care about anyone's opinion." Every word of it is accurate, and every word of it is bait. They are pre-closing his escape routes: a man who does not care about opinions cannot dodge.
  • Matthew tells you what Jesus saw (v. 18). "But Jesus, aware of their malice." Not aware of their question, aware of their malice. Then he names them: "you hypocrites."
  • He asks for the coin and asks who is on it (vv. 19-20). "Show me the coin for the tax." He does not produce one himself; they hand it over. "Whose likeness and inscription is this?" He makes them say the answer out loud.
  • They marvel and leave (v. 22). No follow-up, no counter. This is the first of three attempts in chapter 22 to trap him, and all three end the same way.

Interpretation

Why does a wedding parable end with an army burning a city?

"But they paid no attention and went off, one to his farm, another to his business, while the rest seized his servants, treated them shamefully, and killed them. The king was angry, and he sent his troops and destroyed those murderers and burned their city." Matthew 22:5-7, ESV

Read the escalation across the three parables. In the two sons (21:28-32) the offense is a son who says yes and does not go. In the wicked tenants (21:33-41) the servants are beaten and killed and finally the son is killed. Here both happen at once, and the wronged party is a king, which raises the crime from breach of contract to treason. Jesus is walking the same audience up a staircase, and they have already convicted themselves at the top of the previous one (21:41).

Verse 7 landed with terrible weight on Matthew's first readers. Within a generation of Jesus saying it, Roman troops besieged Jerusalem, and in AD 70 the city and the temple burned. MacArthur on chapter 22 says so plainly: "The judgment Jesus described anticipated the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70. Even the massive stone temple was destroyed by fire and reduced to rubble in that conflagration." That is worth pausing over, because MacArthur reads Matthew through a futurist lens and still takes this one as already fulfilled in the first century. Our course's postmillennial home reading, which handles much of Matthew 24 the same way (see partial preterism), agrees with him here without argument. Keep that in your pocket for the Olivet Discourse: the AD 70 reading is not a fringe move, it is the natural reading of Jesus' judgment language against that generation, and the most futurist commentator on your shelf uses it too.

Now the fairness caution, which cuts the other way. A parable is a story making a point, not an allegory in which every prop has a hidden referent. The safe method is to let the story's own emphasis set the meaning and to resist assigning identities to details the text never assigns. The king, the son, the servants, and the burned city are anchored by the surrounding chapters and by history. The oxen and the fat calves are scenery. Read the picture, not the wallpaper.

And then verse 10 does something the invited guests never expected. The hall fills with people scraped off the roads, "both bad and good." Not the good only. The gathering is indiscriminate, which is what an offer of grace looks like from the outside: it does not screen for quality, because quality was never the currency. MacArthur calls verse 9 an illustration of "the free offer of the gospel, which is extended to all indiscriminately." If that phrase "both bad and good" makes you slightly uncomfortable, you have understood it.

Then why is the man with no garment thrown out?

"But when the king came in to look at the guests, he saw there a man who had no wedding garment. And he said to him, 'Friend, how did you get in here without a wedding garment?' And he was speechless." Matthew 22:11-12, ESV

This is the part that troubles honest readers, and the objection deserves stating at full strength: a man is dragged in off the street at an hour's notice and then punished for not being dressed for a royal wedding. That looks like a trap.

It is not, and the reason is in the story itself. Every guest was collected the same way, from the same roads, at the same notice. If proper attire were the standard, the hall would be empty and the king would be inspecting a room full of offenders. Instead exactly one man is without a garment. The only explanation that fits is the one long recognized by interpreters: the host supplied the garments at the door. That is what a king does when he fills his hall with strangers. So the missing garment is not poverty, it is refusal. MacArthur puts it exactly: "this man's lack of a proper garment indicates he had purposely rejected the king's own gracious provision. His affront to the king was actually a greater insult than those who refused to come at all, because he committed his impertinence in the very presence of the king."

Scripture gives clothing a settled meaning here. "He has clothed me with the garments of salvation; he has covered me with the robe of righteousness" (Isa 61:10). In Revelation the Lamb's bride "was granted" to clothe herself in fine linen, and the granting is the point (Rev 19:8). This is what Reformed theology calls imputed righteousness: a righteousness that is not produced by you but credited to you, Christ's own standing counted as yours and worn like a garment. The man in verse 11 represents the person who joins the visible church, takes a seat at the table, and quietly keeps his own clothes on, preferring a righteousness he made to one he must be given (cf. Rom 10:3; Phil 3:8-9).

Which is why "he was speechless" is the most devastating line in the parable. MacArthur's whole note on it is three words: "he had no excuse." He is not silenced by injustice; he is silenced because there is nothing to say. A garment was there. He did not take it.

"For many are called, but few are chosen." Matthew 22:14, ESV

Handle this verse with care and without turning the lesson into a systematics lecture. Scripture speaks of God's call in two ways. There is the general call, the summons to repent and believe that goes out to everyone who hears the gospel, and it is genuine: the servants really did go to the main roads. And there is what Paul usually means by "called," the effective drawing of God that actually brings a person in (Rom 8:30; John 6:44). Verse 14 is using the first sense. Many hear the invitation; few turn out to be the chosen who wear the garment. Two things stand together in that sentence, and Reformed theology insists on both: those who reject the invitation do so willingly and their exclusion is just, while those who come in are there by grace and by God's choosing, not by their own superior response. If you find yourself inside the hall and clothed, verse 14 does not invite you to congratulate yourself. It has just told you who bought the clothes.

What is Jesus actually saying about Caesar?

"Tell us, then, what you think. Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?" Matthew 22:17, ESV

First, see the alliance. The Pharisees were religious separatists who regarded Roman occupation as an offense against God's ownership of his land. The Herodians were a political faction whose fortunes depended on Roman-backed Herodian rule, drawn largely from the Sadducee-aligned temple establishment. On nearly every question that mattered they were opponents. MacArthur notes that "the fact that these groups would conspire together to entrap Jesus reveals how seriously both groups viewed him as a threat." When rivals stop fighting each other, look at what they have both decided to fight.

Second, see the trap, because it is engineered. The tax in view is the poll tax, a denarius a year per head, the most hated of all the Roman levies precisely because a head tax implied that Rome owned the heads. If Jesus says pay it, he endorses the occupation and the crowds that shouted Hosanna three days ago walk away. If he says do not pay it, the Herodians have a witnessed statement of sedition to hand the governor. There is no third answer available in the question as posed, and that is the design. The flattery in verse 16 exists to seal the exits.

"And Jesus said to them, 'Whose likeness and inscription is this?' They said, 'Caesar's.' Then he said to them, 'Therefore render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's.'" Matthew 22:20-21, ESV

He asks for the coin, and the argument turns on one word: likeness, the image. The coin carries Caesar's image, so let it go back to Caesar; it is his currency, stamped with his face, running in his realm. Pay the tax. That much is a real answer, and it settles something: legitimate civil authority is affirmed here, not dismissed. Paul and Peter say the same (Rom 13:1-7; 1 Pet 2:13-17). Christians pay their taxes, and Jesus is the reason.

But do not stop where the sermon usually stops. If the rule is that a thing bearing an image belongs to the one whose image it bears, then finish the sentence. What bears the image of God? "So God created man in his own image" (Gen 1:27). You do. Caesar gets back his coins because they carry his face; God gets back you, entire, because you carry his. That is not a tidy division of life into a civil sphere and a religious sphere with each keeping to its own side. It is a claim of total ownership by God over everything and everyone, including Caesar, with a small metal disc conceded along the way. As MacArthur says, Jesus "did not suggest (as some suppose) that Caesar had sole or ultimate authority in the social or political realms. Ultimately, all things are God's."

So the authority of the state is genuine and it is bounded, and the boundary is drawn by the same sentence that grants it. Caesar may claim what is Caesar's. When he reaches for what is God's, which includes worship, conscience, and the whole person, he is taking what was never his, and the Christian answer is the one the apostles gave in Acts 5:29. No wonder they marveled and went away. They came holding a two-sided trap and left holding a coin.

Application

  • About God He is a host before he is a judge. He invites twice after being ignored once, he fills his hall with the bad and the good off the roads, and he hands out the clothing at the door. His severity in verses 7 and 13 is not a different God from the one giving the feast; it is what happens when sustained generosity is finally answered with contempt. And he owns everything, Caesar included.
  • About you Notice that the first refusal in verse 5 was not hatred, it was a farm and a business. Perfectly good things, on a perfectly ordinary Tuesday, and they were enough to keep men out of the feast. Then ask the harder question from verse 11: are you wearing the garment you were given, or the one you made? Anyone can sit in the hall. And on the coin: name the place where you have quietly handed to something else what belongs only to God, whether that is a government, a party, an employer, or your own preferences.
  • Do Two concrete things today. First, write down the one commitment on this week's calendar that most reliably crowds out God, your farm or your business, and cut, move, or shrink it before the week ends; do it on the calendar, not in your head. Second, take a coin or a bill out of your pocket, look at whose face is on it, and then say aloud what bears God's image and therefore belongs to him. Keep the coin on your desk for the week as the reminder.

Church prompt
"Render to Caesar" is argued about in every congregation, usually with more heat than text. Ask two people at church this week what they think the phrase actually settles, and listen for whether their answer stops at the coin or goes on to the image. Then ask an elder a sharper question: where has your church decided that a civil authority reached past Caesar's realm into God's, and what did the church do about it? The answer will tell you how the boundary in verse 21 gets drawn in practice, which is where the verse costs something.

Questions welcome, always: "isn't it unfair to throw out a man who was dragged in off the street?", "if many are called but few are chosen, how do I know which I am?", "does paying taxes mean obeying a government in everything?" Ask your teacher.

Lesson 0073 · Unit 8: Chapters 26-28

Matthew 27:1-26: Judas and Pilate

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 27:1-26 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 27:1-26 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations. Watch especially for money, for who says the word "innocent" and who says the word "guilty," and for every attempt in the chapter to get rid of responsibility.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Two men, one problem

Matthew puts two men side by side here and lets you compare them. Judas has sold Jesus and wants the sale undone. Pilate has been handed Jesus and wants him off his desk. Both of them know they are handling an innocent man. Both of them take a public action designed to discharge the guilt, and Matthew records both actions with the same flat, merciless attention: silver thrown on a floor, water poured over hands. Neither works. That is the passage.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The council waits for daylight (vv. 1-2). "When morning came, all the chief priests and the elders of the people took counsel against Jesus to put him to death." They already had their verdict at night (26:66); the dawn session makes it official, and then they bind him and hand him to Pilate, the Roman governor, because Rome kept the power of execution to itself.
  • Judas "changed his mind" (v. 3). Matthew says he saw that Jesus was condemned, and the seeing produced the change. He is not moved by argument; he is moved by consequence.
  • Judas confesses accurately (v. 4). "I have sinned by betraying innocent blood." Every word of that sentence is true. He names the act, names it as sin, and names the victim as innocent. Note who he says it to: the chief priests and elders.
  • He makes restitution (vv. 3, 5). He brings back the thirty pieces of silver, all of it, and when they refuse it he throws it into the temple. Then he goes and hangs himself.
  • The priests are scrupulous about the wrong thing (v. 6). "It is not lawful to put them into the treasury, since it is blood money." They have just bought a betrayal with that money and they will not let the coins soil the accounts.
  • The money buys a graveyard for foreigners (vv. 7-8). The potter's field, "to bury strangers in," still called the Field of Blood "to this day" when Matthew writes.
  • Matthew cites the prophecy under Jeremiah's name (vv. 9-10). "Then was fulfilled what had been spoken by the prophet Jeremiah." The wording is closest to Zechariah 11:12-13. Write that down; we will handle it below rather than skate past it.
  • The charge changes shape before Pilate (v. 11). The council convicted him of blasphemy (26:65). Pilate asks, "Are you the King of the Jews?" A religious charge would not interest Rome; a rival king would.
  • The silence amazes the governor (vv. 12-14). "He gave him no answer, not even to a single charge, so that the governor was greatly amazed." Pilate has seen defendants. He has not seen this.
  • Pilate knows the motive (v. 18). "For he knew that it was out of envy that they had delivered him up." Matthew tells you plainly that the judge understood the prosecution was corrupt.
  • His wife sends a warning mid-trial (v. 19). "Have nothing to do with that righteous man, for I have suffered much because of him today in a dream." A Gentile woman, a dream, a verdict about Jesus' righteousness.
  • Barabbas is not a petty criminal (vv. 16-21). Matthew calls him "a notorious prisoner"; Mark 15:7 and Luke 23:19 specify insurrection and murder. The crowd asks for him by name.
  • The crowd is worked (v. 20). "The chief priests and the elders persuaded the crowd to ask for Barabbas and destroy Jesus." Matthew names the persuaders before he records the shout.
  • The hand-washing and the shout (vv. 24-25). Pilate washes his hands, says "I am innocent of this man's blood; see to it yourselves," and the crowd answers, "His blood be on us and on our children."
  • The verdict in one sentence (v. 26). "Then he released for them Barabbas, and having scourged Jesus, delivered him to be crucified." The guilty man walks; the innocent man goes to the whip.

Interpretation

Judas had remorse, confession, and restitution. Why was he lost?

"Then when Judas, his betrayer, saw that Jesus was condemned, he changed his mind and brought back the thirty pieces of silver to the chief priests and the elders, saying, 'I have sinned by betraying innocent blood.' They said, 'What is that to us? See to it yourself.'" Matthew 27:3-4, ESV

Take Judas seriously for a moment, because most people do not. He felt real anguish. He said a true sentence about his own sin, using the right word for it. He gave back every coin, which is more than most guilty men do. If you were building a checklist for a repentant sinner, he ticks three boxes.

And he perished. Repentance in the biblical sense is a turning: away from sin and toward God. Judas turned, but look at the direction. He went to the chief priests. He carried his confession to the men who had paid for the crime, and their answer is one of the coldest lines in Scripture: "What is that to us? See to it yourself." Sin's accomplices are never sin's absolution. Twenty feet away, in a courtyard on the same night, another disciple had done something comparable and gone the other direction. Peter denied Christ three times with oaths, wept bitterly, and turned back to Christ, and Peter is preaching in Acts 2.

So state the lesson sharply, because it is sharp: the difference between Judas and Peter is not the size of the sin, it is the direction of the turn. Paul draws the same line: "godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation without regret, whereas worldly grief produces death" (2 Corinthians 7:10). MacArthur on chapter 27 puts it the same way at verse 3: "Judas felt the sting of his own guilt, but this was not genuine repentance. There is a godly sorrow that leads to repentance, but Judas's remorse was of a different kind, as demonstrated by his suicide."

This is worth sitting with, because worldly grief is common and it counterfeits well. Hating what you did, being unable to sleep, wanting the consequence lifted, even confessing to someone: none of that is yet turning to Christ. The question is never how badly you feel. The question is where you take it.

Then notice the priests. They will not bank the coins, "since it is blood money" (v. 6). These men commissioned a betrayal, suborned false witnesses (26:59-60), and are at this hour arranging an execution, and their conscience trips over a bookkeeping technicality. Jesus had already named this exactly: straining out a gnat and swallowing a camel (23:24). A conscience can be extremely active and completely blind, and yours can too.

An honest difficulty: why does Matthew say Jeremiah?

Verse 9 attributes the potter's-field prophecy to Jeremiah, but the closest wording is Zechariah 11:12-13, the rejected shepherd weighed out thirty pieces of silver and told to throw them "to the potter" in the house of the LORD. This is a real question, and an old one; readers have noticed it since at least the early church. We are not going to hide it from you.

Three main proposals, none of which requires an error:

  • Jeremiah headed the scroll. The Hebrew canon was grouped as Law, Prophets, and Writings (Luke 24:44), and in some Jewish orderings Jeremiah stood first among the prophetic books. A citation from anywhere in that collection could then be filed under the name at its head, much as we might say "the Psalms say" for something in Psalm 137. This is MacArthur's answer at verse 9.
  • Matthew is blending texts. Jeremiah 19 sends the prophet to buy a potter's flask, smash it in the Valley of Hinnom, and announce that the place will be called "the Valley of Slaughter" because it is full of "the blood of innocents"; Jeremiah 32 has the prophet buy a field for silver as a witness. Potter, field, silver, innocent blood, a burial ground: the motifs are Jeremiah's, the quoted words are Zechariah's, and citing the more prominent prophet when merging sources was a normal ancient practice (Mark 1:2-3 does something similar).
  • An early copying variation. A minority have argued the name entered the text in transmission. The manuscript evidence does not favor this, so it is the weakest of the three.

The first two are both reasonable and can even work together. What matters for you is the habit: when you meet a hard seam in Scripture, do not paper over it and do not panic. Write the difficulty down, find out what careful readers have said about it, and notice that in twenty centuries the honest answers have usually been available.

Why does Matthew make sure you know Barabbas was guilty?

"So when they had gathered, Pilate said to them, 'Whom do you want me to release for you: Barabbas, or Jesus who is called Christ?' For he knew that it was out of envy that they had delivered him up." Matthew 27:17-18, ESV

Matthew calls him "a notorious prisoner" (v. 16); the other Gospels fill in that he was a rebel who had committed murder in an insurrection. So the scene at the pavement is not a choice between two innocent men or two unknowns. It is a choice between a guilty man and a man the judge has just been told is righteous, and the guilty man goes home.

Sit with the shape of that. One man deserved to die and did not. One man deserved nothing and died. And Barabbas, of all people, walked away literally free because Jesus took the cross that had his name on it. This is the doctrine of substitution, one standing in the place of another and bearing what was owed, and Matthew does not stop to explain it because he has staged it in the plot. Isaiah had written it centuries earlier: "he was pierced for our transgressions" (Isaiah 53:5). Jesus had said it himself six chapters ago: "to give his life as a ransom for many" (20:28).

The irony is thicker still. Barabbas is charged with insurrection against Caesar. Jesus is executed under the placard "King of the Jews," which is the charge of insurrection against Caesar. Rome releases the actual revolutionary and crucifies the innocent one in his place, and in doing so acts out the gospel it is trying to suppress.

Now trace Pilate. He receives the prisoner, hears the silence, and is amazed (v. 14). He knows the motive is envy (v. 18). He gets a warning from his own wife calling Jesus righteous (v. 19), which is the second time in this Gospel that Gentiles outside Israel have been steered toward Jesus by a dream, the first being the magi in 2:12. And with all of that, he condemns a man he believes is innocent, because a crowd is getting loud. Cowardice does not require ignorance. Pilate is not confused; he is afraid.

Does washing your hands actually remove anything?

"So when Pilate saw that he was gaining nothing, but rather that a riot was beginning, he took water and washed his hands before the crowd, saying, 'I am innocent of this man's blood; see to it yourselves.'" Matthew 27:24, ESV

The gesture had a biblical pedigree; Deuteronomy 21:6-9 has elders wash their hands over an unsolved killing to declare they did not shed the blood. But those elders were actually innocent. Pilate is the only man in the courtyard with the legal power to stop this, and he uses that power to authorize it. He then borrows a ritual to announce that he did not do the thing he is at that moment doing.

Notice also his words: "see to it yourselves." That is the priests' line from verse 4, handed back. Everyone in this chapter is trying to pass the parcel. And Matthew has already shown you where that road ends, twenty verses earlier: Judas also tried to hand his guilt to somebody else, and it came back to him with interest. Water does not transfer responsibility. Only one thing in this passage actually removes blood-guilt, and it is the blood of the man they are sending out to be scourged.

Verse 25: a verse that has been terribly misused

"And all the people answered, 'His blood be on us and on our children!'" (v. 25). For centuries this sentence has been quoted to justify hatred, expulsion, and murder of Jewish people. Say it plainly: that use of this verse is wicked, it is a misreading, and no Christian may repeat it.

Read what Matthew actually wrote. Verse 20 tells you first who produced this shout: "the chief priests and the elders persuaded the crowd." This is a manipulated mob in one courtyard on one morning, not a nation voting, and certainly not their descendants. Scripture itself forbids the kind of inherited guilt the verse has been made to teach: "Fathers shall not be put to death because of their children, nor shall children be put to death because of their fathers. Each one shall be put to death for his own sin" (Deuteronomy 24:16), and Ezekiel 18 argues the same case at length: "the soul who sins shall die."

And watch what the apostles did with it. Every one of them was Jewish. Weeks later Peter stood up in this same city, told this same crowd that they had crucified the Lord of glory, and then said, "Repent and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins" (Acts 2:38), and three thousand were saved. In Acts 3:17-19 he calls them brothers, grants they acted in ignorance, and calls them to turn. Paul, a Jew, wrote that his heart's desire and prayer for Israel is that they be saved (Romans 10:1). The blood these people called down on themselves is the only blood in the universe that could cover them, and the first sermon of the church offered it to them.

MacArthur's note here is brief: "The Jews accepted the blame for the execution of Jesus and did not hold the Romans responsible." Take it as a comment on this crowd's own words, and let the rest of the New Testament set the boundary. The cross was accomplished by Jewish leaders, a Roman governor, Gentile soldiers, and a deserting band of disciples, "according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God" (Acts 2:23). If you want someone to blame for the death of Christ, Scripture will give you a shorter and more uncomfortable answer than the one this verse has been abused to give.

Application

  • About God: he is not maneuvered by any of this. A council schemes, a betrayer breaks, a governor caves, a crowd is whipped up, and out of that squalid morning comes the exact substitution the prophets described. Human wickedness is real and culpable here, and it serves a purpose it never intended.
  • About you: you can feel terrible about your sin, admit it out loud, pay back what you took, and still be lost, if you never take it to Christ. Remorse looks inward or sideways. Repentance looks up.
  • Do: name one specific sin today that you have handled the Judas way, felt bad about it, maybe managed it, maybe told someone who could not forgive it, but never actually brought to Jesus. Bring it to him now, out loud, by name. Then, if there is a person you wronged, do the Peter thing rather than the Pilate thing: go and own it without hedging, no hand-washing sentences like "if I hurt you" or "mistakes were made."

Church prompt
Ask a pastor or an older believer at your church this: how does our church talk about the Jewish people, and have you ever heard Matthew 27:25 used badly? Then ask the harder personal question, how do you tell the difference in someone, or in yourself, between feeling awful about sin and actually repenting of it? Both questions are pastoral, not academic, and the second one has probably shaped how they counsel people.

Lesson 0040 · Unit 5: Chapters 14-18

Matthew 14:22-36: Walking on Water

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 14:22-36 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
Stop here if you have not read Matthew 14:22-36 twice in your ESV and written down at least five observations. This is one of the most familiar scenes in the Gospels, which makes it one of the easiest to read carelessly. Read it once at normal speed. Then read it again slowly and mark every time the text tells you what someone saw, and every time it tells you what someone feared.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Jesus "made" them go (v. 22). The verb is forceful: he compelled them into the boat and sent them ahead. They are not out there by accident, by poor planning, or by running from him. They are in open water at night because he put them there. Hold that beside 8:23-27, where they entered the storm by following him into the boat. Obedience is what got them wet both times.
  • This time he is not aboard (vv. 22-23). In chapter 8 he was asleep in the stern, present but seemingly unconcerned. Here he is on shore and they are alone on the water. Matthew has raised the difficulty, not repeated it.
  • He goes up the mountain alone to pray (v. 23). Matthew records Jesus praying privately only a handful of times, and it always sits at a hinge: here, and again in Gethsemane (26:36-44). Note also what he had just heard in v. 13, that John had been executed, and what he had just done, feeding a crowd that wanted to make him a king on their terms.
  • The timing and the distance (vv. 24-25). The boat is "a long way from the land, beaten by the waves, for the wind was against them," and he comes in the fourth watch, the last stretch of the night, roughly 3:00 to 6:00 a.m. by Roman reckoning (so MacArthur on chapter 14 at v. 25). They had been rowing into that wind for most of the night before help arrived.
  • What they see is not what frightens them (v. 26). They see him and conclude "It is a ghost!" The terror in this scene is caused by the presence of Jesus, misread. Fear here is a failure of recognition, not a failure of nerve.
  • Three short sentences in v. 27. "Take heart; it is I. Do not be afraid." Command, self-identification, command. The middle clause is the ground of the two outer ones.
  • Peter's exact request (v. 28). He does not say "let me try." He says, "Lord, if it is you, command me to come to you on the water." He asks for a word, and he only moves after he gets one: "Come" (v. 29).
  • The precise moment of failure (v. 30). "But when he saw the wind, he was afraid, and beginning to sink he cried out." The wind had been blowing the whole time. What changed was where he was looking.
  • How quickly the hand arrives (v. 31). "Jesus immediately reached out his hand and took hold of him." Peter never got beyond arm's length. The rebuke comes after the grip, not instead of it.
  • The response in the boat (v. 33). They worshiped, and said, "Truly you are the Son of God." That is a confession, and it is the first time the disciples make it. Compare 8:27, where the same men could only manage a question.

Interpretation

What is Jesus claiming by walking on the sea and saying "it is I"?

And in the fourth watch of the night he came to them, walking on the sea. But when the disciples saw him walking on the sea, they were terrified, and said, "It is a ghost!" and they cried out in fear. But immediately Jesus spoke to them, saying, "Take heart; it is I. Do not be afraid." Matthew 14:25-27, ESV

Start with the action rather than the words. In the Old Testament, treading the waves is something God does and no one else does. Job describes the Almighty as the one "who alone stretched out the heavens and trampled the waves of the sea" (Job 9:8), and the psalmist says of the exodus, "Your way was through the sea, your path through the great waters; yet your footprints were unseen" (Psalm 77:19). The sea in Hebrew imagination is chaos, the thing that swallows sailors and needs a Maker to rule it. A man strolling across it in the dark is not doing a trick. He is doing something the Scriptures reserve for God, and he is doing it deliberately in front of witnesses.

Now the words, carefully. The ESV renders v. 27 "it is I." The underlying Greek is egō eimi, which can be translated "I am." That phrase carries enormous weight in some contexts, most famously in Exodus 3:14, where God names himself to Moses, and in the Greek of Isaiah 40-55 where the Lord repeatedly says "I am he." Here is the honest part: in ordinary Greek, egō eimi is also just how a person says "it's me" when someone asks who is at the door, and it is used that way elsewhere in the New Testament (see John 9:9). So the phrase by itself does not prove a divine claim, and you should not present it to a skeptical friend as though it does. What loads it here is the setting. A figure who is walking on the chaos-water at the darkest hour of the night says "I am, do not be afraid," and the men in the boat respond by worshiping him as the Son of God. Read the sentence inside its own scene and the overtone is not manufactured; it is unavoidable. That is how a good cross-reference works: it does not put meaning into a text, it lets the text's own background be heard.

Why did Jesus put them in the storm and then stay away?

Verse 22 is the detail most retellings skip. "Immediately he made the disciples get into the boat and go before him to the other side." They are obeying. Obedience sails them straight into a headwind that beats them for hours, and this time there is no sleeping Master in the stern to shake awake. If you have been taught that being in God's will means favorable conditions, this verse quietly dismantles it. The storm in chapter 8 and the storm here were both entered under orders.

What has changed is what he is doing while they row. He is on the mountain praying, alone, after burying a cousin murdered by a tetrarch and after refusing a crowd that wanted to make him their bread king. Matthew is careful about that sequence. The disciples' worst night is spent inside their Lord's prayer, and their rescue arrives on his schedule rather than theirs. Note also that he does not calm the wind on his way out. The wind ceases only when he gets into the boat (v. 32). The point of the delay is not that God is slow. It is that the disciples needed to learn who was coming, and they could only learn it in water deep enough to drown in.

What exactly went wrong with Peter?

But when he saw the wind, he was afraid, and beginning to sink he cried out, "Lord, save me." Jesus immediately reached out his hand and took hold of him, saying to him, "O you of little faith, why did you doubt?" Matthew 14:30-31, ESV

Be exact here, because this passage gets preached badly in both directions. Peter is not a fool for asking; he asked for a word from Christ and refused to move without one, which is more than the other eleven did. And he is not a hero of technique whose method slipped. Matthew tells you the mechanism in five words: "when he saw the wind." The wind was no stronger at v. 30 than at v. 29. Nothing in the situation changed. What changed is that his attention moved from the person who said "Come" to the conditions he was walking through. Faith in this Gospel is not a force you generate; it is a direction you face. "Little faith" is Matthew's recurring diagnosis for disciples who have real faith aimed at the wrong object (6:30; 8:26; 16:8).

Then notice the order of v. 31, because the order is the pastoral point. Jesus grabs him first. The rebuke is delivered by a man who already has hold of his hand. Peter is sinking, but he is sinking close enough to be reached immediately, which is what happens when your failure occurs while you are on your way to Christ. "O you of little faith, why did you doubt?" is a real rebuke, and it is spoken inside a rescue, in that order and never the other way around. Christ does not wait for improvement before he takes hold, and he does not pretend the failure did not happen either.

And then the boat. "Truly you are the Son of God." Set it next to 8:27, where after the first storm the same men asked, "What sort of man is this, that even winds and sea obey him?" That was a question with no answer attached. Now the question has become a confession, made in worship. This is the shape of Unit 5: Matthew is walking these men from bewilderment toward the words Peter will say out loud at Caesarea Philippi in 16:16. Do not miss how it happened. They did not reason their way there in a classroom. They got there in a boat at four in the morning.

Application

  • About God He is Lord of the thing that terrifies you, and he walks on it. But he is also the Christ who prays alone on a mountain and reaches out a hand at arm's length. Power over the sea and a hand that catches a sinking man belong to the same person, and you need both to be true at once.
  • About you Your hardest week may be the result of obedience, not of a wrong turn. Stop reading rough conditions as evidence that you are out of God's will. And be honest about where your eyes go: like Peter, you probably do not lose faith so much as re-aim it at the wind.
  • Do Name the one "wind" you have been staring at this week: a diagnosis, a bill, a person, a decision. Write it on a card, and under it write Matthew 14:27 in full. Put the card where you will see it first thing tomorrow, and pray "Lord, save me" before you look at the situation again.

Primary source
In your local MacArthur notes, read three short notes and follow their pointers: 14:25 (which fixes the fourth watch at "3:00—6:00 a.m."), 14:33 (which sends you to 27:43 and 27:54, where the same title is used at the cross, once in mockery and once in confession by a centurion), and 14:36 (which sends you back to the note on 9:20 on the fringe of the garment, the tassels of Numbers 15:38-40). Compare 9:20 with 14:36 yourself: one desperate woman touched the tassel and was healed, and now a whole region is doing it. For preaching, the GTY Matthew archive has messages on 14:22-33; listen specifically for how he handles v. 31, since the test of any sermon on this text is whether the rebuke gets separated from the rescue.
Church prompt
Ask someone at your church this week about a season when obeying God led them into a harder situation, not an easier one, and what they learned about him in it. Then ask what helped them stop staring at the wind. This is a better question than a debate, and the people who have rowed all night usually know something the rest of us have only read.

Questions welcome, always: "does 'it is I' really mean 'I AM' from Exodus 3, or is that reading too much in?", "was Peter right to ask to walk out there, or was that presumption?", "if Jesus rebuked their little faith, how much faith is enough?" Ask your teacher.

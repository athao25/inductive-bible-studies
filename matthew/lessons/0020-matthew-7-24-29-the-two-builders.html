Lesson 0020 · Unit 2: Chapters 5-7

Matthew 7:24-29: The Two Builders

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 7:24-29 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
Six verses, and they end the longest sermon in the Bible. Stop and read Matthew 7:24-29 twice, slowly, before you read another line here. Write at least five observations. A hint for where to look: read verses 24-25 and then 26-27 side by side and mark every word that is the same in both, then every word that is different. The whole parable turns on that comparison.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Both men hear (vv. 24, 26). "Everyone then who hears these words of mine and does them" and "everyone who hears these words of mine and does not do them". Hearing is not the dividing line. Both men sat through the sermon.
  • Both men build (vv. 24, 26). Each one builds a house. The foolish man is not lazy and he is not irreligious; he is a builder with a finished house, exactly like his neighbor.
  • Both men get the same storm (vv. 25, 27). The three clauses are repeated word for word: "the rain fell, and the floods came, and the winds blew". No milder weather for the wise man. The storm is not the difference.
  • One word of the storm changes (vv. 25, 27). The winds "beat on that house" and it did not fall; the winds "beat against that house" and it fell. Same force, opposite outcome, and the reason is stated in verse 25: "because it had been founded on the rock".
  • The only variable is the foundation (vv. 24, 26). Rock or sand. And notice what kind of variable that is: a foundation is underground. Until the storm arrives, the two houses look alike from the street.
  • The standard is "these words of mine" (vv. 24, 26). Twice. Jesus does not say "the words of the Law" or "the words of the prophets". He makes obedience to his own sayings the bedrock.
  • The sermon ends on a crash (v. 27). "And it fell, and great was the fall of it." No third option, no partial credit, no closing invitation to think it over.
  • The voice changes at verse 28. Jesus stops and Matthew speaks: "And when Jesus finished these sayings, the crowds were astonished at his teaching." The crowds have been listening since 5:1, and their reaction is astonishment at authority (v. 29), not agreement with content.

Interpretation

1. If both men hear, what actually holds the house up?

"Everyone then who hears these words of mine and does them will be like a wise man who built his house on the rock. And the rain fell, and the floods came, and the winds blew and beat on that house, but it did not fall, because it had been founded on the rock." Matthew 7:24-25, ESV

Read the two men as a controlled experiment. Same teaching, same builder's labor, same house, same storm. One variable: doing. That is Jesus' whole point, and it lands hard on anyone who thinks of himself as a serious listener. Attending, agreeing, taking notes, being moved: all of that is on the hearing side of the line, and the foolish man had all of it.

Notice too what the parable refuses to say. It does not say the foolish man built nothing. Hearing without doing is not a neutral state, a pause before you decide. It is itself a construction project, and its foundation is sand. Every sermon you hear and do not obey is another course of bricks on that house.

This is the same line Jesus drew three verses earlier: "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the kingdom of heaven, but the one who does the will of my Father who is in heaven" (7:21). Verses 21-23 and 24-27 are two pictures of one warning. MacArthur on chapter 7 puts the caution exactly where it belongs: "Jesus was not suggesting that works are meritorious for salvation, but that true faith will not fail to produce the fruit of good works." Obedience is not the price of the house. It is the evidence that the house was ever on rock. James says it in one line: "be doers of the word, and not hearers only" (James 1:22).

2. Why is it astonishing that the rock is Jesus' words?

Any Jewish hearer knew who the Rock was. "The Rock, his work is perfect" (Deut. 32:4). "The LORD is my rock and my fortress" (Ps. 18:2). "Who is a rock, except our God?" (2 Sam. 22:32). To build on the rock meant to build on God himself. Jesus takes that image and puts his own sayings in the load-bearing position.

He is also standing inside a long tradition and quietly taking it over. Israel's wisdom writings are full of two ways: two paths in Psalm 1, wisdom and folly each building a house in Proverbs 9, and the storm test itself in Proverbs 10:25, "When the tempest passes, the wicked is no more, but the righteous is established forever." A wisdom teacher's job was to point you to that road. Jesus says the road runs through his words. The sermon that began with "Blessed are the poor in spirit" ends with the preacher himself as the foundation of the world.

3. Why does Matthew end the sermon with a formula?

"And when Jesus finished these sayings, the crowds were astonished at his teaching, for he was teaching them as one who had authority, and not as their scribes." Matthew 7:28-29, ESV

Verse 28 is a seam in the book. Matthew closes each of his five great teaching blocks, the discourses, with this same "when Jesus had finished" formula: 7:28, 11:1, 13:53, 19:1, 26:1. You met the pattern back in the book map; today you have finished discourse one of five. Mark it. The Sermon on the Mount is behind you, and chapter 8 turns from the King's words to the King's works.

The crowd's reaction names why. A scribe was a professional student and copyist of the Law, and his authority was entirely borrowed: he taught by chaining citations, Rabbi A said in the name of Rabbi B. As MacArthur on chapter 7 summarizes it, "The scribes quoted others to establish the authority of their teachings; Jesus was his own authority." Go back through chapters 5-7 and count how many rabbis Jesus cites. None. Instead: "But I say to you", six times in chapter 5, and now "these words of mine" as the rock. The crowds were not astonished that he was interesting. They were astonished that he spoke like the Author.

Application

  • About God The King's words are not advice to be evaluated; they are the ground under everything you build. And he is patient: the storm is not today, which is precisely why the foundation can be ignored for years.
  • About you Your danger is almost certainly not that you refuse to listen. You are twenty lessons into a study of Matthew. The danger is the gap between how much of this sermon you can now explain and how much of it you have actually done.
  • Do Pick one specific command from the Sermon that you have heard and not obeyed: the reconciliation of 5:23-24, the secret giving of 6:3-4, the log in your own eye in 7:5. Choose one. Do it today, before this day ends, in a way another person could observe.

Primary source
In your local MacArthur notes, read the note on 7:24-27 and the note on 7:29 together. Two things to look for. First, he reads the house as "a religious life" and the rain as "divine judgment", which is why the crash in verse 27 is so total. Second, ask whether his summary of the wise builder's foundation, "repentance, rejection of salvation by works, and trust in God's grace", is fully drawn out of these four verses or partly imported from elsewhere in Scripture. That question is not a criticism; it is the exact discipline this course is teaching you. Fuller treatment in his sermons on Matthew 7 at the GTY Matthew archive.
Church prompt
This is a good week to ask someone at church, an elder or an older believer, a plain question: "what is one thing you heard preached that you actually changed?" Then answer it yourself out loud. Sunday preaching is the main place most of us hear these words of Jesus, and this parable says the sermon is not finished when the service ends.

Questions welcome, always: "does 'these words of mine' mean the sermon only, or everything Jesus said?", "if both houses look identical, how would I know which one I am living in?", "why is the last word of the Sermon on the Mount a warning instead of a promise?" Ask your teacher.

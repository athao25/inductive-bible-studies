Matthew 17:1-13: The Transfiguration

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 17:1-13 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If not, stop here. Read Matthew 17:1-13 twice in your ESV and write at least five observations. Two things to track: every detail of light in the scene (face, clothes, cloud) and exactly where each sentence of Peter's speech is cut off. Also read the last six verses of chapter 16 again first, because verse 1 opens with a time stamp that ties this scene to that one.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Matthew dates it (v. 1). "And after six days." Matthew almost never counts days like this. He is stapling this scene to the one before it: the first announcement of the cross, Peter's rebuke, and the call to take up a cross and follow. The glory comes to men who have just been told to expect suffering.
  • Three men, not twelve (v. 1). Peter, James, and John, "by themselves," up "a high mountain." The same three are pulled aside again in Gethsemane (26:37). Nine disciples never saw this.
  • The change is in him, not on him (v. 2). "He was transfigured before them, and his face shone like the sun, and his clothes became white as light." The Greek verb behind "transfigured" is metamorphoo, a change of form from the inside out; it is the same word Paul uses of believers being "transformed" in Romans 12:2 and 2 Corinthians 3:18. Nothing is shining on Jesus. Something is shining out of him.
  • Moses' face was lit from outside; this one is not (v. 2). Exodus 34:29-35 has Moses coming down Sinai with a face that shone because he had been near God, a borrowed glow that faded and had to be veiled. Matthew's wording gives Jesus a face like the sun, which is a source, not a reflector.
  • Two men appear, and they are talking (v. 3). Not posed, not silent: "Moses and Elijah, talking with him." Luke's parallel tells you the subject, his coming departure at Jerusalem (Luke 9:31). Even the glory conversation is about the cross.
  • Peter speaks, and the Father cuts him off (vv. 4-5). "He was still speaking when, behold, a bright cloud overshadowed them." Matthew tells you the interruption happened mid-sentence. That is a deliberate detail, not a transition.
  • The voice repeats the baptism, plus three words (v. 5). "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased" is word for word what heaven said at the Jordan in 3:17. What is new is the command at the end: "listen to him."
  • Terror, then a touch (vv. 6-7). They "fell on their faces and were terrified," and Jesus "came and touched them." The one whose face was blazing a moment earlier is close enough to put a hand on them, and the first words out of the glory are "Rise, and have no fear."
  • The scene ends by subtracting (v. 8). "And when they lifted up their eyes, they saw no one but Jesus only." Moses gone, Elijah gone, cloud gone, light gone. What remains is the man they walked up the hill with.
  • A gag order with an expiration date (v. 9). "Tell no one the vision, until the Son of Man is raised from the dead." Jesus does not say never; he sets a date, and the date is the resurrection, which he had announced six days earlier (16:21).
  • The disciples' question is about the scribes, not about Jesus (v. 10). Having just seen Elijah, they ask why the scribes teach that Elijah must come first. Malachi 4:5-6 is behind the scribal teaching.
  • Jesus answers in two tenses (vv. 11-12). "Elijah does come, and he will restore all things. But I tell you that Elijah has already come, and they did not recognize him." He affirms the prophecy and then relocates its fulfillment into the recent past.
  • The last line of the conversation is about the cross again (v. 12). "So also the Son of Man will certainly suffer at their hands." What they did to John forecasts what they will do to Jesus. Verse 13 says the disciples understood he meant John the Baptist.

Interpretation

Why show three men the glory six days after announcing the cross?

"And after six days Jesus took with him Peter and James, and John his brother, and led them up a high mountain by themselves. And he was transfigured before them, and his face shone like the sun, and his clothes became white as light." Matthew 17:1-2, ESV

Start with the placement, because Matthew went out of his way to give it to you. MacArthur on chapter 17 notes that "the precise reference to the amount of time elapsed is unusual for Matthew," and concludes that "he is carefully drawing the connection between Jesus' promise in 16:28 and the event that immediately follows." That connection matters. Six days ago these men heard that their King must be killed, that Peter's protest was satanic, and that following such a King means carrying a cross. Then three of them are taken up a mountain and shown what he actually is. The vision is not a reward for good behavior. It is confirmation issued before a hard road, the way a soldier is shown the flag before the march, not after.

Now the verb. MacArthur puts it briefly: "Christ underwent a dramatic change in appearance, so the disciples could behold him in his glory." Press on the mechanics of it, because this is where people go wrong. Nothing was added to Jesus on that mountain. The word Matthew uses describes a change of form proceeding from within, so what happened was that a veil came down for a few minutes and the disciples saw what had been true of him every day they had known him. He had been eating fish and sleeping in boats with the glory of God banked behind a carpenter's face. Understand that, and the incarnation stops being an idea and becomes a scandal: the sun in a peasant's body, walking Galilee, letting people talk over him.

So why Moses and Elijah, of all the saints available? MacArthur gives the standard and correct answer, that they represent "the Law and the Prophets respectively, both of which had foretold Christ's death." Add two more threads the text expects you to pull. Both of these men met God on a mountain, Moses at Sinai (Exodus 24; 34) and Elijah at Horeb (1 Kings 19), and both were told to stand in a cleft or on the rock while glory passed by. And both had strange exits from this life: Moses was buried by God himself with no man knowing the grave (Deuteronomy 34:5-6), and Elijah never died at all but went up in a whirlwind (2 Kings 2:11). The two representatives of the old covenant Scriptures, the two mountain men, the two whose departures were unlike anyone else's, are standing here talking with Jesus about his departure. The whole Old Testament is on that hillside, and it is discussing the cross.

What exactly was wrong with Peter's three tents?

"And Peter said to Jesus, 'Lord, it is good that we are here. If you wish, I will make three tents here, one for you and one for Moses and one for Elijah.' He was still speaking when, behold, a bright cloud overshadowed them, and a voice from the cloud said, 'This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased; listen to him.'" Matthew 17:4-5, ESV

Peter is not being irreverent. He is being a good Jew: the tents are the booths of the Feast of Booths (Leviticus 23:34-42), and MacArthur reads the request as "expressing a wish to stay in that place." That is the first error. A preview is not a residence. This moment was given so the three could carry it down the mountain into the next six months of suffering, and Peter wants to build a shrine and move in.

The second error is the arithmetic. Three tents means three honored guests, and MacArthur names it exactly: "Peter erred in placing Moses and Elijah on the same level as Christ. Christ was the very one to whom Elijah and Moses had pointed." One of these three wrote the law, one of them called Israel back to it, and one of them is the Son. You do not build them matching accommodations. Notice that the correction is not delivered by argument but by removal: verse 8 clears the mountain of everyone except Jesus, and the law and the prophets go home, their witness delivered.

Then the interruption. The Father does not wait for Peter to finish the sentence. A bright cloud overshadows them, and any reader of the Old Testament knows this cloud: it led Israel out of Egypt, filled the tabernacle so that Moses could not enter, and settled on Solomon's temple (Exodus 40:34-35; 1 Kings 8:10-11). Later Jewish writers called that visible presence the shekinah glory, from a Hebrew root meaning "to dwell." Peter offers to build a tent, and God answers by being one.

Out of the cloud comes the sentence from the Jordan, unchanged, with three words added: "listen to him." Weigh those three words against the previous chapter. Six days ago Peter took Jesus aside and told him he had it wrong about the cross. Now Peter is talking again, and the Father interrupts to say: stop arranging, stop proposing, listen. That is the corrective this disciple needed, and it is the one sentence from the scene the church has to keep. Peter himself never forgot it; decades later he writes about being "with him on the holy mountain" and having heard that voice, and then immediately points readers to something he calls more sure, "the prophetic word," the Scriptures (2 Peter 1:16-19). The man who wanted to camp in the vision ends up telling the church to go read their Bibles.

And do not skim past verses 7 and 8. They fall on their faces terrified, which is the right reaction to God (Isaiah 6:5; Ezekiel 1:28; Revelation 1:17), and then the glory walks over and touches them. "Rise, and have no fear." Then: "they saw no one but Jesus only." That is the theological punchline of the whole scene. Strip away the vision, the visitors, the voice, and the light, and what you are left with is not less than what you had. It is the same person, and he is enough.

Elijah has come: how does John the Baptist fulfill Malachi?

"But I tell you that Elijah has already come, and they did not recognize him, but did to him whatever they pleased. So also the Son of Man will certainly suffer at their hands." Matthew 17:12, ESV

The scribes were not wrong. Malachi 4:5-6 promises Elijah before "the great and awesome day of the LORD," and their objection would have been obvious: if you are the Christ, where is Elijah? Jesus answers on their terms. Yes, Elijah comes and restores; and he has already been here, and you murdered him. Back at 11:14 Jesus had said it with an escape hatch built in, "if you are willing to accept it, he is Elijah who is to come." Here, to disciples, he says it flat.

The fulfillment is not reincarnation, and Matthew has already told you how it works. The angel said of John that he would go "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17), and John himself denied being Elijah in person when the priests asked (John 1:21). MacArthur states it the same way: "John came in the spirit and power of Elijah—and the Jewish leaders had killed him." So the prophecy is fulfilled by a man who matches the pattern: wilderness, camel's hair, a hard word to a compromised king and queen. This is typology, the way the Old Testament's persons and events prefigure their fulfillment in Christ and his forerunner, and it is the single most useful lens Matthew has taught you so far.

One honest note on where readings diverge. Because a futurist scheme like MacArthur's expects most prophecy to land in a still-future tribulation, it can leave room for another literal Elijah before a final day, often reading verse 11 ("Elijah does come, and he will restore all things") as a still-outstanding promise alongside John. Our postmillennial home reading, and the amillennial one too, take verse 12 as Jesus' own interpretation of verse 11: the coming is already accomplished in John, and the "restoration" is the repentance-preaching that turned hearts before the Messiah arrived. Argue it from the text, not the label. Verse 12 begins "But I tell you," which is Jesus correcting an expectation, and verse 13 says the disciples understood him to mean John and not somebody later. That is a strong case, and it does not require anyone to deny that God could do a similar work again.

Then hear the sting in the tail. "So also the Son of Man will certainly suffer at their hands." Jesus reads John's death as his own forecast. The forerunner ran ahead in ministry, and he ran ahead in martyrdom too. Six days after the first passion prediction, and minutes after being declared the beloved Son out of the shekinah cloud, Jesus walks down the mountain talking about being killed. The glory never changed the plan; it confirmed who was carrying it out.

Application

  • About God He is not stingy with assurance before he asks hard things. He gave three frightened men one look at what their Master really is, then sent them back down to a demon-possessed boy and a road to Jerusalem. And he is jealous for his Son's uniqueness: the Father will let a good man's sentence be cut in half rather than allow Jesus to be filed alongside anyone else, however great.
  • About you Your instinct is Peter's instinct, to build a tent on the good moment and stay. Mountain experiences in the Christian life, a conference, a retreat, a week when prayer came easily, are given as provisions for the valley, not as addresses. And notice what the Father asked for instead of architecture: listening. The most spiritual thing available to you today is not another experience; it is obeying words you have already been given.
  • Do Two concrete things. First, sit with Matthew 17:8 for two minutes: "they saw no one but Jesus only." Name one thing you have quietly set beside Christ as a second necessity for your peace (a person, a plan, a level of income, a political outcome), and pray it off the mountain by name. Second, take the Father's command literally today: read one chapter Jesus is speaking in (Matthew 5, 6, or 7 will do), find one sentence you have not obeyed, and do that one thing before you sleep.

Church prompt
"Listen to him" is a command about how a congregation weighs its inputs. Ask someone at church this week how they tell the difference between a genuine impression and a Bible verse, and what happens when the two conflict. Then ask an elder or your pastor how your church guards against Peter's tent-building instinct, the pull to organize around a past experience or a favorite era rather than around the ongoing preaching of Christ. Both questions are friendlier than they look, and the answers usually reveal what a church actually believes about Scripture.

Questions welcome, always: "were Moses and Elijah really there, or was it a vision?", "why keep this quiet until the resurrection?", "if John was Elijah, why did John say he was not?" Ask your teacher.

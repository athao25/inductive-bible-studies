Lesson 0076 · Unit 8: Chapters 26-28

Matthew 27:57-66: The Sealed Tomb

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 27:57-66 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 27:57-66 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations. It is ten quiet verses and easy to skim past; slow down. Watch for every detail that would matter to someone later asking "are you sure he was dead, and are you sure that was the right tomb?"

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

The day between

This is the stillest passage in the Gospel. The shouting is over, the sky has cleared, and a body is carried to a garden. Matthew slows to a whisper, and he does it deliberately: everything he records here is evidence he intends you to hold in your hand when you turn the page. Read it as the sabbath of the story, the long quiet Saturday before anything happens.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Joseph is introduced with three facts (v. 57). He is "from Arimathea," he is "a rich man," and he "also was a disciple of Jesus." Matthew names his town, his wealth, and his allegiance in a single sentence.
  • He asks; Pilate orders (v. 58). "He went to Pilate and asked for the body of Jesus. Then Pilate ordered it to be given to him." A private citizen approaches the governor who has just executed the man, and the governor releases the corpse.
  • The burial is careful, not hurried in quality (vv. 59-60). A "clean linen shroud," "his own new tomb, which he had cut in the rock," and "a great stone" rolled to the entrance. Every adjective is doing work: clean, new, great.
  • Two women sit and watch (v. 61). "Mary Magdalene and the other Mary were there, sitting opposite the tomb." Matthew does not say they wept or prayed. He says where they were sitting and what they were facing.
  • The next scene is dated by the Sabbath (v. 62). "The next day, that is, after the day of Preparation," the chief priests and Pharisees "gathered before Pilate." Men who would not enter a Gentile governor's headquarters for fear of defilement (John 18:28) transact business on the Sabbath.
  • The enemies quote him accurately (v. 63). "Sir, we remember how that impostor said, while he was still alive, 'After three days I will rise.'" One sentence contains both a slur and a correct citation.
  • Their stated fear is a stolen body (v. 64). "Lest his disciples go and steal him away and tell the people, 'He has risen from the dead,' and the last fraud will be worse than the first."
  • Three separate securities are named (vv. 65-66). Pilate says, "You have a guard of soldiers. Go, make it as secure as you can." So they went and "made the tomb secure by sealing the stone and setting a guard." Stone, seal, soldiers.

Interpretation

Why does Matthew tell us Joseph was rich?

Because a crucified criminal did not normally get a burial like this. Rome's usual practice was to leave the body on the cross or throw it into a common grave for the executed. An honorable tomb required someone with standing, nerve, and money to go ask for it. Mark and Luke add that Joseph was a member of the Sanhedrin, the ruling council that had condemned Jesus the night before, and that he had not consented to their decision. MacArthur on chapter 27 notes that Joseph and Nicodemus, "both being prominent Jewish leaders, buried Christ in Joseph's own 'new tomb' (Matt. 27:60), thus fulfilling exactly the prophecy of Isa. 53:9."

Put the two texts side by side and read slowly.

"And they made his grave with the wicked and with a rich man in his death, although he had done no violence, and there was no deceit in his mouth." Isaiah 53:9, ESV
"When it was evening, there came a rich man from Arimathea, named Joseph, who also was a disciple of Jesus." Matthew 27:57, ESV

Isaiah wrote seven centuries earlier, and the line is strange on its face: the servant's grave is assigned both with the wicked and with a rich man. Jesus was executed between two criminals, which is the first half, and buried in a wealthy man's private tomb, which is the second. This is what the course has been calling fulfillment: not a lucky coincidence, but a detail God wrote into prophecy long before he arranged the circumstances that would satisfy it. And notice who had to act for it to happen. A frightened council member had to decide, that afternoon, to walk into Pilate's presence and identify himself with a condemned man. He was free the whole time; Isaiah was right the whole time.

One more thing hangs on Joseph's request, and it is not sentimental. Pilate released the body. Roman governors did not hand over prisoners who might still be alive; Mark 15:44-45 says Pilate was surprised Jesus had died so quickly and summoned the centurion to confirm it before granting the request. The transfer of the body is an official death certificate, signed by the same authority that ordered the execution. Whatever else is argued later, Rome checked.

Why does Matthew bother telling us where two women were sitting?

"Mary Magdalene and the other Mary were there, sitting opposite the tomb." Matthew 27:61, ESV

Because in two days somebody is going to say the women went to the wrong grave. Matthew forecloses it. The same two women watch the body go in, watch the stone go across the entrance, and sit facing that spot; they are the ones who come back in 28:1, and the angel says to them, "Come, see the place where he lay." Same women, same tomb, unbroken chain of witness.

Do not rush past who these witnesses are. In first-century Jewish and Roman courts a woman's testimony carried little or no weight. If you were fabricating a resurrection account to persuade skeptics, these are the last people you would put at the center of it, and you certainly would not have them be the only ones who saw the burial and the only ones who found the tomb open. Matthew records it because it happened, and the awkwardness of the detail is part of why it is credible.

What did the guard actually accomplish?

"Sir, we remember how that impostor said, while he was still alive, 'After three days I will rise.' Therefore order the tomb to be made secure until the third day, lest his disciples go and steal him away and tell the people, 'He has risen from the dead,' and the last fraud will be worse than the first." Matthew 27:63-64, ESV

Read that verse again and let the irony land, because Matthew certainly intends it. The people who remembered that Jesus predicted his resurrection were his enemies. The disciples, who had heard him say it at least three times plainly (16:21; 17:22-23; 20:18-19), had forgotten it entirely; on Sunday morning not one of them is waiting at the tomb, and every one of them has to be convinced. The men who called him "that impostor" quoted him correctly. Unbelief here is not a failure of information. They had the data and rejected the person.

Then look at what their caution built. They asked for security until the third day, precisely the window in which the thing they feared would happen. Pilate gave them a detachment. They sealed the stone, which in that world meant a cord across the stone fixed at both ends with clay or wax bearing an official stamp, so that any tampering would be visible and would be a crime against Rome. Now count the obstacles between the body and the world: a great stone, a Roman seal, and armed soldiers whose lives depended on their watch.

Every one of those was placed by people who did not believe. And every one of them became, on Sunday, a piece of evidence they could not answer. If the tomb had been left open and unwatched, the stolen-body explanation would have been easy and permanent. Instead the tomb that opens is a tomb the state itself had certified as sealed and guarded. Matthew is writing to Jewish readers who had heard exactly that story circulating in their own city, and he tells you in 28:11-15 where it came from: the guards were paid to say the disciples stole the body while they slept. He answers the charge here, before the resurrection is even narrated. Notice also the quiet self-refutation in the cover story, which is worth spotting on your own before you get there: sleeping witnesses cannot testify to what happened while they slept.

This is a pattern you have now watched all through Matthew. Herod's murder of the infants sends the child to Egypt so that a prophecy is fulfilled (2:13-15). The council resolves not to act during the feast, and the Lamb dies at Passover anyway (26:5). Judas prices him at thirty pieces of silver, and Zechariah is fulfilled to the coin (27:9-10). Now the authorities spend Saturday building the strongest possible case against themselves. God does not merely survive opposition; he employs it. "You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (Genesis 50:20) is not a slogan, it is a description of how this Gospel works.

And then, for one day, nothing. The stone is in place, the seal is set, the soldiers are standing, and two women are sitting somewhere in Jerusalem with nothing left to do. It is worth ending here rather than reaching forward. The people in this story did not know what Sunday was. Let the chapter close the way they experienced it.

Application

  • About God: he is not on the defensive. The precautions taken by men determined to bury the story became the evidence that the story is true, and they cost God nothing to arrange. He writes the details of a rich man's tomb into Isaiah seven hundred years before there is a Joseph of Arimathea to fill it.
  • About you: Joseph had been a quiet disciple in a hostile room, and the day everything looked lost was the day he stopped being quiet. Also consider the harder mirror in verse 63: it is possible to know what Jesus said with real accuracy and still call him an impostor. Information is not faith.
  • Do: take ten minutes today and sit in the Saturday. Read the passage once more slowly, without reading chapter 28, and pray honestly about the situation in your life that currently looks sealed and guarded. Name it out loud. Then, tomorrow, read what happens to the stone.

Church prompt
Ask someone at your church what they were taught about Holy Saturday, the day between Good Friday and Easter, and whether your congregation marks it at all. Then ask a harder question: what is the earliest reason they would give a skeptical friend for believing the tomb was actually empty? Compare the answer with what Matthew put in this passage on purpose.

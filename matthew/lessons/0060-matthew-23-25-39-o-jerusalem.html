Lesson 0060 · Unit 6: Chapters 19-23

Matthew 23:25-39: O Jerusalem, Jerusalem

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 23:25-39 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 23:25-39 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations. Watch especially for what is on the outside and what is inside, for how many times "you" appears, and for the moment the tone changes from thunder to tears.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Outside and inside, again and again (vv. 25-28). Two woes run the same contrast twice: the cup is clean outside, full inside; the tomb is beautiful outside, full of bones inside. Verse 28 states the point flatly: "outwardly appear righteous to others, but within you are full of hypocrisy and lawlessness."
  • A command, not just a rebuke (v. 26). "First clean the inside of the cup and the plate, that the outside also may be clean." Jesus does not abolish the outside; he orders the sequence.
  • They build tombs and indict themselves (vv. 29-31). They honor dead prophets and say they would never have shed prophets' blood. Jesus takes their own sentence and turns it: "thus you witness against yourselves that you are sons of those who murdered the prophets."
  • A command that is really a verdict (v. 32). "Fill up, then, the measure of your fathers." He is not encouraging sin; he is announcing that the cup of guilt is nearly full and they will top it off within the week.
  • The vocabulary of judgment (v. 33). "You serpents, you brood of vipers" is the exact phrase John the Baptist used in 3:7. The book has come full circle on this group.
  • Future tense verbs (v. 34). "I send you prophets and wise men and scribes, some of whom you will kill and crucify." He speaks as the one who commissions messengers, and he already knows what will happen to them.
  • Abel to Zechariah (v. 35). The first murder in Genesis to a murder in 2 Chronicles, which is the last book of the Hebrew Bible's own ordering. The sweep is not two names; it is "every martyr in Scripture, start to finish."
  • The hinge verse (v. 36). "Truly, I say to you, all these things will come upon this generation." Underline it. You will meet the phrase again.
  • The tone breaks (vv. 37-39). Seven woes end, and the next word is a repeated name: "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem." The imagery turns from vipers to a mother hen. The last word of the chapter is a blessing, not a curse.

Interpretation

Why does cleaning the inside first make the outside clean?

"You blind Pharisee! First clean the inside of the cup and the plate, that the outside also may be clean." Matthew 23:26, ESV

This one sentence restates the whole logic of the Sermon on the Mount. Back in chapter 5 Jesus went behind the sixth commandment to anger and behind the seventh to lust, and in chapter 6 he went behind giving, prayer, and fasting to the audience you are performing for. The point was never that external obedience does not matter. The point is direction: a clean heart produces clean conduct, while polished conduct over a greedy heart produces only a nicer-looking sepulchre. MacArthur on chapter 23 puts the image sharply: "Who would want to drink from a cup that had been washed on the outside but was still filthy inside?"

The tomb picture (v. 27) is sharper still once you know the season. Passover was days away, and tombs around Jerusalem were routinely whitewashed before the feast so that pilgrims streaming into the city would not touch a grave by accident and become ceremonially unclean (Numbers 19:16). Jesus is standing in the temple, surrounded by pilgrims, pointing at a hillside of freshly painted graves and saying: that is you. Bright, conspicuous, and built to warn people away from defilement, while being full of death yourselves.

What does "this generation" mean, and why mark it now?

"Truly, I say to you, all these things will come upon this generation." Matthew 23:36, ESV

Mark this verse and remember where you saw it, because the same phrase comes back one chapter later at 24:34, and that verse is the biggest interpretive fork in the Gospel. Notice the order of your reading: you meet "this generation" here first, in a context nobody disputes. Jesus has just named the accumulated blood-guilt of centuries and says the reckoning falls on the people standing in front of him.

This course's home reading takes the phrase at face value: the generation then living, with the Roman destruction of Jerusalem and the temple in AD 70 in view. We are not arguing that out yet; the full case, and the alternatives, come in Unit 7. For now simply notice that MacArthur, whose eschatology is futurist and premillennial, reads verse 36 the same way we do: "Historically, this was the generation that experienced the utter destruction of Jerusalem and the burning of the temple in AD 70." The disagreement in Unit 7 will not be about whether Jesus can speak of AD 70. He plainly does, here. It will be about how far that reading extends into chapter 24.

How can the fiercest chapter end in tears?

"O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it! How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!" Matthew 23:37, ESV

Read that immediately after "you serpents, you brood of vipers" and feel the whiplash. It is deliberate. The fierceness and the tears come from the same heart: he denounces them because he loves them, and he weeps because the denunciation is true. A hen covering chicks is not a picture of power but of shelter at her own expense; when the fire comes, the hen burns and the chicks live. "How often would I have gathered" is an ache, not a shrug, and "you were not willing" is a real refusal, not a technicality.

Then verse 38: "See, your house is left to you desolate." "Your house" is the temple, which Jesus called "my house" only two chapters earlier (21:13). The pronoun has changed hands. And the very next sentence of the story is 24:1, "Jesus left the temple and was going away." After verse 38 that departure is not stage direction; it is the glory of God walking out of the building. Watch for it when you read tomorrow.

Verse 39 closes with a promise, not a slammed door: "you will not see me again, until you say, 'Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord'" (quoting Psalm 118:26, the same words the crowds shouted in 21:9). Readers differ on what the "until" opens onto. MacArthur reads it as a future national turning of Israel to her Messiah (Romans 11:23-26). Others read it as the acclamation that will be given at his return, willingly by his people and unavoidably by everyone else. Both readings agree on the shape: the last word Jerusalem hears from her King is Blessed, and the door is left ajar.

Application

  • About God: he is not conflicted between wrath and compassion. The same Christ says "brood of vipers" and "how often would I have gathered you," and neither line is theater. Judgment in Scripture is never cold.
  • About you: hypocrisy is not primarily lying to others; it is caring more about the outside of the cup than about what you are actually drinking. The cure is not to stop polishing the outside but to start with the inside.
  • Do: name one area where your visible practice outruns your inner reality (giving that is seen, a reputation for patience you do not feel, a Bible open where others can notice). Today, do the corresponding inside work in private: confess it by name, and do one act in that same area that literally nobody will know about.

In your local MacArthur notes copy, read three notes in this order: 23:27 (why whitewashing mattered), 23:36 (his AD 70 reading of "this generation"), and 23:37 (a careful treatment of how God can sincerely desire what he does not sovereignly bring about). That last note is longer than the others and worth the time. For preaching, his Matthew series is at gty.org; look for the sermons on the lament over Jerusalem.
Church prompt
Ask a mature believer at church this week: how do we speak plainly about sin without becoming the kind of people this chapter condemns? Matthew 23 rules out both cowardice and cruelty, and it is easier to name that than to do it. Ask how they have seen it done well.

Questions welcome, always: "does 'fill up the measure of your fathers' mean Jesus told them to sin?", "which Zechariah is he talking about?", "if God wanted to gather them, why did it not happen?" Ask your teacher.

Matthew 19-23: The King in Jerusalem

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 19-23 as a whole (ESV) · Unit 6 recap

Did you reread Matthew 19-23?
No new passage today. Before you go further, read straight through Matthew 19, 20, 21, 22, and 23 in one sitting. It takes about thirty-five minutes. Do not stop to study; read for the shape of the whole. Track two things as you go: every time someone asks Jesus a question, and who is asking it. Mark the verse where the questions stop. That single verse is the spine of this unit, and the rest of the lesson hangs on it.

The architecture of Unit 6

Unit 5 ended with a confession and a cross. Unit 6 walks that cross to the city where it will happen. Three movements, and they are not interchangeable: a road, an entrance, and a confrontation. Matthew arranged them, and the arrangement is the argument.

Movement one: the road that inverts everything (19:1 to 20:34)

Jesus leaves Galilee (19:1) and does not come back. Along the way he takes up three subjects that every culture, then and now, uses to sort people, and he turns each one upside down.

  • Marriage (19:1-15). Asked a technical divorce question, he refuses the technical ground and goes back to Genesis, "from the beginning" (19:4-6). The disciples conclude it is better not to marry, and Jesus answers by blessing children, who own nothing and can argue nothing, as the pattern of the kingdom.
  • Wealth (19:16-30). A man with everything asks what he lacks, and cannot part with it. The disciples are astonished, because wealth was widely read as proof of God's approval. MacArthur on chapter 19 puts it flatly: the camel and the needle mean "it is impossible," and Jesus is "underscoring the impossibility of anyone's being saved by merit."
  • Greatness (20:17-28). Right after the third prediction of his death, two disciples ask for the best seats. The correction is not "aim lower" but "aim at service," and it is grounded in what he is going to Jerusalem to do: give his life as a ransom for many.

Between wealth and greatness sits the parable of the laborers (20:1-16), bracketed on both sides by the same sentence: the last will be first, and the first last (19:30; 20:16). That repetition is Matthew telling you what the whole road means.

Movement two: the entrance as a staged claim (21:1-17)

Nothing about the entry is spontaneous. Jesus sends for a specific animal in a specific village, and Matthew supplies the reason with his usual fulfillment formula, quoting Zechariah 9:9. The crowds spread cloaks, which was homage reserved for royalty (2 Kings 9:13), and shout "Hosanna to the Son of David." Then he walks into the temple and clears it, and heals in it, and receives the praise of children out of Psalm 8. He is not sneaking into Jerusalem. He is claiming it, in public, by prophecy, and the claim is made at the temple rather than at the palace.

Movement three: the confrontation, and its exact shape (21:18 to 23:39)

This is the part most readers experience as a jumble of arguments. It is not. Read the sequence:

  • A sign, then a challenge (21:18-27). The fig tree in leaf but without fruit withers. Then the chief priests and elders ask the question that starts everything: "By what authority are you doing these things?"
  • He answers with three parables of judgment (21:28 to 22:14). Two sons, one who says no and goes; wicked tenants who kill the son to seize the vineyard; a wedding feast whose invited guests refuse and whose city is burned. Each one ends with the hearers condemned by their own verdict, and 21:45 says they knew it.
  • They try three traps in succession (22:15-40). Taxes to Caesar, from Pharisees and Herodians together. Resurrection, from Sadducees who deny it. The greatest commandment, from a lawyer. Three parties, three angles, three failures.
  • He asks one question they cannot answer (22:41-46). If the Messiah is David's son, why does David call him Lord in Psalm 110? And Matthew closes the exchange: "nor from that day did anyone dare to ask him any more questions."
  • Then he speaks, uninterrupted (23:1-39). Seven woes, the whitewashed tombs, the blood of the prophets, and the lament over the city, ending "your house is left to you desolate."

That is a deliberate literary structure: challenge, parables, three traps, one counter-question, silence, verdict. The debate does not fizzle out. It is closed, by him, and then sentence is passed. In 24:1 he leaves the temple, and the next thing he says is that it will come down.

Interpretation

Why does everything in this unit run backward?

Count the reversals. The last are first and the first last, twice (19:30; 20:16). The great one is the servant and the first is the slave (20:26-27). The children, who cannot argue theology, are the picture of the kingdom (19:14). A rich man walks away empty and men who left everything are told they gain a hundredfold (19:29). Two blind beggars on the roadside call Jesus "Son of David" (20:30) while the disciples who can see are still negotiating seating. The crowd's children shout the truth in the temple while the chief priests are indignant about it (21:15-16).

even as the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many. Matthew 20:28, ESV

That verse is the hinge of the whole pattern, and it explains why the reversals are not merely clever. They describe the King himself before they describe anyone else. MacArthur's note on 20:28 is worth having word for word: "A 'ransom' is a price paid to redeem a slave or a prisoner. Redemption does not involve a price paid to Satan. Rather, the ransom is offered to God, to satisfy his justice and wrath against sin." The reversal is not a life-hack about humility. It is substitution. The one with the highest claim in Jerusalem takes the lowest place in it, and that is how anyone else gets in at all.

Why does Matthew keep talking about fruit?

Track this thread through the unit and it becomes impossible to miss.

  • A fig tree covered in leaves, advertising fruit, carrying none, withered (21:19). Leaves are the promise; figs are the point.
  • Tenants who have the vineyard and refuse to hand over the fruit when the owner sends for it (21:34-39).
  • The verdict spoken over that parable: the kingdom "will be taken away from you and given to a people producing its fruits" (21:43).
  • Men who measure out a tithe of mint and dill and cumin while neglecting justice and mercy and faithfulness (23:23). On this MacArthur is exactly right: their error was not obeying the fine points but being "satisfied with their focus on the incidentals and externals" while resisting the law's spiritual meaning.

One qualification on 21:43, since it is a fork. MacArthur reads "a people producing its fruits" as the church replacing national Israel in a temporary way, with a distinct future for ethnic Israel still ahead; that is his dispensational framework at work, and you should know it is a framework and not the verse. Our home reading takes the phrase at face value and forward: the vineyard is not destroyed, it is re-tenanted, and it is expected to produce. Whatever you conclude about Israel's future (Romans 11 is the place to fight that out, not Matthew 21), the sentence in front of you says the kingdom goes to people who bear fruit. That is a warning to anyone holding leaves.

Where do these two threads meet?

They meet at the temple door. The reversal thread says the King rules by giving himself away; the fruit thread says his people are known by what they produce, not by what they display. Put them together and the temple, the most impressive display in Israel, is the obvious next casualty. That is precisely where chapter 23 lands, at a house left desolate, and it is precisely the subject the disciples raise in 24:1 when they point out the buildings. Unit 7 begins with a question about a building and ends up being about everything. You have just watched Jesus close every argument in that building. Now watch him tell you what happens to it.

Application

  • About God He answered every honest question and every dishonest one, and he did it in public, at cost, with the cross a week away. He is not fragile under examination, and he was not bluffing when he called the greatest a servant; he priced it at his own life.
  • About you This unit is uncomfortable because its targets are religious people with good habits. The tithed herbs were real obedience. The fig tree really had leaves. The question this unit puts to you is not whether you have the appearance of faith but whether anything is actually growing on the branch: justice, mercy, faithfulness, in the places where they cost you something.
  • Do Today, take the four items of 23:23 (justice, mercy, faithfulness, and the fine points you do keep) and write one honest line under each about your last week. Then pick the weakest of the four and do one specific thing about it before you sleep: a call, a payment, an apology, an hour given to someone who cannot repay it.

Primary source
In your local MacArthur notes copy, read four notes as a set, in this order: 21:19 (the fig tree as a standing symbol of fruitless Israel), 21:43 (who "a people producing its fruits" is), 22:7 (the burned city, which he ties to AD 70), and 23:36 ("this generation," where he says the same thing again). Notice something important before Unit 7: on 22:7 and 23:36 MacArthur reads the judgment as fulfilled in AD 70, which is exactly the move a partial preterist makes at 24:34. Hold that observation; you will use it. For fuller treatment, the GTY Matthew archive has a long series on Matthew 23; look for the messages on the seven woes and on the lament over Jerusalem.
Church prompt
Matthew 23 is Jesus at his harshest, and every word of it is aimed at religious leadership. That makes it awkward to read in church and worth reading there anyway. Ask a leader in your congregation which woe they find most convicting for pastors today, and listen without arguing. Then ask a friend the version aimed at the pew: where in your own life is the outside of the cup cleaner than the inside, and who would know?

Questions welcome, always: "was cursing a fig tree out of season fair?", "if the kingdom was taken from Israel in 21:43, is there any future for Israel?", "why did Jesus answer some questions and refuse others?" Ask your teacher.

Matthew 15:21-39: Crumbs and Abundance

~30 min · Passage: Matthew 15:21-39 (ESV, read first!)

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • He leaves Israel on purpose (v. 21). "Jesus went away from there and withdrew to the district of Tyre and Sidon." Tyre and Sidon are Gentile port cities on the Phoenician coast, outside the land. This is the only recorded time in Matthew that Jesus goes into pagan territory.
  • Matthew picks an old word for her (v. 22). He calls her "a Canaanite woman". Nobody in the first century was called a Canaanite; Mark, telling the same event, calls her "a Syrophoenician woman" (Mark 7:26). Matthew reaches back for the label of the nations Israel was told to drive out of the land.
  • She uses a title that is not hers to use (v. 22). "Have mercy on me, O Lord, Son of David." That is a Jewish messianic address. A Canaanite has no share in David.
  • Jesus answers with silence (v. 23). "But he did not answer her a word." Matthew states it flatly and does not explain it. Nowhere else in this Gospel does a request for mercy meet silence.
  • The disciples want her gone, and the same verb comes back twice (vv. 23, 32, 39). "Send her away, for she is crying out after us." The word they use for her is the word Jesus later refuses to use about the hungry crowd ("I am unwilling to send them away hungry") and finally uses when the meal is over ("after sending away the crowds").
  • Two refusals, then a request stripped bare (vv. 24-25). "I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel." Her reply is three words: "Lord, help me." She drops the argument and kneels.
  • The hard saying is a proverb, not a slur thrown in anger (v. 26). "It is not right to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs." Notice the vocabulary: children, bread, table, house. It is a picture of a family meal, and the whole conversation stays inside that picture.
  • She does not argue with the ordering; she reasons from inside it (v. 27). "Yes, Lord, yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters' table." She concedes the priority and finds grace in it. She asks for no place at the table, only for what falls from it.
  • The commendation is public and superlative (v. 28). "O woman, great is your faith!" Only one other person in Matthew hears his faith called great, and he was a Gentile too (8:10, the centurion).
  • Then the crowds praise a God who is not theirs by birth (v. 31). "And they glorified the God of Israel." No Jewish crowd would need to say it that way.
  • The second feeding is told with different numbers (vv. 34-38). Seven loaves, seven baskets left over, four thousand men. The first feeding had five loaves, twelve baskets, five thousand (14:17-21). Matthew keeps both sets straight, and 16:9-10 shows Jesus did too.
  • Compassion is the stated reason again (v. 32). "I have compassion on the crowd because they have been with me now three days and have nothing to eat." The same word drove the first feeding (14:14).

Interpretation

Why is this story placed exactly here?

Matthew has just finished a fight about defilement. Pharisees from Jerusalem accused the disciples of eating with unwashed hands; Jesus answered that nothing entering a person defiles him, but what comes out of the heart does (15:11, 17-20). The ink is barely dry on that when Matthew walks him out of the holy land and into pagan Phoenicia, where the first person he meets is a woman whose ancestry, religion, gender, and demon-troubled household would make her, by every category the Pharisees cared about, unclean four times over.

That is not accidental sequencing. It is the argument of chapter 15 finished in narrative: the clean hands of Israel's teachers hide dirty hearts, and here is a dirty-by-definition Canaanite whose heart produces the purest faith in the chapter. And Matthew's word choice sharpens it. "Canaanite" is an archaic, almost provocative term, carrying Genesis and Joshua with it: these were the nations under the ban, the people Israel was to dispossess. Matthew could have written what Mark wrote. He reaches for the older, harder word so that you feel the distance she is crossing, and so that the mercy she receives is measured against the largest possible gap.

What do we do with "the children's bread and the dogs"?

But he did not answer her a word. And his disciples came and begged him, saying, "Send her away, for she is crying out after us." He answered, "I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel." Matthew 15:23-24, ESV
And he answered, "It is not right to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs." Matthew 15:26, ESV

Do not let anyone rush you past this. A desperate mother asks for mercy, and she gets, in order: silence, a statement that she is outside the mission, and a proverb that puts her among dogs. If that lands badly on you, you have read it correctly. It is meant to land hard, or the ending has no force. Four things belong in a responsible answer, and none of them alone is the whole answer.

First, the word for "dogs" is a diminutive. The Greek behind it (kynarion) is not the term for the scavenging street pack that roamed ancient towns and was a standing insult; it names the small dog that lives in the house, under the table, fed by the family. MacArthur on chapter 15 makes exactly this point: "Christ employed a word here that speaks of a family pet. His words with this woman are not to be understood as harsh or unfeeling." The woman's own reply proves she heard it that way, because she picks up the picture and keeps going with it: household dogs, a master's table, crumbs that fall during the meal. Street curs are not at anyone's table. This does not make the saying gentle, but it does rule out contempt.

Second, the saying states a real order, and you have met it before. "I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" repeats the restriction Jesus put on the Twelve in 10:5-6: go nowhere among the Gentiles, but to the lost sheep of the house of Israel. This is sequence, not exclusion. The covenant promises were given to Abraham and to David, the Messiah is Israel's before he is anyone's, and the gospel goes "to the Jew first and also to the Greek" (Romans 1:16). Notice the words Jesus actually uses: the bread belongs to the children first, and the dogs are in the house. The saying does not say the dogs are fed never. It says they are not fed instead of the children. The order will hold through the ministry, break open at the resurrection, and end with "make disciples of all nations" (28:19), which is the same house with the table extended.

Third, he is drawing faith out, not driving her off. This reading is not a pious dodge, because the outcome establishes it. If Jesus wanted her gone, he had only to keep silent; the disciples had already offered to handle it. Instead he answers, and each answer gives her something to hold onto and push against. And what he says at the end is not "very well, then", but a public commendation in front of the men who wanted her removed. MacArthur says it plainly: "he was tenderly drawing from her an expression of her faith." Compare his treatment of the woman with the discharge of blood (9:20-22) or of Peter sinking (14:31), and you will see a Lord who regularly makes faith speak before he answers it.

Fourth, he is teaching the disciples in front of her. They asked him to send her away. He voices their theology back to them in its starkest form, lets a Canaanite woman out-believe them in the open air, and then commends her. If you have ever wondered how these same men later needed a rooftop vision and a Gentile centurion's household to accept that God shows no partiality (Acts 10), remember they had watched this and still had not learned it.

What must be said honestly: none of this dissolves the difficulty completely, and no honest teacher should claim it does. Jesus tests this woman in a way that would be cruel in anyone else's mouth, and he does it to a mother whose child is suffering. Scripture is not embarrassed by that, and neither should we be. It records the delay, the disciples' hardness, and the sting of the saying, because the point of the scene is a faith that survives all three.

What exactly is great about her faith?

She said, "Yes, Lord, yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters' table." Then Jesus answered her, "O woman, great is your faith! Be it done for you as you desire." And her daughter was healed instantly. Matthew 15:27-28, ESV

Look at what she does not do. She does not dispute the priority of Israel. She does not claim she has been treated unfairly. She does not appeal to her need, though it is real, or to her worth, which is the argument the Jewish elders made for the centurion in Luke 7:4-5. She takes the whole framework Jesus has just laid down, agrees with it ("Yes, Lord"), and then finds inside it a place where mercy is still available to her. If the children are fed first, there will be crumbs; if the dogs are household dogs, they are close enough to the table to receive them. She is not asking Jesus to break his own order. She is asking him to be as generous inside it as she believes him to be.

That is a stunning piece of theological reasoning, produced under pressure, by a pagan woman with a demonized daughter and no formal instruction whatsoever. She has grasped something the Pharisees of verses 1-9 never grasped: that God's grace is not a rationed supply where one person's portion means another's loss. She wants the crumbs of a Messiah, and she is right that his crumbs are enough.

So "great is your faith" is not a reward for persistence in the abstract. Faith in the Bible is never measured by intensity of feeling; it is measured by the truth of what it lays hold of. Hers is great because it sees him accurately: Lord, Son of David, able to heal at a distance with a word, and generous beyond the boundaries of his own stated mission. Track the phrase across Matthew and the pattern is unmistakable. Twice Jesus calls a faith great, and both times it belongs to a Gentile (8:10 and here), while "O you of little faith" is what he says to his own disciples (6:30; 8:26; 14:31; 16:8). Matthew, writing to Jews, keeps putting Gentile faith in the foreground: Rahab and Ruth in the genealogy, the magi in chapter 2, the centurion in chapter 8, this woman here, and the centurion at the cross who says "Truly this was the Son of God" (27:54). The book that argues hardest that Jesus is Israel's Messiah keeps showing outsiders recognizing him first.

Why feed a crowd twice?

Skeptics call verses 32-38 a duplicate of chapter 14, a single story told twice. Jesus settles that himself in 16:9-10, where he asks the disciples about both feedings and holds the two sets of numbers apart. And the details differ throughout: five loaves against seven, twelve baskets against seven, five thousand men against four thousand, one day of crowds against three days with him. Even the word for basket changes; chapter 14 uses the small personal hamper a Jewish traveler carried, while here Matthew uses a large provision basket, the kind Paul was let down over a wall in (Acts 9:25).

The setting is the point. MacArthur traces the route from Mark 7:31: north from Tyre to Sidon, then a wide arc around to the eastern side of the sea, into the Decapolis, a league of ten Hellenistic cities that was predominantly Gentile territory. And the tell is in the text itself, at verse 31: the crowds "glorified the God of Israel". People who belong to Israel do not describe their own God that way. So Jesus, having ended his Galilean ministry with bread for five thousand Jews, now ends his Gentile ministry with bread for four thousand who are largely not.

Read the two halves of this passage together and the arrangement preaches. A Canaanite woman argues that crumbs from the master's table are enough for people like her. Within a few verses, Jesus sits a Gentile crowd down on the ground and hands them loaves until they are satisfied, and there are seven full hampers of leftovers. She asked for crumbs. He served a banquet, and had more left over than he started with. The prophets promised exactly this: a feast the LORD would spread "for all peoples" on his mountain (Isaiah 25:6), and Jesus is here on a mountain (v. 29), feeding them.

One more thing worth naming. In verse 33 the disciples ask, "Where are we to get enough bread in such a desolate place to feed so great a crowd?" They watched him do this weeks ago. MacArthur's comment is deserved: no wonder he called them men of little faith. But notice the mercy in his response. He does not rebuke them here; he asks how many loaves they have and uses what they hand him. The Canaanite got great faith commended; the disciples got their little faith patiently fed. Both got bread.

Application

  • About God He is generous past the edges of what he has publicly promised, and he is generous in a way that keeps his promises intact. He does not abolish the order of redemption to help this woman; he shows that his order was always headed toward a table with room. And he is not embarrassed to be argued with by someone who takes him at his word.
  • About you Test your faith by what it lays hold of, not by how strongly you feel it. Hers was great because it was accurate about him. Then ask the uncomfortable question the disciples raise: who is the person whose need you have quietly wanted "sent away" because their crying out is inconvenient to you? Jesus fed the crowd the disciples wanted dismissed.
  • Do Two things today. First, pray for something you have half-decided you have no standing to ask for, and pray it the way she did: agree with what God has said, then ask him to be generous inside it. Second, identify one person on the outside of your church's natural circle, someone whose background or situation makes them easy to overlook, and do one concrete thing for them today: a text, a meal, an invitation, a name spoken to an elder.

Church prompt
Bring verse 23 to your small group this week: "Send her away, for she is crying out after us." Ask honestly who the church finds inconvenient to hear from, and what happens to those people when they keep asking. Then ask a second question with the feeding attached: our church has bread. Who outside our natural circle is getting only crumbs from us, and what would it look like to sit them down and feed them until they are satisfied?

Matthew 13:1-23: The Sower and the Soils

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 13:1-23 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If not, stop here. Read Matthew 13:1-23 twice in your ESV and write at least five observations. Watch two things especially: what stays the same in all four scenes of the story, and the fact that Jesus interrupts his own parable teaching (vv. 10-17) to explain why he is teaching this way. Do not skip that middle section because it looks like a detour. It is the hinge.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. From the last lessons, without looking:

A new kind of teaching, and the third discourse

Today opens the third of Matthew's five great teaching blocks, the Kingdom Parables of chapter 13. Like the others it closes with the formula "when Jesus had finished these parables" (13:53). The five discourses are laid out side by side on the book map; open it and see where you are. The Sermon on the Mount gave the righteousness of the kingdom (5-7), the Mission Discourse sent out its messengers (10), and now, after eleven chapters in which people have been steadily choosing sides, Jesus describes how the kingdom actually grows.

He does it in a form he has not used before at this scale. A parable is a story drawn from ordinary life that carries a kingdom meaning: not a fable (no talking animals, no moral tacked on), not an allegory where every detail is a code (Jesus interprets four elements here and leaves the rest alone), and not a simple illustration meant to make a hard point easy. As MacArthur puts it in his note on 13:3, "A parable is a long analogy, often cast in the form of a story," and it "required more explanation" than the plain images Jesus had used earlier (compare 5:13-16, salt and light, which need no decoding). Hold that word; verses 10-17 will tell you what parables are for, and the answer is not what most people assume.

Notice the staging in verses 1-2. Jesus leaves the house, sits beside the Sea of Galilee, and the crowd grows so large that he pushes off in a boat and teaches from the water while the people stand on the beach. The shoreline there rises into farmland. He is not inventing a scene; he is pointing at one. A sower on the hillside, a footpath cutting through the field, rocky shelf where the soil runs thin, a fringe of weeds at the edge. Everyone listening has walked past all four that week.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The seating changes (vv. 1-2). "That same day Jesus went out of the house and sat beside the sea." He teaches from a boat, and "the whole crowd stood on the beach." He sits (the posture of a rabbi teaching with authority); they stand. And "that same day" ties this directly to the hostility of chapter 12.
  • One sower, one seed, four grounds (vv. 3-8). The story never says the sower changed his throw or that some seed was defective. The only variable in the entire parable is what the seed lands on.
  • Three failures, one success, and the success is enormous (v. 8). The good soil yields "in one case a hundredfold, in another sixty, and in another thirty." Ordinary yields in that world ran far lower. The harvest is not merely adequate; it is extravagant.
  • The parable ends with a summons, not a lesson (v. 9). "He who has ears, let him hear." Jesus offers no interpretation to the crowd at all. He tells a story and stops.
  • The disciples ask about the method, not the meaning (v. 10). Their question is "Why do you speak to them in parables?" They are as puzzled by the form as by the content, and Jesus answers the form question first, at length.
  • Two groups are named, and the difference is something given (v. 11). "To you it has been given to know the secrets of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it has not been given." Both verbs are passive. Someone does the giving and the withholding.
  • Isaiah gets the longest quotation (vv. 14-15). Matthew's fulfillment quotations are usually a line or two. Here he runs Isaiah 6:9-10 out in full, ending with "lest they should see with their eyes... and turn, and I would heal them."
  • The order of the interpretation matches the order of the parable (vv. 18-23). Path, rocky ground, thorns, good soil. And every single one begins with hearing: "hears the word... hears the word... hears the word... hears the word." Four hearers, four outcomes.

Interpretation

What do the four soils actually mean?

The great discipline here is to let Jesus interpret his own story. This parable has been made to say a hundred things it does not say. Jesus gives the key in verses 18-23, and you use his key or you use none.

The path (vv. 4, 19). "When anyone hears the word of the kingdom and does not understand it, the evil one comes and snatches away what has been sown in his heart." Field paths were beaten hard by foot traffic and baked by sun, as MacArthur on chapter 13 notes at verse 4; the seed cannot even break the surface. Notice what Jesus supplies that you would never have guessed: the birds are not chance, they are Satan. The problem is not that this hearer rejected the word after weighing it. It never got in. Understanding is the missing thing, and something personal and hostile removes the seed before understanding can happen.

The rocky ground (vv. 5-6, 20-21). Not soil with stones in it but a shelf of bedrock under a thin skin of dirt. MacArthur's note is exactly right: "From the top it looks fertile, but there is no depth." The heat of the shallow rock actually makes the shoot come up faster. Jesus reads it: "this is the one who hears the word and immediately receives it with joy, yet he has no root in himself, but endures for a while, and when tribulation or persecution arises on account of the word, immediately he falls away." Two "immediatelys" bracket the whole life of this response. Quick growth, quick death. And note carefully what kills it: not doubt, not argument, but cost. This is the hearer who was never told there would be a price, or who heard and did not count it.

The thorns (vv. 7, 22). Weeds whose roots survived the plowing, invisible at sowing time. This is the only soil where the plant lives and grows to maturity and still fails.

As for what was sown among thorns, this is the one who hears the word, but the cares of the world and the deceitfulness of riches choke the word, and it proves unfruitful. Matthew 13:22, ESV

Two thorns, and they are opposites: anxiety and prosperity. What you fear losing and what you enjoy having strangle the same plant. "Deceitfulness" is a precise word; riches lie, and the lie is that they will finally quiet the cares. Nothing in this description is scandalous. No adultery, no apostasy, no persecution. A slow crowding out by a hundred reasonable things is enough. This is the soil most of us in a comfortable country should fear.

The good soil (vv. 8, 23). "This is the one who hears the word and understands it. He indeed bears fruit and yields, in one case a hundredfold, in another sixty, and in another thirty." Two marks: understanding (the thing the path lacked) and fruit (the thing the thorns lost). The three yields matter. MacArthur states the balance well: "As there were three soils with no fruit, thus no salvation, there are three kinds of good soil with fruit. Not all believers are equally fruitful, but all are fruitful." Fruitfulness varies. Fruitlessness is not an option on the good soil.

Now the observation that governs the whole thing: the seed does not vary and the sower does not vary. Same word, same thrower, four results. That kills two errors at once. It kills the idea that the failures were caused by a defective message or a poor presentation, and it kills the idea that you should sow only where you expect a return. The sower in the story broadcasts across everything. Notice too that a soil cannot till itself, which is why this parable ends in prayer rather than in self-improvement.

Why does Jesus speak in parables at all?

Most people assume the answer is "to make hard things simple." Jesus says something close to the opposite, and this is the harder half of the passage.

And he answered them, "To you it has been given to know the secrets of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it has not been given. For to the one who has, more will be given, and he will have an abundance, but from the one who has not, even what he has will be taken away. This is why I speak to them in parables, because seeing they do not see, and hearing they do not hear, nor do they understand." Matthew 13:11-13, ESV

The "secrets" (older translations say "mysteries") are not spooky knowledge for insiders. In the New Testament a mystery is truth that was hidden in former ages and has now been disclosed, and it becomes yours by revelation, not by cleverness. So a parable does two things at once. To the person who has already come to Jesus, it reveals: the disciples ask, and they are told (v. 18, and again at v. 36). To the person who has decided against him, it conceals: he hears a nice story about farming and goes home with nothing. The same words. The difference is not intelligence or education; it is whether the hearer has come.

That is a judgment, and Matthew wants you to feel it as one. He quotes Isaiah 6:9-10 in full, the commission God gave Isaiah when he sent him to a nation that had already refused him.

"'You will indeed hear but never understand, and you will indeed see but never perceive.' For this people's heart has grown dull, and with their ears they can barely hear, and their eyes they have closed, lest they should see with their eyes and hear with their ears and understand with their heart and turn, and I would heal them." Matthew 13:14-15, ESV

Read the verbs and note who does what. "Their heart has grown dull." "Their eyes they have closed." The closing is their own act. God's judgment here is judicial hardening: he confirms people in the direction they have already chosen. This is not God blindfolding innocent bystanders and then punishing them for not seeing. Remember where you are in Matthew. These same hearers have watched the miracles, demanded another sign anyway (12:38), and attributed the work of the Holy Spirit to Satan (12:24). Chapter 13 follows chapter 12; the veil comes down after the verdict is in, not before.

And here is the mercy inside the judgment. MacArthur's note on 13:3 is worth keeping whole: veiling the truth this way "was both an act of judgment and an act of mercy. It was 'judgment' because it kept them in the darkness that they loved (cf. John 3:19), but it was 'mercy' because they had already rejected the light, so any exposure to more truth would only increase their condemnation." More light on a settled rejecter does not soften him; it deepens his guilt. He adds at 13:13 that Matthew stresses their unbelief as the cause while Luke 8:10 stresses God's initiative, and "both things are true, of course," with the warning from Ezekiel 33:11 that God takes no delight in the death of the wicked. That is the honest Reformed shape of it: God is sovereign in the withholding, man is guilty in the refusing, and Scripture never lets you cancel one with the other.

Verse 12 states the principle behind it: "to the one who has, more will be given." Revelation is not a static deposit. Respond to the light you have and you receive more; refuse it and you lose even what you were holding. Nobody stands still in front of Jesus.

One flag for tomorrow. MacArthur reads chapter 13 through a dispensational lens, in which the parables describe a "mystery form" of the kingdom filling the gap while the promised earthly kingdom is postponed to a future millennium. Our home reading (postmillennial) takes the same parables as describing one kingdom that really has arrived in Jesus and grows from small beginnings to fill the earth. Nothing in today's verses forces that choice, so we will not force it here. It arrives properly in the next lesson with the mustard seed and the leaven.

What is the blessing of verses 16-17, and why is this parable placed here?

"But blessed are your eyes, for they see, and your ears, for they hear. For truly, I say to you, many prophets and righteous people longed to see what you see, and did not see it, and to hear what you hear, and did not hear it." Matthew 13:16-17, ESV

After the darkest paragraph in the chapter, Jesus turns to twelve ordinary men and pronounces them blessed. Weigh what he is claiming. Isaiah, who saw the LORD high and lifted up; David, who wrote the psalms these disciples sing; Abraham, who "rejoiced that he would see my day" (John 8:56). Every one of them strained toward what these fishermen are receiving on a beach on a Tuesday. Peter later says the prophets themselves searched their own writings trying to work out what the Spirit in them was pointing at (1 Peter 1:10-12). You hold in your hand, in a language you can read, the thing the greatest saints in redemptive history longed for and did not get. That should end complaints about devotional dryness for a while.

And notice the placement. This parable is about responses to the word, and it lands at the end of the longest stretch of responses in the book. Chapter 11: John's disciples ask, the unrepentant cities are warned, and the Father hides these things from the wise and reveals them to little children (11:25-27). Chapter 12: the Pharisees plot to destroy him (12:14), then commit the unpardonable blasphemy (12:31-32), then demand a sign, and Jesus names as his family "whoever does the will of my Father" (12:50). Matthew has been showing you the soils for two chapters. Now Jesus names them. The parable of the sower is not a change of subject; it is the interpretation of everything you have just read, and it is the reason chapter 13 stops explaining things to the crowd.

Application

  • About God He is generous past calculation, scattering the word over ground that will never yield, and he is sovereign over who understands it. Both at once. The withholding is never arbitrary and never falls on an innocent; the giving is never earned.
  • About you Do not ask which soil you are as if the answer were fixed. Ask which soil you have been this month. Which thorn is growing in your field right now, the anxiety or the money, and what has it quietly choked out that used to bear fruit? A hard path can be broken up. Thorns can be pulled.
  • Do Two things today. First, write down the three or four cares that are actually occupying your mind this week, then pray through the list by name and hand each one over, out loud. That is thorn-pulling, and it takes ten minutes. Second, sow: send one person a specific verse or a real invitation to church, without pre-judging their soil. That decision is not yours to make.

Church prompt
Worth raising in a small group this week: our evangelism instincts assume that a failed hearing means a failed presentation. This parable says three of four failures had nothing to do with the seed or the sower. What would change about how your church invites, follows up, and grieves over people if that were taken seriously? And a second, harder question for an elder: how do we tell rocky-ground joy from real conversion in someone newly excited, and what do we owe them before the cost arrives rather than after?

Questions welcome, always: "if God withholds understanding, how is anyone responsible for not understanding?", "can someone move from being thorny ground to good soil?", "does the rocky-ground hearer lose a salvation he really had?" Ask your teacher.

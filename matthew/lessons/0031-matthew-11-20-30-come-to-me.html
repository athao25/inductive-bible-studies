Matthew 11:20-30: Come to Me

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 11:20-30 (ESV, read first!) · Memory verse 6 activates today

Did you read it?
Stop here if you have not read Matthew 11:20-30 twice in your ESV and written down at least five observations. Today's passage is eleven verses long and turns a corner so sharply in the middle that reading someone else's summary first will rob you of the shock. Read it aloud once. Then read it again with a pen, and mark where the tone changes.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • A stated reason for the woes (v. 20). Matthew does not leave the motive to guesswork: Jesus denounced these cities "because they did not repent." The problem is not that they lacked evidence. Most of his mighty works had been done there. The problem is what the evidence failed to produce.
  • Three named towns, all local (vv. 21-23). Chorazin, Bethsaida, and Capernaum sit within a few miles of one another on the north shore of the Sea of Galilee. This is Jesus' own neighborhood and, in Capernaum's case, his ministry headquarters (4:13). The harshest words in the chapter fall on the places that saw him most.
  • Comparison cities chosen for scandal (vv. 21-24). Tyre and Sidon were pagan Phoenician ports under prophetic sentence (Ezekiel 26-28). Sodom is the Bible's standing byword for judgment. Jesus says these would have repented, and Sodom would still be standing, given what Galilee received.
  • A repeated comparative (vv. 22, 24). "More bearable" and "more tolerable" on the day of judgment. Both statements assume a judgment that is real, future, and graded. Nobody is told they escape it; some are told theirs will be heavier.
  • No atrocity is charged. Read the accusation carefully. These towns are not accused of murdering prophets, running Jesus out, or mocking him. Their sin is that they watched and stayed the same.
  • A hinge phrase (v. 25). "At that time Jesus declared." The next words are addressed to the Father, not the crowd. The passage moves from public denunciation to prayer without a paragraph break, and the prayer is thanksgiving.
  • A double action in the prayer (v. 25). Hidden and revealed, both attributed to the Father, and both received with thanks. Note who is on each side: the wise and understanding, and little children.
  • Three exclusive claims stacked (v. 27). All things handed over to the Son; the Father alone knows the Son; the Son alone knows the Father, plus whomever the Son chooses. Count how many times the word "no one" appears.
  • The verbs of vv. 28-29 are imperatives. Come, take, learn. The invitation is a command, and what it promises twice is rest (vv. 28, 29).

Interpretation

Why is Capernaum worse off than Sodom?

"And you, Capernaum, will you be exalted to heaven? You will be brought down to Hades. For if the mighty works done in you had been done in Sodom, it would have remained until this day." Matthew 11:23, ESV

On any scale of visible wickedness, Sodom wins the contest easily. Jesus' logic runs on a different axis: responsibility rises with revelation received. That is the principle behind the whole paragraph, and it is not new. It is why Amos says of Israel alone, "You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities" (Amos 3:2). Privilege is not insulation; privilege is accountability.

MacArthur on chapter 11 draws out the two implications plainly. On vv. 22 and 24 he says the comparatives indicate "that there will be degrees of punishment in hell for the ungodly", and on v. 23 he notes something worth sitting with: "there is no record that the people of that city ever mocked or ridiculed Jesus, ran him out of town, or threatened his life. Yet the sin of that city—indifference to Christ—was worse than Sodom's gross wickedness." No eschatological system is doing any work here; this is straight from the text.

Take the sobering step yourself. You own a Bible in your own language, you can hear expositional preaching on demand, and you are thirty-one lessons into a study of this Gospel. On Jesus' scale, that is not a safety margin. That is Capernaum's position. Repentance, remember, is a turning, not a feeling of being impressed.

If the Father hides and reveals, why does Jesus give thanks?

At that time Jesus declared, "I thank you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that you have hidden these things from the wise and understanding and revealed them to little children; yes, Father, for such was your gracious will." Matthew 11:25-26, ESV

This is one of the strongest statements of God's sovereign freedom in the Gospels, and it is not a debater's proof text but a prayer. Notice three things in order. First, the hiding and the revealing are both the Father's act. Second, the sorting does not follow intelligence or credentials; if anything it runs against them, and MacArthur is right that there is sarcasm in "wise and understanding" as a label for the Jerusalem experts. Third, the ground given is not anything in the recipients but the Father's own pleasure: "for such was your gracious will."

Then v. 27 pushes further. "All things have been handed over to me by my Father, and no one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son and anyone to whom the Son chooses to reveal him." Two "no one" statements bracket a claim of mutual, exhaustive knowledge. Read it as a creature speaking and it is either madness or blasphemy; no prophet ever claimed to know God as God knows him. Read it as the Son speaking and it is simply the truth, and it explains why saving knowledge of the Father arrives by revelation rather than by research.

This is what Reformed theology means by election: God's free choice, made before the world began and grounded in his good pleasure rather than in anything foreseen in us, to give some sinners to his Son and to open their eyes (see Ephesians 1:4-6). Two pastoral guardrails. It never means God turns away anyone who comes; v. 28 is next in the same breath. And it is never information you can use to sort your neighbors, because you cannot read the register. What you can do is what Jesus does here, which is thank the Father that anyone sees at all.

Why does the invitation come with a yoke attached?

"Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light." Matthew 11:28-30, ESV

The order of the paragraph is the whole argument. Verse 27 says salvation is the Son's sovereign gift; vv. 28-30 issue the widest invitation in the Gospel. Election and invitation are not in tension here, they are in sequence, and MacArthur's note on 11:28-30 says it as well as anyone: "the truth of divine election in v. 27 is not incompatible with the free offer to all in vv. 28-30." Your job is not to resolve the two but to obey the imperative in the second one.

Now the yoke. A yoke is the wooden beam laid across an animal's shoulders to harness it to work. Jesus does not offer to take the beam off; he offers to replace it with his own. So this is an exchange of masters, not a discharge from all obligation. What changes is who is driving and what he is like. The burden of self-salvation is crushing precisely because it never ends; his burden is light because he carries the weight of it himself and because he is, in his own words, "gentle and lowly in heart."

Do not hurry past that phrase. It is the only place in the four Gospels where Jesus describes his own heart, and of everything he could have named (holy, mighty, righteous, all true) he named this. And notice who is invited: not the qualified, but "all who labor and are heavy laden." That is the first Beatitude again (5:3), poverty of spirit as the only entrance requirement, which is no requirement at all except honesty.

Application

  • About God The same mouth pronounced the woes and issued the invitation, in one sitting, without changing its mind. A God who is only severe cannot be loved and a God who is only tender cannot be trusted. Refuse to choose between vv. 21 and 28; the text will not let you.
  • About you Ask which of the two halves you instinctively skip. If you skip the woes, your Christ is sentimental. If you skip the invitation, your Christ is a taskmaster, and you are probably still wearing the old yoke while attending church in it.
  • Do Name one specific truth you have heard clearly and have not obeyed: a sin you have kept, a call you have dodged, a person you have not forgiven. Write it on one line today, and take one concrete step on it before you sleep. Hearing without turning is exactly what Capernaum did.
Memory verse 6: Matthew 11:28-30
Verse 6 activates today, and it is the longest one on the slate so far. Learn it in three bites, one per verse: the call and the promise (v. 28), the yoke and his heart (v. 29), the reason (v. 30). Say each bite aloud ten times, then chain them. Full text and the reason for the choice are at the memory verse slate.

Two accuracy traps to watch. It is "labor and are heavy laden", not "labor and are heavy burdened". And v. 29 promises "rest for your souls" while v. 28 promises simply "rest"; keep them distinct. Before you close, run verses 1 through 5 once from memory as well.

Primary source
In your local MacArthur notes, read three notes in this order: 11:23 (why indifference outweighed Sodom's wickedness), 11:26 (which he calls "a powerful affirmation of the sovereignty of God over all the affairs of men"), and 11:28-30, where he holds election and the free offer together in one paragraph. Watch how he gets from the sovereignty of v. 27 to the open invitation of v. 28 without softening either. For preaching, the GTY Matthew archive has messages on 11:28-30; look for how he handles the yoke image, since that is where most treatments go thin.
Church prompt
Ask someone at church this week how they would explain v. 27 and v. 28 to a person who says the two contradict each other. Do not lead with a label like Calvinism; lead with the two verses, in order, and let the sequence do the work. Listen more than you argue. This passage is a good test of whether a doctrine has been received as comfort or as ammunition.

Questions welcome, always: "if God hides things from some people, how is the judgment on them just?", "does a lighter judgment mean some in hell are nearly fine?", "what exactly is the yoke Jesus takes off me, if he leaves one on?" Ask your teacher.

Lesson 0071 · Unit 8: Chapters 26-28

Matthew 26:31-56: Gethsemane

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 26:31-56 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 26:31-56 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations. Watch for how many times Jesus prays and what changes between the prayers, for every word about the Scriptures being fulfilled, and for who is said to strike the shepherd.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • He predicts the desertion of all of them, not just Judas (v. 31). "You will all fall away because of me this night." The betrayer has already left the table; these eleven are the loyal ones, and they are told in advance what they will do.
  • He proves it from Zechariah, and God is the one striking (v. 31). "I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered." Zechariah 13:7 has the command "Strike the shepherd"; Matthew's wording makes the speaker God himself. Note who holds the sword in that sentence.
  • The restoration is promised before the failure happens (v. 32). "But after I am raised up, I will go before you to Galilee." He schedules the reunion before they have run.
  • Peter's vow is sincere and doubled (vv. 33, 35). "Though they all fall away because of you, I will never fall away," and then "Even if I must die with you, I will not deny you!" Matthew adds that all the disciples said the same.
  • The prediction gets a clock on it (v. 34). "This very night, before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times." A time, a number, and a signal.
  • Matthew's vocabulary for Jesus' distress is extreme (vv. 37-38). "He began to be sorrowful and troubled," then "My soul is very sorrowful, even to death." Nowhere else in this Gospel is he described like this.
  • Three circles, and he ends up alone (vv. 36-39). Eight disciples sit at the edge, three go further in, and Jesus goes further still and "fell on his face." A man who normally prayed standing or looking up is face down in the dirt.
  • He prays three times and returns three times (vv. 39-44). Each time he finds them sleeping. The second prayer is not identical to the first; the third repeats the second, "saying the same words again."
  • The signal for the arrest is a kiss (vv. 48-49). "The one I will kiss is the man." Judas greets him "Rabbi," the same word he used at the table (26:25), and kisses him.
  • Jesus stops the only defense offered (vv. 51-53). A sword is drawn, an ear comes off, and Jesus tells the swordsman to put it away, then names a force of more than twelve legions of angels he refuses to call.
  • The word "must" governs the whole scene (v. 54). "How then should the Scriptures be fulfilled, that it must be so?" Repeated in v. 56: "But all this has taken place that the Scriptures of the prophets might be fulfilled."
  • The last clause is eight flat words (v. 56). "Then all the disciples left him and fled." No comment, no excuse, no softening. Verse 31 has come true within the hour.

Interpretation

Why does Jesus say God will strike the shepherd?

"You will all fall away because of me this night. For it is written, 'I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered.' But after I am raised up, I will go before you to Galilee." Matthew 26:31-32, ESV

Everything in the next two chapters is done by wicked men: a betrayer, a rigged council, a cowardly governor, a mocking cohort, nails. And before any of it starts, Jesus quotes a prophet who says the striking is God's. That is the single most important sentence for understanding what happens on Friday. The men are guilty of murder, and behind their free and evil choices the hand that falls on the shepherd is the Father's. Isaiah had said it just as bluntly: "it was the will of the LORD to crush him" (Isaiah 53:10). Keep both halves. If the cross is only what men did to Jesus, it is a tragedy. If it is also what God did to Jesus, it is an atonement.

Then notice the order of verses 31 and 32. He tells them they will scatter, and in the same breath tells them where to meet him afterward. The resurrection appointment is made before the desertion is committed. MacArthur on chapter 26 is careful here: the word for "fall away" is the same one used in 24:10, but "Jesus spoke of something less than full and final apostasy. In a moment of fleshly fear they disowned Christ (26:34), but he prayed that their faith would not fail (Luke 22:32; John 17:9-11), and that prayer was answered." Grace is not a reaction to your failure. It was arranged before it.

Peter's answer, "Though they all fall away because of you, I will never fall away," is worth pausing over, because it is not hypocrisy. He means every word, and hours later he will draw a sword against an armed mob for Jesus. His problem is not insincerity; it is that he believes his loyalty is a stronger thing than his flesh. Jesus is about to say aloud what Peter has not yet learned about himself.

What is in the cup?

"My soul is very sorrowful, even to death; remain here, and watch with me." And going a little farther he fell on his face and prayed, saying, "My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will." Matthew 26:38-39, ESV

Read that honestly and a problem surfaces. Christians have gone to stakes and lions singing. Socrates drank hemlock discussing philosophy. Here the Son of God is face down on the ground, shaking, asking for a way out. If the cross is only a painful death, then Jesus faced it worse than his own martyrs did. Any answer that dodges that question is not worth having.

The answer is in the word "cup," and it is not a metaphor Jesus invented. In the Old Testament the cup handed to someone from God's hand is a fixed image for his judgment on sin.

"For in the hand of the LORD there is a cup with foaming wine, well mixed, and he pours out from it, and all the wicked of the earth shall drain it down to the dregs." Psalm 75:8, ESV

Isaiah calls it "the cup of his wrath" and "the cup of staggering" (Isaiah 51:17). Jeremiah is handed "this cup of the wine of wrath" and told to make the nations drink it (Jeremiah 25:15). Habakkuk, Lamentations, and Ezekiel use the same picture. When Jesus says "this cup," a Jew who knew the prophets knew exactly what was in it, and it was not Roman hardware. MacArthur puts it as plainly as it can be put: "His anguish had nothing to do with fear of men or the physical torments of the cross. He was sorrowful because within hours the full cup of divine fury against sin would be his to drink."

So the comparison with the martyrs collapses, because the martyrs never drank this. They died with the favor of God resting on them. He died under the wrath of God resting on him, in the place of others, which is what the blood sacrifices sealing every covenant have pointed at since Sinai. The cry from the cross, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (27:46), is the same cup at the bottom. His recoil in the garden is not less courage than a martyr's. It is a clearer sight of what he is about to swallow.

And then the hinge of the whole passage: "nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will." This is not the divine Son arguing with the Father; the will of God is one. This is a real human will, with real human horror in it, laid down. MacArthur says it well: it "graphically reveals how Christ in his humanity voluntarily surrendered his will to the will of the Father in all things." Everything Adam lost in a garden by saying "my will," Christ recovers in a garden by saying "yours."

Now compare the two prayers word by word, because Matthew wants you to.

"My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will." ... "My Father, if this cannot pass unless I drink it, your will be done." Matthew 26:39, 42, ESV

The first asks whether there is another way. The second assumes there is not. Something happened in between: the question was answered, and the answer was no. That "no" from the Father to the prayer of his sinless Son is the reason there is a "yes" available to you. There was no other way, and he took the way there was. Then he prays it a third time "saying the same words again," which tells you that settled obedience still needs repeating, and that praying the same thing more than once is not weak faith.

Why not the angels?

Three times he comes back and finds them asleep, and the rebuke is astonishingly gentle: "Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation. The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak" (v. 41). He is in agony, and he still diagnoses them kindly. He is not excusing them; he is telling them the truth about the same body he is wearing. Peter is about to prove the sentence exactly.

Then the torches arrive, and Judas kisses him, and Jesus says, "Friend, do what you came to do" (v. 50). The Greek behind "friend" is not the warm word for a close friend but a word closer to "comrade"; it is neither an insult nor an embrace, and it is the last thing Jesus ever says to him. When the sword comes out, Jesus gives three reasons to put it back, and they run from the general to the specific:

"Put your sword back into its place. For all who take the sword will perish by the sword. Do you think that I cannot appeal to my Father, and he will at once send me more than twelve legions of angels? But how then should the Scriptures be fulfilled, that it must be so?" Matthew 26:52-54, ESV

First, the principle. Peter has just made himself a vigilante. Whatever the injustice, private men do not get to wield the sword; that is God's grant to the civil authority (Genesis 9:6; Romans 13:4). A right cause does not turn a private sword into a legal one.

Second, the arithmetic. A Roman legion was about 6,000 men, so twelve legions is more than 72,000 angels, and one angel once killed 185,000 men in a night (2 Kings 19:35). The cohort holding the torches is not the strongest force in that garden by an unimaginable margin. Jesus is not overpowered. He is unresisting, which is a different thing entirely.

Third, the "must." "How then should the Scriptures be fulfilled, that it must be so?" This is the reason under the other two. Notice what Matthew has been doing all book: the fulfillment formula has appeared over and over, and here it becomes the argument for standing still while men tie your hands. He restates it to the crowd: "Day after day I sat in the temple teaching, and you did not seize me. But all this has taken place that the Scriptures of the prophets might be fulfilled" (vv. 55-56). They needed darkness, a traitor, and clubs to take a man who had been sitting in public every day that week. The arrest is a confession of their own cowardice, and it is still exactly on script.

Then the last line: "Then all the disciples left him and fled." Matthew does not soften it and does not moralize. Verse 31 is now history. The shepherd is struck and the sheep scatter, on schedule, and the only one left standing in the garden is the one who told them it would happen and who had already booked the meeting in Galilee.

Application

  • About God: the Father did not spare him. The cup did not pass, the angels were not sent, and the striking was God's own. Every ounce of comfort you have in Christ was bought by a "no" spoken to the prayer of the only man who ever deserved a "yes."
  • About you: you are Peter. Your loyalty is sincere and your flesh is weak, and both are true at once. The remedy Jesus gives is not "try harder"; it is "watch and pray," which is exactly what the disciples slept through and he did not.
  • Do: pray one honest prayer today in the shape of verse 39. Name the thing you actually want God to remove, out loud and without editing it into something more spiritual, and then say the second half and mean it: "nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will." If you cannot mean it yet, pray it a second time, and a third, as he did.

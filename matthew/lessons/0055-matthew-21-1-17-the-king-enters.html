Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Jesus arranges this (vv. 1-3). He sends two disciples ahead with detailed instructions: the village, the tied donkey, the colt with her, the words to say if challenged. Nothing here is spontaneous. For nearly twenty chapters he has told healed people and even his own disciples to stay quiet about who he is (8:4, 9:30, 12:16, 16:20, 17:9). Now he stages a parade.
  • The title he gives himself (v. 3). "The Lord needs them." It is the only self-designation in the errand, and it is enough to release the animals.
  • Matthew stops the story to quote (vv. 4-5). "This took place to fulfill what was spoken by the prophet." The quotation is Zechariah 9:9 with an opening line from Isaiah 62:11, and its center is one word about the king: humble.
  • Two animals, and a plural pronoun (v. 7). "They brought the donkey and the colt and put on them their cloaks, and he sat on them." Matthew alone mentions the mother. This is a real question in the text and we will handle it below rather than skip it.
  • The crowd's actions are royal, not merely enthusiastic (v. 8). Cloaks on the road and cut branches on the road. Spreading garments under a man was the homage given to a king at his accession (2 Kings 9:13).
  • The shout is quoted Scripture (v. 9). "Hosanna to the Son of David! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord! Hosanna in the highest!" That is Psalm 118:25-26, and the crowd applies it to Jesus by name of office: Son of David.
  • The city asks and the crowd downgrades (vv. 10-11). "The whole city was stirred up, saying, 'Who is this?'" Answer: "This is the prophet Jesus, from Nazareth of Galilee." True, and far below what they were shouting one verse earlier.
  • In the temple he acts, quotes, heals, and answers (vv. 12-16). Overturned tables, overturned seats, two Old Testament texts fused into one sentence, then the blind and the lame coming to him in the temple, then children shouting, then indignant chief priests, then a line from Psalm 8.

Interpretation

1. Why does the silence end here, and why a donkey?

Through the whole ministry Jesus has suppressed publicity, because a public messianic claim before the appointed time would have collided with a popular movement that wanted a general, not a Savior (John 6:15). Now the Passover crowds are pouring into Jerusalem, and he does the one thing he has refused to do: he makes an unmistakable public claim. The reason is timing. The hour has come, and the claim is made in the week it will be answered with a cross.

"Say to the daughter of Zion, 'Behold, your king is coming to you, humble, and mounted on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a beast of burden.'" Matthew 21:5, ESV

Now the animal. In the ancient world the mount was the message. A commander returning from conquest rode a warhorse; a king arriving in peace, to be received rather than to subdue, rode a donkey (see 2 Sam. 16:2, 1 Kings 1:33, where Solomon rides David's mule to his own coronation). Jesus is not being modest about his kingship; he is being precise about it. He claims the throne and specifies the terms in the same gesture: this King comes to save, not yet to judge. MacArthur notes that the exactness of the Zechariah fulfillment "would not have escaped the Jewish multitudes" (MacArthur on chapter 21, note on 21:5). Keep the whole of Zechariah 9 in view: the same chapter that puts the king on a donkey has him cutting off the war horse and speaking peace to the nations, with dominion "to the ends of the earth" (Zech. 9:10). Revelation 19:11 will put him on a white horse. Same King, different errand, and this is the errand of the donkey.

The hard question: how many animals?
Verse 7 says they put cloaks "on them" and "he sat on them," and Mark, Luke, and John mention only the colt. Skeptics have used this for centuries to claim Matthew misread Hebrew poetry and invented a second animal. Three things answer it honestly. First, Zechariah 9:9 uses Hebrew parallelism, where the second line restates the first in other words, so "a donkey" and "a colt, the foal of a beast of burden" name one animal twice. Matthew quotes the couplet in full, as any Jewish writer would, without implying two riders. Second, the colt was unridden (Mark 11:2, Luke 19:30); an unbroken young animal is far calmer beside its mother, so bringing her along is exactly what you would expect, and Matthew, writing for readers who knew animals, simply reports what happened. Third, on the plural "he sat on them," the nearest plural noun in the sentence is the cloaks, and MacArthur takes it that way: "he sat. I.e., on the clothes" (note on 21:7). Matthew is not confused; he is more detailed than the others, which is what an eyewitness tends to be.

2. What are the crowds actually saying, and do they mean it?

"Hosanna to the Son of David! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord! Hosanna in the highest!" Matthew 21:9, ESV

Hosanna is a Hebrew word carried straight over into Greek, and it is not a cheer. It means save now, and it comes from Psalm 118:25, one of the psalms sung at Passover. So the crowd is praying while it celebrates: save us, right now, Son of David. They have the office right. They have the Scripture right. What they do not have is what he came to save them from. Five days later a Jerusalem crowd will shout "Let him be crucified" (27:22). Whether these are the same individuals matters less than what Matthew shows: correct words about Jesus are not the same as submission to him.

Watch verses 10-11 carefully. The city asks "Who is this?" and the answer that comes back is "the prophet Jesus, from Nazareth of Galilee." Every word true. A prophet is a high office, and he is one. But the crowd has just quoted a royal psalm and used a royal title, and now, asked directly, it settles for the smaller answer. That instinct is still with us. It is easy to say true and admiring things about Jesus that stop short of "my Lord and my God."

3. What was wrong with the temple trade, and how sharp is the warning?

"It is written, 'My house shall be called a house of prayer,' but you make it a den of robbers." Matthew 21:13, ESV

Start with the practical situation, because the abuse is easy to misstate. Pilgrims travelled from all over the empire and could not bring livestock; they needed to buy an animal on arrival, and it had to pass priestly inspection. The temple tax had to be paid in approved coinage, so foreign money had to be exchanged. None of that is wrong in itself. Two things were wrong. The rates: MacArthur observes that merchants and money changers "were charging such excessive rates that the temple marketplace took on the atmosphere of a thieves' den" (note on 21:12), with the high-priestly family profiting from it (note on 26:3). Worshippers arriving to meet God were being fleeced at the door by God's own officials. And the place: this trade filled the court of the Gentiles, several acres of the temple mount, and the only part of the complex where a non-Jew was permitted to pray. The one square foot of Israel's worship set aside for the nations had been turned into a stockyard and a currency market.

That is why the quotation Jesus picks is Isaiah 56:7, and why the part he leaves hanging matters. Isaiah's full line is "my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples," in a passage about foreigners who join themselves to the LORD. They had monetized the Gentiles' prayer room. Then he fuses in Jeremiah 7:11, "den of robbers." Look up Jeremiah 7 before you decide how severe this is. It is the Temple Sermon, where the LORD tells a generation chanting "the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD" that he will do to this house what he did to Shiloh, and Shiloh was destroyed. So verse 13 is not only a rebuke of dishonest trading; it is a prophetic notice served on the building. Three chapters later Jesus says it out loud: "there will not be left here one stone upon another" (24:2). It happened in AD 70.

Note also whose authority is being asserted. He calls the temple "my house." Only one Person in Scripture gets to say that.

"And the blind and the lame came to him in the temple, and he healed them." Matthew 21:14, ESV

Do not read past that verse. In 2 Samuel 5:8, tied to David's capture of Jerusalem, we get the saying "The blind and the lame shall not come into the house," and by Jesus' day the disabled were kept at the margins of temple life. Jesus clears out the commerce and the first thing that fills the emptied space is exactly the people the space had excluded, being healed by the Son of David inside his Father's house. He does not merely stop the wrong use; he shows the right one.

Then the children take up the same shout, "Hosanna to the Son of David," and the chief priests are indignant, which tells you they understood the title perfectly. Jesus answers with Psalm 8:2: "Out of the mouth of infants and nursing babies you have prepared praise." Read the psalm. The praise there is prepared for the LORD. Jesus quotes it to defend children praising him, and does not correct a syllable of what they are singing. As MacArthur puts it, the reply "amounted to an inescapable assertion of his deity" (note on 21:16). Children got it; the professionals were offended.

Application

  • About God He announces his kingship on a borrowed donkey and defends it by pointing at children. Real authority does not need to look impressive, and it does not flinch from overturning tables when worship is being sold.
  • About you The crowd said true things loudly and then gave a smaller answer when asked directly. Where do your words about Jesus outrun your actual submission to him, and where do you settle for calling him a good teacher rather than your King?
  • Do Today, pray the crowd's word honestly: name one specific thing you need saved from (a temper, a habit, a fear, a grudge) and pray "Hosanna, save now" about that one thing by name. Then do the temple half: find one thing in your own week that is crowding out prayer and clear it out today, not next month.

In your local MacArthur notes copy, read the notes on 21:7, 21:9, 21:12, and 21:16 together. On 21:7 he gives the "he sat on the clothes" reading of the plural; on 21:12 he explains the money changers, the excessive rates, and the court of the Gentiles; on 21:16 he draws the deity conclusion from Psalm 8. Note that his 21:9 dating (Monday, 10 Nisan, AD 30, tied to Daniel 9) reflects his dispensational timetable for Daniel's seventy weeks; the fulfillment of Zechariah 9:9 does not depend on that calculation, so weigh the date and the fulfillment separately. Church prompt
The abuse Jesus attacked was commerce crowding out prayer in the one space reserved for outsiders. Ask someone at church this week: is there anything in how we run our life together that quietly makes it harder for an outsider to come and pray? Not a hunt for scandal, just an honest look at the doorway. Ask also who our "blind and lame" are, the people our arrangements accidentally keep at the edge.

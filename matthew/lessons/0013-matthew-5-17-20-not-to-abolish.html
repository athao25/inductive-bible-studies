Matthew 5:17-20: Not to Abolish

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • He answers a charge nobody has made yet (v. 17). "Do not think that I have come to abolish." People were going to think it. The Beatitudes and the antitheses that follow sound new enough that Jesus heads off the accusation before it lands.
  • "The Law or the Prophets" is a whole-Bible phrase (v. 17). In first-century Jewish speech that pairing was a standard way of naming the entire Old Testament, the same way we might say "Genesis to Malachi." He is not talking about the ten commandments only, and he is not talking about rabbinic tradition at all.
  • "I have come" is a claim about himself (v. 17). Twice in one verse. Prophets said "thus says the LORD"; this speaker talks about the purpose of his own arrival, and the purpose is the destiny of the Scriptures.
  • Not abolish, but fulfill: the verse turns on one contrast (v. 17). Whatever "fulfill" means, it is the opposite of "abolish," and Matthew has already used that word family seven times for prophecy coming true (1:22; 2:15, 17, 23; 4:14, and more).
  • Verse 18 sets two time markers, not one. "Until heaven and earth pass away" and "until all is accomplished." Notice they are not obviously the same date. Underline both; the whole debate lives here.
  • The smallest possible units (v. 18). Not a book, not a chapter, not a command: an iota and a dot. The iota is the smallest Hebrew letter (yodh); the dot is a tiny stroke that distinguishes one letter from a lookalike.
  • Verse 19 turns from Jesus to the disciple, and both parties stay in (v. 19). The one who relaxes commands and teaches others to do so "will be called least in the kingdom of heaven"; the doer and teacher "will be called great." Least in the kingdom is still in the kingdom. Verse 19 is about rank.
  • Verse 20 is not about rank (v. 20). "You will never enter the kingdom of heaven." The sanction jumps from demotion to exclusion, and the bar is set at the most religiously meticulous men alive.
  • The little word "for" chains all four verses (vv. 18, 20). Thesis (17), because Scripture is unbreakable (18), therefore treat every command as weighty (19), because the required righteousness is higher than you think (20). It is one argument, not four sayings.

Interpretation

What does "fulfill" actually mean?

"Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them." Matthew 5:17, ESV

Three senses have been proposed, and the honest answer is that the text supports all three at once. First, he obeys it perfectly. He is the only person who ever kept the law without a gap, which is why he can be baptized "to fulfill all righteousness" (3:15) and why Paul can say he was "born under the law" (Galatians 4:4). Second, he brings out its full intended meaning. That is exactly what verses 21-48 proceed to do: not "you have heard, but Moses was wrong," but "you have heard, and here is what it always required, down to the heart." Third, he is the goal the law was pointing at. The sacrifices, the priesthood, the temple, the Passover lamb are types, pictures with a reality attached, and the reality is Christ (Hebrews 10:1; Colossians 2:16-17). A photograph is not abolished when the person walks into the room.

MacArthur on chapter 5 reads it the same layered way: "He fulfilled the moral law by keeping it perfectly. He fulfilled the ceremonial law by being the embodiment of everything the law's types and symbols pointed to. And he fulfilled the judicial law by personifying God's perfect justice." Note what is not on the list: replacing it. Jesus did not come to start a religion; he came to finish a story.

How long is "until all is accomplished"?

"For truly, I say to you, until heaven and earth pass away, not an iota, not a dot, will pass from the Law until all is accomplished." Matthew 5:18, ESV

Be honest: this verse is debated, and the debate is not between believers and skeptics but among people who all hold Scripture to be inerrant. Two readings are live. (1) Both clauses mean the same thing, the end of the present created order, so the entire Old Testament stands in force in some sense until the last day. (2) The second clause qualifies the first: Scripture holds until everything it points to is accomplished, and Matthew uses that language for the cross and the vindication that follows. On this reading nothing is erased, but a fulfilled promise now functions differently, the way a paid mortgage is still a real document you no longer make payments on. A third variation ties "all is accomplished" to AD 70, when the temple and its whole sacrificial apparatus were destroyed and the old administration visibly ended.

What all readings share is verse 18's actual assertion, and you should not lose it in the argument: the Old Testament is God's word down to the accent marks. MacArthur states it well, that Christ is "affirming the utter inerrancy and absolute authority of the OT as the word of God". Verse 19 then draws the practical conclusion: a disciple who shrugs at "one of the least of these commandments" and teaches others to shrug has not graduated past the law, he has misread his King.

Fork #1: what happens to God's law now?

This is the first of five places where our teachers genuinely diverge. Read all three sympathetically; each is trying to obey verse 17.

Theonomic and postmillennial (Durbin, our home lens). God's law expresses God's unchanging character, so its standards of justice remain the standard for persons and for nations, with the ceremonial shadows fulfilled in Christ. Strongest point: verse 18's "not an iota, not a dot" is aggressively conservative language, verse 19 attaches a sanction to relaxing even the least commandment, and nothing in the passage hints that most of the Old Testament is about to become optional. If Christ's reign advances until the nations are discipled (28:19-20), they are discipled to something, and Scripture is the only law God has published.

Classic Reformed and covenantal (White). The historic threefold division sorts the Mosaic law into moral (the Decalogue, rooted in God's character, permanently binding), ceremonial (sacrifice, priesthood, food and purity laws, fulfilled and therefore expired in Christ), and civil or judicial (Israel's case law as a nation, expired with that nation though its equity still instructs). Strongest point: the New Testament plainly treats categories differently, declaring all foods clean (Mark 7:19) and calling the food and festival rules a shadow (Colossians 2:16-17) while still quoting the commandments as binding (Romans 13:9). The division is a summary of that observed pattern, not an arbitrary grid.

Dispensational (MacArthur). The Mosaic covenant as an administration ended at the cross; believers are under "the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2) rather than under Moses, and the Sermon functions primarily as an exposition of the kingdom's righteousness that exposes human inability. Strongest point: Scripture itself says the old covenant is "obsolete" and "ready to vanish away" (Hebrews 8:13), and Galatians treats the law as a guardian whose term has finished (Galatians 3:24-25). MacArthur is no antinomian; he insists the moral content abides. His system simply locates its authority in Christ's own commands rather than in Sinai's continuing jurisdiction.

Where they agree, which is most of it: Christ kept the law perfectly, no sinner is justified by law-keeping, the Old Testament is inerrant and profitable, and the ceremonial system is finished because its substance has come. The live question is how much of Israel's civil order remains a model for nations today. That question is worth studying for years; do not let it distract you from verse 20, which has your name on it either way.

How can my righteousness exceed the Pharisees'?

"For I tell you, unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven." Matthew 5:20, ESV

Feel the force of this before you soften it. The scribes were professional students of Scripture; the Pharisees tithed their garden herbs and fasted twice a week. If righteousness is a ledger of external deeds, these men were unbeatable, and Jesus has just told a crowd of fishermen and farmers that they must beat them. As an accounting problem, this is hopeless, and it is meant to be.

So the surplus cannot be quantity; it has to be kind. The rest of the Sermon spends two and a half chapters proving it. Pharisaic righteousness stopped at the hands, so Jesus goes to the heart: no murder becomes no contemptuous anger (5:21-22), no adultery becomes no lust (5:27-28), giving and praying and fasting are stripped of their audience (6:1-18), and the standard is finally stated at full height in 5:48. Nobody clears that bar. Which is precisely how the law does its proper work, closing every avenue of self-merit and leaving the sinner with nothing but grace. The righteousness that exceeds the Pharisees' is not a better performance; it is a righteousness given, Christ's own record credited to those who believe (Romans 4:5; Philippians 3:9), and then worked outward into an obedience that starts in the heart. Note the order carefully: received first, lived second. Reverse it and you have rebuilt Pharisaism with Christian vocabulary.

Application

  • About God He does not change his mind about his own word. Every command he ever gave still reflects who he is, and every promise he ever made lands on Christ. Your Bible is one book with one Author and one subject.
  • About you You are more likely to relax a commandment than to abolish one (v. 19). Relaxing sounds reasonable; it usually sounds like "that part is cultural" or "God understands my situation." Ask which commandment you have quietly downgraded, and whether you have taught anyone else to downgrade it.
  • Do Today, name the one commandment you have been relaxing. Write it on paper with the specific way you bend it. Then do the opposite thing once, before bedtime: make the apology, delete the thing, keep the promise, tell the truth you have been shading. One act, today, not a resolution for next month.

Two things to look for: the threefold moral/ceremonial/judicial sorting in the note on 5:17, which is the divide the fork card above turns on, and the two-sided note on 5:20 (a call to deeper holiness, and an impossible barrier to works-salvation). Church prompt
Ask a pastor or elder this week how your church understands the Old Testament law for Christians today, and whether the threefold division is taught there. It is a fair, friendly question and the answer will tell you a great deal about how your church reads its Bible. Listen for what they do with the fourth commandment in particular; that is where the categories get tested in practice.

Questions welcome, always: "if the law is fulfilled, why do we ignore the food laws?", "what is the difference between abolishing and relaxing?", "is 'until all is accomplished' the cross or the last day?" Ask your teacher.

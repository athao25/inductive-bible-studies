Matthew 17:14-27: Faith and the Temple Tax

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 17:14-27 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If not, stop here. Read Matthew 17:14-27 in your ESV, twice. Write at least five observations: what the father actually says about his son, what Jesus calls the generation, what question the disciples ask privately, how the disciples react to the death prediction this time, and every step of the strange errand Jesus gives Peter at the end. Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The location changes hard (v. 14). "And when they came to the crowd." Six verses ago Jesus was shining on a mountain with Moses and Elijah. Now he is at the bottom of the hill with a desperate father and disciples who have just failed publicly. Matthew sets glory and failure back to back on purpose.
  • The father kneels and describes symptoms, not theology (vv. 14-15). "He has seizures and he suffers terribly. For often he falls into the fire, and often into the water." A parent's report: what the boy does, how much it costs him, how near death it keeps him.
  • The complaint is against the disciples (v. 16). "I brought him to your disciples, and they could not heal him." The failure is public and already known to the crowd.
  • Jesus' lament is aimed wide (v. 17). "O faithless and twisted generation, how long am I to be with you? How long am I to bear with you?" Two "how long" questions in a row. This is grief, not cool instruction.
  • The healing itself takes one sentence (v. 18). Jesus rebuked the demon, it came out, the boy was healed instantly. Matthew spends far more words on the disciples' failure than on the cure.
  • The disciples ask privately (v. 19). "Why could we not cast it out?" They are genuinely surprised. In chapter 10 they were sent out and did this very thing.
  • The second death prediction lands differently (vv. 22-23). Delivered, killed, raised on the third day, and this time "they were greatly distressed." In 16:22 Peter argued. Now nobody argues. Something is getting through.
  • The tax scene is all conversation until the last line (vv. 24-27). A question to Peter, Peter's quick "Yes," Jesus speaking "to him first" before Peter can raise it, an argument about kings and sons, and then one flat instruction about a hook, a fish, and a coin.

Interpretation

1. What does "faith like a grain of mustard seed" actually promise?

"Because of your little faith. For truly, I say to you, if you have faith like a grain of mustard seed, you will say to this mountain, 'Move from here to there,' and it will move, and nothing will be impossible for you." Matthew 17:20, ESV

Read the two halves together or you will misread both. Jesus diagnoses "little faith" and then prescribes faith the size of the smallest seed in a Galilean farmer's hand. If the cure for small faith were bigger faith, the mustard seed is a strange thing to hold up. The seed is a picture of smallness. So the problem was not that their faith was insufficiently large; it was that it was pointed at the wrong thing. MacArthur puts it exactly here: their failure "probably lay in a failure to make God, rather than their own gifts, the object of their confidence" (MacArthur on chapter 17, note on 17:19). They had done this before. They had a track record. Somewhere between chapter 10 and this valley, the track record became the thing they trusted.

"Moving mountains" was a stock Jewish idiom for accomplishing what looks impossible; rabbis who solved hard problems were called mountain-movers. Jesus is not issuing a promise about geology. He is saying that the smallest real trust in the living God reaches things that no amount of self-confidence reaches.

Guard rail: this is not a formula
Verse 20 is regularly quoted to mean that if you believe hard enough, and stop doubting, you get the outcome you want, and that a failed outcome proves you did not believe hard enough. That reading turns faith into a currency you pay God with, and it crushes suffering people. Two things rule it out. First, the mustard seed makes quantity the wrong axis entirely. Second, Jesus assumes what 1 John 5:14 says out loud, that we ask "according to his will." Faith is trust in a Person who is free, wise, and good, not leverage over him. The disciples' problem was self-reliance, and self-reliance dressed up as intense believing is still self-reliance.

One textual note you may see in your margin: verse 21 ("But this kind never comes out except by prayer and fasting") appears in the ESV footnote rather than the text, because the earliest manuscripts do not contain it. Nothing in the passage depends on it.

2. Why does Matthew put the second passion prediction here?

"The Son of Man is about to be delivered into the hands of men, and they will kill him, and he will be raised on the third day." And they were greatly distressed. Matthew 17:22-23, ESV

The Son of Man who was just blazing with glory on the mountain is now the one "delivered into the hands of men." "Delivered" is a passive with God behind it and Judas in front of it. Note the change in the disciples: the first time (16:21-22) Peter took Jesus aside and rebuked him. This time there is no argument, only grief. That is progress, of a painful kind. They still do not hear "raised on the third day," which is the part that should have produced hope, but they have stopped telling him he is wrong. Sorrow that submits is further along than confidence that corrects.

3. Why does the Son pay a tax he does not owe?

The two-drachma tax was the half-shekel of Exodus 30:11-16, an annual levy on every Israelite male over twenty for the upkeep of the sanctuary (see also 2 Chron. 24:9). It was roughly two days' wages, and it funded the temple: God's house.

"What do you think, Simon? From whom do kings of the earth take toll or tax? From their sons or from others?" And when he said, "From others," Jesus said to him, "Then the sons are free." Matthew 17:25-26, ESV

Peter had already blurted "Yes" outside. Inside, Jesus gets to him "first," before Peter can even raise it, and reasons with him instead of correcting him. The logic is short: kings tax other people's households, not their own family. Now feel the size of the claim. The tax funds this temple, and this temple belongs to his Father, and he is the Son. He is not a subject of that house. He owns it. This is the same voice that said "something greater than the temple is here" (12:6), made concrete in a two-drachma coin.

"However, not to give offense to them, go to the sea and cast a hook and take the first fish that comes up, and when you open its mouth you will find a shekel. Take that and give it to them for yourself and for me." Matthew 17:27, ESV

Then he pays. Not because he owes it, but "not to give offense." He proves the right and waives it in the same breath. This is one of the clearest pictures in the Gospels of a principle the apostles later teach at length: a genuine freedom can be voluntarily surrendered so that no one is needlessly tripped up (1 Cor. 8:9-13; 9:19; Rom. 14:13-21), and believers submit to lawful authorities rather than making an issue of their liberty (Rom. 13:1-7; 1 Pet. 2:13-17), a set of cross-references MacArthur also gathers at 17:24. Note carefully what is not happening: Jesus is not conceding that the claim on him is just. He is declining to make his freedom a stumbling block.

And notice the coin. One shekel, exactly the amount for two men, in the mouth of the first fish Peter catches. It is the only miracle of this kind in the Gospels, and it has a light touch to it: the Lord of creation, who could have opened a purse, sends a fisherman fishing and pays the temple's bill out of the sea he made. The King is not poor. He is unbothered.

Application

  • About God The one who owes nothing pays anyway. His glory on the mountain and his gentleness with a failing father and failing disciples are the same character, six verses apart.
  • About you The disciples' failure came from trusting a past success rather than a present God. Where are you running on your own track record, in prayer, in work, in patience with your family?
  • Do Today, name one right you actually have (being owed an apology, being right in an argument, a preference you could insist on) and lay it down for someone's sake without announcing it. Then pray one honest prayer for something impossible to you, aimed at God rather than at your own certainty.

Church prompt
Verse 27 is a rare, concrete case of surrendering a real right so as not to trip anyone up. Ask someone at church this week: where in our congregation's life do we insist on being right when laying it down would cost us little and serve someone a lot? Distinguish carefully between waiving a preference (which this passage commends) and waiving the truth of the gospel (which Paul refused to do, Gal. 2:5).

Lesson 0007 · Unit 1: Chapters 1-4

Matthew 3:1-12: The Voice in the Wilderness

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 3:1-12 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If not, stop here. Read Matthew 3:1-12 in your ESV, twice, and write at least five observations: what John wears, where he stands, what he says first, who comes out to him, and what he says will happen to the trees and to the chaff. Then come back. The lesson checks your list before it explains anything.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. From the last two lessons, without looking:

Mind the gap

Between the last word of chapter 2 and the first word of chapter 3, roughly thirty years pass. Matthew left Jesus as a child in Nazareth; he picks him up as a grown man walking to the Jordan (3:13). Luke fills in one boyhood scene (Luke 2:41-52) and nothing else. That silence is deliberate. Matthew is not writing a biography of a life; he is building a case about a King, and he skips whatever does not serve the case. Notice too that "in those days" (3:1) is a vague Hebrew-style time marker, not a claim that these events follow immediately.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Location, verse 1. John preaches "in the wilderness of Judea", the barren country west of the Dead Sea, about a day's walk from Jerusalem. The herald of a king announces him in a desert, not a palace or a temple court.
  • His opening sentence, verse 2. "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand." Two clauses: a command and a reason. Underline it, because Jesus will preach the identical sentence as his own first sermon in 4:17.
  • The quotation, verse 3. Matthew stops the narrative to cite Isaiah 40:3. John is not merely a preacher; he is a prophecy walking around.
  • His clothing and food, verse 4. Camel's hair, a leather belt, locusts, wild honey. Matthew records the wardrobe of no other character in the Gospel. If a detail this odd is included, it is doing work.
  • The crowds and the confession, verses 5-6. Jerusalem, all Judea, and the whole Jordan region come out, and they are baptized "confessing their sins". Confession is spoken; the sins are named, not felt in general.
  • The change of address, verse 7. The tone flips the moment the Pharisees and Sadducees arrive. To the crowds, preaching. To the leaders, "You brood of vipers!"
  • Fruit language runs through it, verses 8, 10, 12. Bear fruit, trees that bear no good fruit, wheat and chaff. Three images, one point: what is produced reveals what is real.
  • Three baptisms named, verse 11. Water (John's, now), Holy Spirit and fire (the Coming One's). John names his own inferiority twice: mightier than I, sandals I am not worthy to carry.

Interpretation

1. Why is the wardrobe in the Bible?

Now John wore a garment of camel's hair and a leather belt around his waist, and his food was locusts and wild honey. Matthew 3:4, ESV

Go find the cross-reference. When Israel's king asked what the prophet who had rebuked him looked like, the answer was: "He wore a garment of hair, with a belt of leather about his waist." And the king said, "It is Elijah the Tishbite" (2 Kings 1:8). The outfit was an identification. Malachi then closed the Old Testament with a promise: "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and awesome day of the LORD comes" (Mal 4:5). So John walks out of the desert dressed as the answer to the last sentence Israel had heard from God, four hundred years earlier. Jesus makes the connection explicit later: "he is Elijah who is to come" (11:14). Not Elijah reincarnated, but Elijah's office filled, in his spirit and power (Luke 1:17). MacArthur on chapter 3 notes the same link and adds that the locusts were a permitted food (Lev 11:22), so John is austere, not lawless.

2. What does "repent" actually mean?

This is the most misdefined word in the passage, so define it once and keep it. The Greek behind it means to change one's mind, and that has led many to flatten it into "an attitude adjustment". John will not allow that. He demands proof:

You brood of vipers! Who warned you to flee from the wrath to come? Bear fruit in keeping with repentance. Matthew 3:7-8, ESV

Repentance is a radical turning from sin that shows up in the fruit of a changed life. It is not regret (Judas had regret, 27:3-5). It is not a private mental note. And it is not itself a work you perform to earn anything; the works are its evidence, the way fruit is evidence of a living tree, not the cause of it. Repentance and faith are two sides of one turn: turning from sin, turning to God. Note the shape of verse 8 carefully. John does not say "produce fruit so that you may repent". He says produce fruit that matches the repentance you claim.

And the reason attached to the command is not "because you will feel better". It is "for the kingdom of heaven is at hand". The King is arriving; get off the road or get on the right side of it. That the identical sentence becomes Jesus' own first sermon (4:17) tells you John's message and Jesus' message are one message.

3. Why was John's baptism an insult, and why did anyone accept it?

Here is the detail most readers walk past. Ritual washings were ordinary in Israel (Lev 15:13), but a one-time baptism marking entry into the covenant people was administered to Gentile converts, outsiders coming in. John pointed that rite at Jews. Every Israelite who waded into the Jordan was publicly conceding: my ancestry has not saved me, I stand where a pagan stands, I need to be brought in. Given first-century Jewish feeling about Gentiles, this was an astonishing thing to submit to, and the crowds submitted in numbers.

Which is exactly what the leaders would not do:

And do not presume to say to yourselves, "We have Abraham as our father," for I tell you, God is able from these stones to raise up children for Abraham. Matthew 3:9, ESV

Two groups had come out. The Pharisees were a small, rigorous sect (roughly six thousand), separatists whose name means "separated ones", famous for scrupulous obedience to the fine points of the law and its added human traditions. The Sadducees were the opposite number: wealthy, aristocratic, priestly, temple-controlling rationalists who rejected tradition, accepted the authority of the Pentateuch above the rest, and denied the resurrection and the existence of angels (22:23; Acts 23:8). Ritualists against rationalists, legalists against liberals; they agreed on almost nothing. They will agree on killing Jesus. Watch them appear side by side against him again in chapter 22.

John's answer to their pedigree is a pun (in Aramaic, "sons" and "stones" sound alike) and a bombshell: God can manufacture Abrahamic children out of roadside rubble. Descent guarantees nothing. Paul later says the same thing plainly: "those of faith are the sons of Abraham" (Gal 3:7). If you were raised in church, hear this verse in your own accent. Christian parents, a Christian upbringing, and a familiar pew are the modern "we have Abraham as our father".

4. Water, Spirit, and fire: what is the fire?

I baptize you with water for repentance, but he who is coming after me is mightier than I, whose sandals I am not worthy to carry. He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and with fire. Matthew 3:11, ESV

Some read "fire" as a warm image of purifying zeal, or as the tongues of flame at Pentecost. Let the immediate context decide, since that is what context is for. Fire appears twice more within three verses, and both times it burns something up: "every tree therefore that does not bear good fruit is cut down and thrown into the fire" (v. 10), and "the chaff he will burn with unquenchable fire" (v. 12). In this paragraph fire means judgment. So verse 11 sets out a division: the Coming One immerses his people in the Spirit and the unrepentant in wrath. Verse 12 draws the same line with a farm tool. A winnowing fork tosses threshed grain into the wind; the heavy kernels drop, the light husks blow away. The wheat is gathered, the chaff is burned. One threshing floor, one man working it, two destinies.

"The axe is laid to the root of the trees" (v. 10) presses the same urgency. Not the branches, the root, and not next year, "even now". This is a warning that arrives before the blow, which is mercy.

A note on "at hand"
MacArthur on 3:2 grants that the kingdom is "a present reality" now ruling in believers' hearts, but adds that "one day it will be established in a literal earthly kingdom (Rev. 20:4-6)". That last clause is his futurist premillennialism supplying content the verse itself does not carry; John says only that the reign is arriving, and it arrives in the person of the King. Our home postmillennial reading takes "at hand" at full strength: the kingdom genuinely broke in with Christ and advances now through the gospel until "the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD" (Isa 11:9), with his bodily return still ahead. Amillennial readers agree the kingdom is present and reject the earthly millennium, but expect no such visible triumph before the end. Nothing in Matthew 3 settles that argument, so hold it loosely here; the fork passages come later. What all three agree on is what John actually preached: repent, because the King is here.

Application

  • About God. He warns before he judges, and he sends the warning by a man in camel hair to a desert nobody had to visit. The axe is real and so is the sermon that precedes it. Both are the same God.
  • About you. Your family line, your church attendance, and your doctrinal accuracy are all "we have Abraham as our father" if there is no fruit. Ask the harder question John asks: not "do I believe the right things?" but "what has actually changed?"
  • Do. Today, name one specific sin out loud to God, the way the crowds confessed at the Jordan, and take one concrete step of turning from it before you sleep: delete the app, make the apology, cancel the subscription, tell one trusted believer. Repentance that costs nothing today is regret wearing a better name.

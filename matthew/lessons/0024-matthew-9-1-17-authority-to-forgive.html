Matthew 9:1-17: Authority to Forgive

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Jesus answers a request nobody made (v. 2). Men carry a paralyzed man to him on a bed. Jesus says, "Take heart, my son; your sins are forgiven." Not one word about the paralysis yet. The presenting problem is the legs; the first word addresses something else entirely.
  • "Seeing their faith" is plural (v. 2). The faith Jesus sees belongs to the group who did the carrying. Matthew never records the paralytic saying anything at all, before or after.
  • The objection is silent (v. 3). The scribes say "This man is blaspheming" only "to themselves." Nothing is spoken aloud, which makes the next verse a claim in itself.
  • Jesus reads the room from the inside (v. 4). "Knowing their thoughts, [he] said, 'Why do you think evil in your hearts?'" Before he heals anything he demonstrates that he knows unspoken thoughts, which Scripture reserves to God (1 Kings 8:39).
  • The healing is offered as evidence, and the sentence breaks (v. 6). "But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" is addressed to the scribes; then, mid-sentence, "he then said to the paralytic, 'Rise, pick up your bed and go home.'" The miracle is a proof exhibit for a claim about sin.
  • The crowd's praise has an odd wording (v. 8). They "glorified God, who had given such authority to men." Not "to this man." Hold that phrase; it matters later in this Gospel.
  • Matthew's own call gets one verse (v. 9). "He saw a man called Matthew sitting at the tax booth, and he said to him, 'Follow me.' And he rose and followed him." The author's entire conversion account is six words long: "And he rose and followed him."
  • The Pharisees do not ask Jesus (v. 11). They ask the disciples, "Why does your teacher eat with tax collectors and sinners?" Jesus overhears and answers anyway (v. 12). Compare v. 14, where John's disciples come to him directly.
  • Three sayings answer three complaints. Physician and sick (v. 12), bridegroom and wedding guests (v. 15), new cloth and new wineskins (vv. 16-17). Each time Jesus answers with a picture rather than an argument, and each picture reframes who he is.
  • A shadow falls across the wedding (v. 15). "The days will come when the bridegroom is taken away from them, and then they will fast." First hint in Matthew that the joy has an ending in view, and "taken away" is not a gentle verb.

Interpretation

Why forgive sins when they came about a paralysis?

And behold, some people brought to him a paralytic, lying on a bed. And when Jesus saw their faith he said to the paralytic, "Take heart, my son; your sins are forgiven." Matthew 9:2, ESV

Chapters 8 and 9 are building a case, and this is the escalation. Disease bowed to him (8:1-17). Wind and sea obeyed him (8:23-27). Demons begged him (8:28-34). Now the deepest disorder of all, the one under the paralysis, is addressed by a word. Jesus goes to the root because the root is the point; a healed body still dies, and a forgiven man does not stay dead.

The scribes' reaction is theologically correct. Only the one sinned against can pardon, and every sin is finally against God (Psalm 51:4), so a man announcing pardon is speaking in God's place. MacArthur on chapter 9 puts it exactly: this "would be a true judgment about anyone but God incarnate, for only the One who has been sinned against has the prerogative to forgive. Jesus' words to the man were therefore an unequivocal claim of divine authority." Notice what that does to the options. Jesus has not been misunderstood, and he does not soften the claim. The only remaining question in the room is who he is.

"For which is easier, to say, 'Your sins are forgiven,' or to say, 'Rise and walk'? But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins"—he then said to the paralytic—"Rise, pick up your bed and go home." Matthew 9:5-6, ESV

The logic is sharper than it first looks. Easier to say is the whole trick. Anyone can say "your sins are forgiven," because nothing visible happens and no one can check. "Rise and walk" is instantly falsifiable; if the man lies there, the speaker is exposed. So Jesus performs the checkable miracle as public proof of the uncheckable one. MacArthur adds a second layer worth keeping: the forgiving "was in reality the more difficult task, however, because it ultimately required him to sacrifice his life." The word was free to say and cost him everything to make good. On the title he chooses here, Son of Man, remember it is not merely a humble self-description; it reaches back to Daniel 7:13-14, where one like a son of man receives everlasting dominion.

Then verse 8: the crowds "glorified God, who had given such authority to men." They got the awe right and the category not quite. They filed it as God empowering a human prophet rather than as God present in a man, which is precisely the seam Matthew keeps working. Yet their wording turns out to be prophetic in a way they did not intend, because the risen Christ will in fact commit the ministry of announcing forgiveness to his people (16:19; 18:18; John 20:23), not as a power the church owns but as a message the church declares.

What does "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" mean, and who are "the righteous"?

Verse 9 is the verse you met on your first day of this course, as the practice text for observation. Read it again now and notice how much more you bring to it: you know what a tax booth was and why the job was treason as well as theft, you know that Matthew is the man writing, and you now watch for who acts first in a sentence. Jesus sees, Jesus speaks, Matthew rises. The author records his own rescue without a word of self-defense, and later, when he lists the twelve, he alone keeps the label "the tax collector" attached to his name (10:3). MacArthur notes the same restraint: "Matthew's own humility is seen here. He did not disguise his past or make any excuse for it."

What follows is a dinner party, and the guest list is the offense. Tax collectors (Jews who bought the right to collect Rome's revenue and skimmed above the quota) and "sinners" (a Pharisaic term for people whose lives ignored the ceremonial law) are reclining at table with him, and table fellowship in that world meant acceptance, not just food. The Pharisees ask the disciples the question. Jesus answers it himself, in three moves.

"Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick. Go and learn what this means: 'I desire mercy, and not sacrifice.' For I came not to call the righteous, but sinners." Matthew 9:12-13, ESV

First, the physician saying. A doctor in a sickroom is not endorsing the disease; his presence is explained by it. Second, "go and learn what this means," and feel the sting. That formula was a standard rabbinic rebuke used on a student who had not done the reading. Jesus aims it at the professional Bible scholars of Israel and tells them to go do their homework on a verse they could have recited in their sleep, Hosea 6:6. MacArthur is right that the citation "emphasizes the absolute priority of the law's moral standards over the ceremonial requirements," and that the Pharisees' fixation on the outward made them "harsh, judgmental, and self-righteously scornful of others." Note the Hebrew idiom too: "mercy, and not sacrifice" is a comparison, not an abolition. God commanded the sacrifices. He is saying that ritual performed by a merciless heart is worth nothing to him, a point Israel's prophets made repeatedly (1 Samuel 15:22; Micah 6:6-8; Isaiah 1:11-17).

Third, and this is where people stumble: "I came not to call the righteous, but sinners." That is not a concession that some people are genuinely righteous and therefore fine without him. Matthew has already told you the whole nation was going out to John to confess sins (3:6), and Jesus has already said that anger and lust are murder and adultery (5:21-30). Paul will state the doctrine flatly, "none is righteous, no, not one" (Romans 3:10). Jesus is speaking of the two self-assessments in the room, not of two classes of people. As MacArthur puts it, "The Pharisees thought they were well—religiously pure and whole. The outcasts knew they were not. Salvation can't come to the self-righteous." The physician cannot treat a patient who insists he is healthy. So the line is not comfort for the Pharisees; it is the worst news they could receive, and only sounds like an exemption.

Why can the new not be sewn onto the old?

"Can the wedding guests mourn as long as the bridegroom is with them? The days will come when the bridegroom is taken away from them, and then they will fast." Matthew 9:15, ESV

John's disciples ask a fair question: everyone serious fasts, so why do yours not? Jesus does not answer with rules about fasting; he answers by naming the occasion. This is a wedding. In the Old Testament the bridegroom of Israel is the LORD himself (Isaiah 54:5; Hosea 2:19-20), so the claim buried in the image is the same claim he made to the scribes an hour earlier. Fasting belongs to seasons of grief and pleading, and grief is out of place while the groom is in the room. Then the shadow: "when the bridegroom is taken away." Not departs, not finishes; taken away, by force. This is the first quiet note of the cross in Matthew, dropped into a discussion about mealtimes, and it means the disciples' fasting days are coming.

"No one puts a piece of unshrunk cloth on an old garment, for the patch tears away from the garment, and a worse tear is made. Neither is new wine put into old wineskins. If it is, the skins burst and the wine is spilled and the skins are destroyed. But new wine is put into fresh wineskins, and so both are preserved." Matthew 9:16-17, ESV

Two household pictures with one point. New cloth shrinks when it is washed and rips the old garment it was meant to mend. New wine still fermenting gives off gas, and an old skin that has already stretched and hardened splits under the pressure. MacArthur reads the images as "analogous to trying to patch new covenant truth onto old Mosaic ceremonial forms," and that is the right frame. What Jesus brings is not a reform movement inside Pharisaic religion, an improved fasting schedule bolted onto the existing system. It is the arrival of the promised new covenant (Jeremiah 31:31-34), and it requires new containers.

Be careful with the two errors on either side of this. It does not mean the Old Testament is discarded; you learned in 5:17 that he came to fulfill it, not abolish it, and here he settles an argument by quoting Hosea. Nor does it mean externals are irrelevant, since Jesus assumes his disciples will fast (v. 15, and 6:16-18 tells them how). It means the ceremonial machinery of the old order, which existed to point forward, cannot be the vessel for the reality it pointed to. The wine is genuinely new; the skins have to be.

Application

  • About God He treats your deepest problem as your deepest problem. He went to the sin under the paralysis, and he proved the invisible pardon with a visible healing, so that the forgiveness you cannot verify rests on evidence you can. And what he desires from you first is mercy, not performance.
  • About you There are only two positions at this table, the sick who know it and the well who think they are. Which question do you find yourself asking more often: why does God let those people in, or why did he let me in? Your answer tells you which chair you are sitting in.
  • Do Two things today. First, name one specific sin out loud to God and then say Matthew 9:2 back to yourself as spoken to you: "Take heart, my son; your sins are forgiven." Second, do what Jesus did at Matthew's table: invite one person you would normally keep at arm's length to share a meal or a coffee this week, and actually send the message today.

Church prompt
Ask this at church this week, gently: who is our congregation's version of a tax collector, the person whose presence at our table would make people uncomfortable? Then ask an elder how your church handles someone with a public, disreputable past who starts attending. And a second question worth raising in a small group: where have we patched new wine into old skins, keeping a form or a tradition running on habit long after anyone could say what it is for?

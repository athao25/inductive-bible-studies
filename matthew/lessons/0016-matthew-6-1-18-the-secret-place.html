Matthew 6:1-18: The Secret Place

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 6:1-18 (ESV, read first!)

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Verse 1 is a heading, not an example (v. 1). "Beware of practicing your righteousness before other people in order to be seen by them." Nothing is named yet. Jesus states the principle first, then gives three cases under it. The paragraph divisions in your Bible may hide this; verse 1 governs everything through verse 18.
  • Three acts, one template (vv. 2-4, 5-6, 16-18). Giving, praying, fasting each get the same six moves: "when you", "do not be like the hypocrites", they do it "that they may be seen", "truly, I say to you, they have received their reward", "but when you", "your Father who sees in secret will reward you." Lay the three side by side and the wording is nearly identical.
  • "When", never "if" (vv. 2, 5, 16). Jesus assumes his disciples give, pray, and fast. None of the three is optional or advanced. Fasting in particular is simply expected.
  • "Hypocrites" appears three times (vv. 2, 5, 16), and so does the identical verdict. "They have received their reward." The Greek word behind it was a commercial term for a receipt: paid in full, account closed.
  • "Your Father" is everywhere (vv. 1, 4, 6, 8, 9, 14, 15, 18). Ten times in eighteen verses. This is the relational center of the whole Sermon on the Mount, and it is what the hypocrite has traded away.
  • The prayer section is much longer than the other two (vv. 5-15). Giving gets three verses, fasting gets three, prayer gets eleven. Jesus interrupts his own symmetry to insert a model prayer (vv. 9-13) and a note on forgiveness (vv. 14-15). The center of the structure is the center of the point.
  • Two wrong prayers, not one (vv. 5, 7). The hypocrite prays to be seen; the Gentile heaps up empty phrases hoping that volume of words works. Showmanship and mechanics, both corrected by the same fact in verse 8: "your Father knows what you need before you ask him."
  • Verses 14-15 are attached to the prayer, not free-floating (vv. 14-15). "For if you forgive others their trespasses..." The word "for" ties them back to the fifth petition. Of the six petitions, only one gets follow-up commentary, and it is the one about forgiveness.

Interpretation

Does 6:1 contradict 5:16?

You read the opposite command eight verses ago, and if you did not feel the collision you were reading too fast.

"In the same way, let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father who is in heaven." Matthew 5:16, ESV
"Beware of practicing your righteousness before other people in order to be seen by them, for then you will have no reward from your Father who is in heaven." Matthew 6:1, ESV

Same speaker, same sermon, same phrase "before others", opposite instructions. Compare the purpose clauses and the tension dissolves. In 5:16 they see, and the traffic runs to God: "give glory to your Father." In 6:1 they see, and the traffic stops at you: "in order to be seen by them." Jesus is not regulating visibility, which is impossible anyway; a city on a hill cannot be hidden (5:14). He is regulating audience. The question is never "did anyone notice?" but "whose applause was I working for?" A public act of mercy done so God is praised obeys both verses at once. A private act of mercy done so you can mention it later breaks both.

This is why the key word is hypocrite. In classical Greek the word simply meant a stage actor, the man who spoke his lines from behind a mask. MacArthur on chapter 6 notes it exactly: "This word had its origins in Greek theater, describing a character who wore a mask." Jesus is not calling them liars about their theology. He is saying their religion is a performance for a paying house, and they got paid. The trumpet in the synagogue (v. 2), the standing in the street corners (v. 5), the deliberately disfigured face (v. 16): these are stagecraft. And the terrible mercy of verse 2 is that the transaction is honest. They wanted to be seen; they were seen; the account is settled. God does not reward hypocrisy, though as MacArthur adds, "he does punish it (cf. 23:13-23)."

How does the Lord's Prayer actually work?

Note the wording of verse 9: "Pray then like this." Not "pray these words", though praying these words is good and ancient. It is a pattern, a shape to pour your own praying into. MacArthur puts it plainly: "The prayer is a model, not merely a liturgy. It is notable for its brevity, simplicity, and comprehensiveness." Walk its architecture.

"Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name. Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread, and forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors. And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil." Matthew 6:9-13, ESV

The address holds two things that our instincts pull apart. "Father" is intimacy: you are a child, not a petitioner in a queue. "In heaven" is majesty: he is not your peer. Lose the first and prayer turns into groveling before a stranger; lose the second and it turns into chatting with a friendly uncle. And the pronoun is "our", not "my". Jesus taught this to a group and made even the closet prayer of verse 6 corporate: alone in a shut room you still pray as one of a family, for the family.

Three petitions turned toward God come first, and the order is the lesson. Your name, your kingdom, your will. Only then three petitions turned toward us: bread for the body, forgiveness for the past, deliverance for the road ahead. Most of us pray the second half and skip the first. Jesus puts God's concerns in front, and by the time you arrive at your own needs you are asking as someone who has already agreed whose kingdom this is.

Sit on the second petition for a moment. "Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven." That is a request for God's reign to advance here, in history, in the actual world of streets and households, with heaven as the standard. Our postmillennial home lens takes that at full weight: Jesus taught his church to pray for a genuine answer in time, and he does not teach us to ask for things the Father declines to give. MacArthur, reading from a futurist frame, hears it chiefly as a plea for the future kingdom age to arrive. Both readings pray the same sentence; ours simply expects some of the answer before the end. That is the whole note, and this is not a fork lesson; the fork comes at chapters 13 and 24. Today the point is that you are authorized to ask.

Does verse 15 mean my forgiveness depends on my forgiving?

Verses 14-15 are the sharpest sentences in the passage, and they are easy to misread into terror.

"For if you forgive others their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you, but if you do not forgive others their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses." Matthew 6:14-15, ESV

Read it against the rest of Scripture, which never contradicts itself. Forgiveness is not purchased; Ephesians 1:7 says we have redemption "through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses, according to the riches of his grace." Nobody buys grace with grace. So what is Jesus doing? Two things at once. First, an evidence claim: an unforgiving heart is proof that the pardon has not actually landed. Notice the fifth petition says "as we also have forgiven our debtors", past tense; the forgiving has already started before the asking. A person who has genuinely received a cancelled debt of that size and still throttles a neighbor over a small one has not understood what happened to him. Matthew 18:21-35, the parable of the unforgiving servant, is the full-length treatment; the servant forgiven ten thousand talents who jails a man over a hundred denarii is exposed, not merely fined. Watch for that lesson.

Second, a fellowship claim. MacArthur's note is careful and correct here: this "is not to suggest that God will withdraw justification from those who have already received the free pardon he extends to all believers", but Scripture does teach that God chastens his children and that we confess sins "in order to obtain a day-to-day cleansing (1 John 1:9). This sort of forgiveness is a simple washing from the worldly defilements of sin... It is like a washing of the feet rather than a bath (cf. John 13:10)." Your standing is secure; your communion is not automatic. Nurse a grudge and you will find the secret place has gone cold, not because the Father has changed his verdict but because you are holding in your hand the very thing you came to ask for.

Application

  • About God He sees in secret. That is a threat to the actor and a comfort to the child. Nothing done for him in an unwitnessed room is lost, and nothing staged in a crowded one fools him. He is also a Father who knows the need before the asking, which means prayer is not informing him or wearing him down.
  • About you The performance instinct does not need a synagogue. It runs on group chats, prayer requests that are really announcements, and the small mental replay where someone finds out what you did. Test yourself the honest way: how does it feel when a good work of yours goes permanently unnoticed?
  • Do Today, one thing in secret, with a rule that you will never mention it. Give money nobody will know about, or take fifteen minutes behind a shut door and pray the six petitions in order, in your own words, God's three first. Then, before you get up, name one person you have not forgiven and forgive them out loud.

Church prompt
Fasting is the one of these three practices most believers have quietly dropped, and Jesus said "when you fast", not "if". Ask someone at your church whether they have ever fasted, and what they learned. Ask your pastor whether the congregation has ever fasted together for something. It is a normal Christian practice hiding in plain sight in a verse everybody has read.

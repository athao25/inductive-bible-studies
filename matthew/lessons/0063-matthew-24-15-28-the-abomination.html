Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Jesus cites a named prophet and a named place (v. 15). "The abomination of desolation spoken of by the prophet Daniel, standing in the holy place." This is the only place in the Olivet Discourse where Jesus names an Old Testament book he is drawing on. Daniel uses the phrase three times (Dan. 9:27; 11:31; 12:11). "The holy place" is temple language, not general-purpose language.
  • There is a parenthesis aimed past the disciples (v. 15). "(Let the reader understand)." Jesus is speaking to four men on a hillside, but a reader is someone holding a written text. Either Jesus is flagging Daniel's readers, or Matthew is flagging his own; either way the aside says: this one takes work, do not skim it. Mark keeps the same parenthesis (Mark 13:14).
  • The response commanded is flight, not resistance or endurance (v. 16). "Then let those who are in Judea flee to the mountains." Not pray, not stand firm, not gather in the temple. Run. And run to a specific direction: uphill, out of the province.
  • The instructions assume Judean architecture and Judean roads (vv. 17-18). The man on the housetop must not go down into his house; flat roofs with outside stairs are a Palestinian house, and the point is that a two-minute detour is two minutes too many. The man in the field must not turn back for his cloak, the outer garment a worker laid at the edge of the field while he worked. These are not images of cosmic catastrophe. They are images of a village emptying in a hurry.
  • Two groups are singled out for grief (v. 19). "Alas for women who are pregnant and for those who are nursing infants in those days!" The pity falls exactly where it would fall in a foot-flight over rough ground. Speed is the whole issue.
  • The prayer they are given is about weather and calendar (v. 20). "Pray that your flight may not be in winter or on a Sabbath." Winter means swollen wadis and cold nights. A Sabbath means shut gates, no travel by custom, and no help on the road. That second concern only registers in a first-century Jewish setting; it is a strikingly small, local, practical thing to build into a prophecy.
  • The tribulation is described in absolute terms (v. 21). "Such as has not been from the beginning of the world until now, no, and never will be." Both directions are closed: nothing before it, nothing after it. Note that this is the kind of superlative the Old Testament also uses for particular historical judgments (see Ex. 11:6; Ezek. 5:9; Dan. 12:1; Joel 2:2), which is a fact worth holding on to before you decide what it must mean here.
  • The days are cut short, and the reason is given (v. 22). "If those days had not been cut short, no human being would be saved. But for the sake of the elect those days will be cut short." God is the unnamed actor doing the shortening, and his reason is his chosen people. Judgment here runs on a timetable set by mercy.
  • False christs return, now with signs (vv. 23-26). They appeared already in verse 5; now they "perform great signs and wonders, so as to lead astray, if possible, even the elect." The counterfeits are not obviously shabby. Jesus' answer is repeated flatly twice: "do not believe it" (vv. 23, 26), plus "do not go out" (v. 26).
  • The deception always trades in secret locations (v. 26). "He is in the wilderness" or "he is in the inner rooms." Out there, or in here. Both offers are the same offer: special access, insider information, a Messiah you have to be told about.
  • Verse 27 answers verse 26 with a picture, not an argument. "As the lightning comes from the east and shines as far as the west, so will be the coming of the Son of Man." Lightning needs no announcement and admits no private viewing. The logic is: if someone has to tell you it happened, it did not happen.
  • The unit closes with a grim proverb (v. 28). "Wherever the corpse is, there the vultures will gather." It carries the same force as verse 27: circling birds are visible for miles. It also adds a note verse 27 lacks, that what draws the gathering is a body, that is, judgment.

Interpretation

What is the abomination of desolation?

"So when you see the abomination of desolation spoken of by the prophet Daniel, standing in the holy place (let the reader understand), then let those who are in Judea flee to the mountains." Matthew 24:15-16, ESV

Start with what Daniel's first readers had already seen. In 167 BC Antiochus IV Epiphanes, the Seleucid king of Syria, took Jerusalem, turned the altar into a shrine to Zeus, and sacrificed pigs on it. That event is the textbook "abomination that makes desolate": a defiling thing set up in the holy place, emptying the sanctuary of worship. Jewish readers did not need this explained. So when Jesus says "when you see the abomination of desolation", he is not inventing a category. He is saying: it is going to happen again.

Two readings run through the rest of this unit, and both are held by serious Reformed people. Take each at its strongest.

The futurist reading says the abomination is still ahead: a coming antichrist figure who desecrates a rebuilt temple. Its strongest point is not tradition but two apostles. Paul describes a "man of lawlessness" who "takes his seat in the temple of God, proclaiming himself to be God" (2 Thess. 2:3-4), and John describes an image set up and given breath (Rev. 13:14-15). If those texts are still future, and if they are describing the same event Jesus is, then verse 15 is still future too. Add verse 21's "such as has not been... and never will be", which does sound like a one-time, end-of-history event. This is MacArthur's position, and he states it plainly in his note on 24:15: the phrase "originally referred to the desecration of the temple by Antiochus Epiphanes... However, Jesus clearly was looking toward a yet-future 'abomination of desolation.'" Name the framework honestly: that is premillennial, dispensational futurism, and it shapes the reading before the reading begins.

The preterist-leaning reading, which is this course's home lens (see preterism), says the abomination is the Roman assault on Jerusalem and the temple in AD 66-70. Its strongest point is Luke. Where Matthew has "the abomination of desolation... standing in the holy place", Luke's parallel in the same discourse reads: "But when you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies, then know that its desolation has come near" (Luke 21:20), followed by the identical order to flee to the mountains. One evangelist appears to be interpreting the other's phrase for a Gentile audience, and the interpretation he gives is armies, not an idol. Second, early Christian tradition (Eusebius, Church History 3.5) reports that the Jerusalem believers, warned by an oracle, left the city before the siege closed and withdrew to Pella across the Jordan. The command in verse 16 was, on this reading, obeyed.

Do not settle it yet. The next lesson argues the fork from the text, and the honest thing to do today is to feel the weight of both.

Why are the instructions so small and so local?

"Let the one who is on the housetop not go down to take what is in his house, and let the one who is in the field not turn back to take his cloak. And alas for women who are pregnant and for those who are nursing infants in those days! Pray that your flight may not be in winter or on a Sabbath." Matthew 24:17-20, ESV

This is the strongest single piece of evidence in the whole debate, and you can weigh it before you know any of the systems. Look at the scale of these commands. Not "the nations", but "those who are in Judea". Not eternal destinies, but a cloak. Not the end of the cosmos, but a roof, a field, a wadi in winter, a shut Sabbath gate. Every instruction is the kind you would give to people you expect to be standing in one small province on one particular morning, with a road out and a reason to take it. The pregnant and nursing are pitied not theologically but physically: they cannot move fast.

Weigh that honestly in both directions. It is real pressure toward the AD 70 reading, because a global end-of-history cataclysm is not escaped by leaving the house without your coat, and running to the hills of Moab does not help if the sun has gone dark. The Sabbath clause presses the same way: it presumes a community still ordering its week by the synagogue calendar, which fits AD 70 in Judea very naturally.

The futurist answer is not empty, so hear it. A future tribulation, on that reading, still has a geographic epicenter, because Daniel's prophecy and Paul's man of lawlessness both locate the offense in the temple, which sits in Judea. MacArthur takes "the mountains" concretely, suggesting the caves and refuges southeast of Jerusalem toward the Dead Sea where David hid from Saul. So the futurist can say: yes, local, because the trigger event is local, even if what follows is global. Whether that answer covers the Sabbath clause and the cloak is exactly the sort of question you should be turning over between now and lesson 64.

Why is the lightning the answer to the false christs?

"So, if they say to you, 'Look, he is in the wilderness,' do not go out. If they say, 'Look, he is in the inner rooms,' do not believe it. For as the lightning comes from the east and shines as far as the west, so will be the coming of the Son of Man." Matthew 24:26-27, ESV

Notice the shape of the deception before you notice the refutation. Every false messiah in verses 23-26 is sold by location: he is over there, he is in here, come and see. That requires a middleman, and the middleman is the point. False religion runs on insider information, on someone who knows where the real thing is and will take you to it, usually for the price of your loyalty. And these counterfeits come with power: "great signs and wonders" (v. 24). Miracles alone are not a test. Jesus says so here, and Deuteronomy 13:1-3 said it first.

Verse 27 dismantles the whole business without arguing about any particular claimant. Lightning is not reported; it is seen. It fills the sky from one horizon to the other, and nobody needs a guide to find it. So the test Jesus gives is not "check this man's credentials" but "if you had to be told, the answer is no". MacArthur puts it well in his note on 24:26: "No one should consider the claims of self-styled messiahs because all of them are false. When Christ returns, no one will miss it." That much is common ground across every position in this room, and it is immediately usable. Verse 28 seals it with a proverb about carrion birds: judgment, like lightning, does not need a press release. It is obvious from a distance, and something has died.

One more thing about verse 24. "So as to lead astray, if possible, even the elect" is a statement of the danger and, in the same breath, a limit on it. MacArthur reads the phrase as implying it is not possible, citing John 10:4-5, and that is consistent with everything the Gospel has said about the Father's keeping. The warning is real; so is the security. Both are for you at once.

Application

  • About God. He shortens the days, and he does it "for the sake of the elect" (v. 22). Even in the worst hour Scripture can describe, the clock is not held by the destroyer. It is held by a Father with his own people in view.
  • About you. Ask what would be your cloak. The man in the field is not told his cloak is worthless; he is told it is not worth turning around for. Somewhere in your life there is a good thing you would go back for at exactly the wrong moment. Naming it now is cheaper than discovering it then.
  • Do (today). Write down one spiritual claim you have believed lately because of who told it to you rather than because you checked it, whether a podcast, a post, or a confident friend. Then open the Bible and check that one claim against a text. Ten minutes. Verse 26 is training for exactly this reflex: the "do not believe it" muscle gets built on small things long before it is needed for large ones.

You are not fishing for a label; you are asking someone you trust to show you their work in the text, which is the skill this whole course is trying to build.

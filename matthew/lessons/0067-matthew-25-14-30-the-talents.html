Matthew 25:14-30: The Talents

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 25:14-30 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 25:14-30 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations. Watch especially for how the amounts are distributed and on what basis, for how long the master is gone, for exactly what the third servant did and did not do, and for the two commendations side by side.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Everything belongs to the master (v. 14). He "entrusted to them his property." Not gave, not sold: entrusted. Nothing in the story ever becomes a servant's own, which is why the accounting at the end is not unfair.
  • The distribution is deliberate and unequal (v. 15). "To one he gave five talents, to another two, to another one, to each according to his ability." The inequality is not an accident of the plot; the master measured it. And no character in the parable ever complains about it.
  • The first two act immediately (vv. 16-17). "He who had received the five talents went at once and traded with them." The one-talent servant, by contrast, "went and dug in the ground and hid his master's money" (v. 18). Same opening move for all three; opposite direction.
  • A long delay (v. 19). "Now after a long time the master of those servants came and settled accounts with them." Mark that phrase. Delay has run through this whole section: the flood came after long ordinary years (24:38), the master "is delayed" (24:48), the bridegroom "was delayed" (25:5). Now again.
  • Two identical commendations (vv. 21, 23). Word for word: "Well done, good and faithful servant. You have been faithful over a little; I will set you over much. Enter into the joy of your master." One produced five, the other two. The words do not scale with the yield.
  • The third servant speaks first about the master, not himself (vv. 24-25). His defense opens "I knew you to be a hard man." The action he took (or did not take) follows from a picture of the master's character, and he says so in that order.
  • He did not steal or squander (v. 25). "Here, you have what is yours." The full amount comes back intact. Nothing is lost. That is his entire defense, and it is not enough.
  • The master answers with the servant's own words (v. 26). "You knew that I reap where I have not sown and gather where I scattered no seed?" He does not concede the description; he turns it into the argument against the man.
  • The bar named is low (v. 27). Not "you should have doubled it." Rather "you ought to have invested my money with the bankers, and at my coming I should have received what was my own with interest." Bankers require no courage and no skill.
  • The ending is severe (vv. 28-30). The talent is taken and given to the man with ten; "to everyone who has will more be given"; and the servant himself is thrown "into the outer darkness. In that place there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth." The last phrase is Matthew's standard language for final judgment (8:12, 13:42, 22:13).

Interpretation

What is a talent, and why does that word need rescuing?

"To one he gave five talents, to another two, to another one, to each according to his ability. Then he went away." Matthew 25:15, ESV

Almost everyone has heard this parable preached as "use your talents for God," meaning your aptitudes: music, teaching, hospitality. Gently but firmly, that is not what the word means here. A talent was a unit of weight, and therefore of money, roughly 75 pounds of silver. Your ESV footnote at verse 15 puts one talent at about twenty years' wages for a laborer. Five talents is a fortune; even the one-talent servant is holding two decades of a working man's income. Nobody in the original audience heard "abilities." They heard a staggering sum of somebody else's money.

The English word "talent" meaning natural aptitude actually comes from this parable, which is a genuinely charming piece of language history and also the reason the misreading is so hard to shake. MacArthur on chapter 25 makes the point at verse 15: "The modern meaning of the word 'talent,' denoting a natural ability, stems from the fact that this parable is erroneously applied to the stewardship of one's natural gifts." The subject is not what you were born with. It is what the master handed you and expects back.

That said, notice the one place where ability does appear: "to each according to his ability." The master knows his servants and calibrates the trust. So the unequal distribution is neither random nor unjust, and the parable never treats it as a grievance. The five-talent man is not more loved and the one-talent man is not shortchanged. Each is given exactly what he can handle, and each is measured by the same standard.

Why is doing nothing the thing that gets condemned?

"Master, I knew you to be a hard man, reaping where you did not sow, and gathering where you scattered no seed, so I was afraid, and I went and hid your talent in the ground. Here, you have what is yours." Matthew 25:24-25, ESV

Follow the logic of that sentence in order: I knew you to be, therefore I was afraid, therefore I hid it. A false view of the master's character produced fear, and the fear produced paralysis. This is the center of the parable. The man did not gamble the money away, did not spend it on himself, did not run off with it. He kept it perfectly safe. And burying it is exactly what is condemned, because safe-keeping was never the assignment.

The master's reply is a piece of careful argument, not just anger. He picks up the servant's own description and hands it back as a question (v. 26). MacArthur's note on that verse is right: "He was allowing the man's own words to condemn him." The point is that the man's excuse defeats itself. If the master really were a ruthless opportunist who harvests fields he never planted, that would be the strongest possible reason to work hard, not the excuse for doing nothing. Fear of a demanding master should produce frantic effort. It produced a shovel. So the stated reason was never the real reason; it was cover for a heart that did not care what the master wanted.

Then verse 27 removes the last defense. Bankers. Not risk, not brilliance, not a doubled return: just handing it to someone else and collecting interest. The floor was that low, and he did not reach it. Whatever the servant's problem was, it was not that the task was too hard.

Does the ending make this a parable about earning salvation?

"And cast the worthless servant into the outer darkness. In that place there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth." Matthew 25:30, ESV

Do not soften that verse. It is final judgment, in Matthew's most consistent vocabulary for it. But read it with the parable's own logic rather than importing a scheme of merit. The servant is not condemned for producing two instead of five; the two-talent man produced less than the five-talent man and heard the identical "well done." Reward tracked faithfulness, not yield. The third man is condemned for a fruitlessness that exposed what he actually thought of his master. He never knew him. He said so himself, in his opening line, and the description he gave was false.

This is the same logic Jesus has used twice already in this Gospel: "you will recognize them by their fruits" (7:16-20), and "the tree is known by its fruit" (12:33). Fruit does not make the tree good; fruit reveals what the tree is. A genuine servant of this master will do something with what he is given, not because doing something purchases sonship, but because a person who truly knows this master cannot sit on his property for a lifetime and feel fine about it. Verse 29, "to everyone who has will more be given," echoes 13:12, where the same principle governed who received understanding of the parables. Grace given and used multiplies; grace despised is finally withdrawn.

One note on the frame. This parable sits in the Olivet Discourse, and the three readings you will compare in the recap lesson (futurist, amillennial, and this course's postmillennial home reading) all agree about what it teaches: the master is away, the return is certain, the interval is for work, and the accounting is real. MacArthur reads the whole of chapter 25 against a still-future tribulation and millennial reign; our home lens reads chapter 24 as largely fulfilled in AD 70 with the bodily return still ahead. Neither difference changes a word of the application here. Whatever your view of the timetable, you are in the "long time" of verse 19 right now.

Application

  • About God: he entrusts real property to servants who cannot possibly earn it, calibrates it to what each can bear, stays away long enough for the trust to be genuine, and then returns and takes the accounting seriously. He is generous and he is not casual.
  • About you: the sin this parable names is not scandal. It is playing it safe out of a wrong picture of God. If you privately suspect God is harsh, stingy, or waiting to catch you out, you will bury things: your money, your gifts, your voice, the gospel itself. Cautious inaction feels like reverence. Here it is called wickedness and sloth.
  • Do: name one specific thing God has entrusted to you that is currently in the ground (a conversation about Christ you keep not having, money you hold tightly, a gift you will not use because you might be bad at it, an hour a day you spend on nothing). Write down why you have not acted, in one sentence. If the sentence is really about fear, put next to it what is true about God instead. Then take the smallest available step today. Bankers, not brilliance.

Church prompt
Ask someone at church this week what your congregation has been entrusted with that is currently buried: a ministry nobody will start, a neighborhood nobody visits, a building sitting empty six days a week. Ask it as a question, not an accusation. Then ask what it would take to hand that one thing to the bankers.

Matthew 7:1-12: Judging and Asking

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • Verse 1 comes with a reason attached (vv. 1-2). "Judge not, that you be not judged" is not a free-standing slogan; the very next word is "For." The reason given is a measuring rule: "with the measure you use it will be measured to you." The warning is about a standard boomeranging, not about standards existing.
  • The log is in the inspector's eye (v. 3). Read it slowly. The man with the vision problem is the one doing the examining. He can see a speck of sawdust at conversational distance while a beam of construction lumber sticks out of his own face.
  • The picture is deliberately absurd (vv. 3-4). A speck is a wood chip or straw; a log is a load-bearing timber. Jesus is not being subtle, he is being funny, and the joke is aimed at people who take themselves very seriously. First-century Galilee laughed at this line.
  • The speck still comes out (v. 5). "First take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother's eye." Not "then mind your business." The removal of the speck is the assumed goal of the whole illustration; self-examination is the qualifying step, not the substitute.
  • One noun names the sin (v. 5). "You hypocrite." Matthew has used that word repeatedly through this sermon (6:2, 5, 16). The problem is the same one as in chapter 6: performance, a public face that does not match a private life.
  • Verse 6 requires you to tell people apart. "Do not give dogs what is holy, and do not throw your pearls before pigs." You cannot obey this command without assessing who is a dog and who is a pig. Set it beside verse 1 and notice that the same paragraph forbids one kind of judging and commands another.
  • Three commands, one promise, then the same three again (vv. 7-8). Ask, seek, knock; given, find, opened. Verse 8 repeats the whole set as a stated principle: "everyone who asks receives." The intensity climbs from asking to searching to pounding on a door.
  • The argument runs from bad fathers to the good Father (vv. 9-11). Bread and stone, fish and serpent: paired items that look alike and are not. Then the hinge, "If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things."
  • Verse 12 signs off on the whole sermon. "So whatever you wish that others would do to you, do also to them, for this is the Law and the Prophets." That last phrase is the same pair of words Jesus used back at 5:17. Two bookends, one argument closed.

Interpretation

Does "judge not" forbid making moral assessments?

"Judge not, that you be not judged. For with the judgment you pronounce you will be judged, and with the measure you use it will be measured to you." Matthew 7:1-2, ESV

This is the most quoted verse in the Bible among people who quote no others, and it is almost always used to end a conversation rather than to obey Christ. So handle it the way you handle everything else: by context. Three things in the immediate neighborhood make the "never assess anyone" reading impossible.

First, verse 5. Jesus tells you to remove the log so that "you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother's eye." Clear sight is the goal, and the removal of your brother's speck is the assumed outcome. If Jesus meant hands off, the sentence would end after "own eye." Second, verse 6. Dogs and pigs were not compliments; obeying that command requires you to look at a person's response to holy things and draw a conclusion. Third, verse 15, a few lines later: "Beware of false prophets," and the test given is fruit inspection. You cannot beware of someone you have refused to evaluate.

So what is forbidden? Censorious judgment, which means fault-finding as a habit, condemning as a posture, judging in order to feel taller. It is judgment that exempts the judge. The measuring language of verse 2 is the point: whatever rule you hold others to becomes the rule held over you, and the man swinging the harshest standard is the one least likely to survive it. MacArthur on chapter 7 puts it cleanly: "this does not prohibit all types of judging (v. 16). There is a righteous kind of judgment we are supposed to exercise with careful discernment (John 7:24). Censorious, hypocritical, self-righteous, or other kinds of unfair judgments are forbidden; but in order to fulfill the commandments that follow, it is necessary to discern dogs and swine (Matt. 7:6) from one’s own brethren (vv. 3–5)."

Now sit with the comedy of verses 3-5, because it is doing real work. The man with a beam in his eye has volunteered for eye surgery. Jesus does not say the speck is imaginary; he says the surgeon is blind. And note where Jesus put the log: not in the sinner being examined, in the one doing the examining. That is the reversal you are meant to feel. The instinct that says "look at him" is precisely the instinct Jesus turns around and points back at you.

If the Father already knows what I need, why ask, seek, and knock?

"Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you. For everyone who asks receives, and the one who seeks finds, and to the one who knocks it will be opened." Matthew 7:7-8, ESV

Jesus already said your Father "knows what you need before you ask him" (6:8). So prayer is not information transfer. Then why press? Because the verbs here are continuous in the Greek: keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking. This is not a formula for getting a wish granted on the first try, it is a call to persistent prayer, the kind that stays at the door. Notice the escalation too. Asking is words. Seeking is words plus effort, you go looking. Knocking is words plus effort plus refusing to leave.

"If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him!" Matthew 7:11, ESV

This is a lesser-to-greater argument, the same move Jesus made with the birds and the lilies in 6:26-30. And look what he slips in without argument or apology: "you then, who are evil." He is not addressing criminals, he is addressing his disciples, on a hillside, mid-encouragement. Human depravity is simply assumed, stated in passing as the weaker half of a comparison. MacArthur notes on 7:11 that "Jesus presupposes the doctrine of human depravity." That is the sharpest edge in this paragraph: the doctrine we argue about most is here treated as too obvious to defend.

And the comfort is built on it. If sinful fathers still manage not to hand their hungry sons a rock, what will a holy Father do? But read the promise exactly: he gives "good things," which means what he judges good, not whatever the request happened to name. The father in the illustration refuses the serpent. Sometimes the answer to your asking is that God will not put a snake in your hand just because you asked for one.

Why does verse 12 say "this is the Law and the Prophets"?

"So whatever you wish that others would do to you, do also to them, for this is the Law and the Prophets." Matthew 7:12, ESV

That phrase is a structural marker. Back at 5:17 Jesus said, "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them." Here the same phrase returns, and everything between the two is the body of the Sermon on the Mount. Verse 12 is not a nice thought dropped in at random; it closes the case. From 7:13 on, Jesus stops teaching kingdom righteousness and starts pressing for a decision: two gates, two trees, two builders.

The famous form of this rule in the ancient world was negative. MacArthur's note on 7:12 gives Rabbi Hillel's version: "What is hateful to yourself do not to someone else." Similar sayings appear across the ancient world. Jesus turns it around, and the reversal is enormous. A negative rule can be obeyed by a man who stays home and touches no one; refrain from harm and you have kept it. The positive form has no such exit. It sends you looking for the thing you would want if you were the one who was sick, broke, new, forgotten, or wrong, and then it tells you to go do that. You cannot satisfy Jesus' version by inaction. The lonely person down the hall is not owed your non-interference, he is owed the visit you would want.

Notice also what the rule assumes: that your desire for your own good is a reliable measuring stick, so put it to work on someone else. This is the same standard that has been running through the whole sermon, and it is why the sermon ends the way it does. Nobody keeps this. That is precisely the point of the two gates that come next.

Application

  • About God He is a Father who gives good gifts, and better ones than we know how to ask for. He is also a Judge whose measure is exact, which is why the standard you hand out is a serious thing to hold.
  • About you Your sharpest criticisms of other people are a map of your own blind spots. And your prayer life is probably shorter on persistence than on words; ask-seek-knock describes someone still at the door, not someone who mentioned it once.
  • Do Two things today. First, name the person you have been quietly critical of this week, and before you say another word about them, write down the log in your own eye that their speck reminds you of. Second, pick one thing you genuinely want done for you (a call, a meal, being included, a debt overlooked) and do that exact thing for one person before bed.

Church prompt
Almost every congregation leans one way on this passage: either it judges too freely or it will not confront anything at all. Ask an elder or a mature member this week how our church handles it when a member is walking into obvious sin, and what verse 5 looks like in practice, doing the self-examination before the conversation. Then ask yourself the harder version: is there anyone who owes you a hard word and has not been given it because nobody wanted to seem judgmental?

Questions welcome, always: "so when am I allowed to say something is sin?", "does 'ask and it will be given' mean I get whatever I request?", "who are the dogs and pigs in verse 6?" Ask your teacher.

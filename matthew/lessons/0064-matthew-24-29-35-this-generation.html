Matthew 24:29-35: This Generation

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The time marker is as tight as language allows (v. 29). "Immediately after the tribulation of those days." Not "long after," not "in a later age." Whatever the tribulation of verses 15-28 was, verse 29 follows hard on its heels. This one adverb constrains every reading that follows.
  • Four cosmic clauses, all in the passive or intransitive (v. 29). Sun darkened, moon not giving its light, stars falling, the powers of the heavens shaken. Nobody is described as doing these things; they simply happen. Note the vocabulary and hold it, because you will meet it again in the prophets.
  • What appears is a sign, and it appears "in heaven" (v. 30). Not "a sign in the sky announcing his arrival on earth." Read the clause exactly: "then will appear in heaven the sign of the Son of Man."
  • The mourning comes before the seeing (v. 30). Order in the sentence: the tribes mourn, then "they will see the Son of Man coming on the clouds of heaven with power and great glory."
  • The coming is "on the clouds," a quotation (v. 30). This is Daniel 7:13 almost word for word. Matthew's Jewish readers would have heard the source instantly, and the direction of travel in Daniel is worth checking before you assume it.
  • The gathering is of the elect, not of the wicked (v. 31). Angels, a loud trumpet call, "from the four winds, from one end of heaven to the other." No judgment of the gathered is mentioned here at all.
  • The fig tree teaches nearness, not date-setting (vv. 32-33). "When you see all these things, you know that he is near, at the very gates." The pronoun is "you," addressed to the four men sitting on the Mount of Olives.
  • Two "pass away" sayings sit back to back (vv. 34-35). "This generation will not pass away until all these things take place." Then: "Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away." Verse 34 begins with "Truly, I say to you," Jesus' formula for a solemn guarantee.

Interpretation

This is the largest fork in the course. Four questions, worked in order. Take them slowly, and notice that the course's own conclusion is stated plainly and its weaknesses are stated too.

1. What kind of language is verse 29?

"Immediately after the tribulation of those days the sun will be darkened, and the moon will not give its light, and the stars will fall from heaven, and the powers of the heavens will be shaken." Matthew 24:29, ESV

Here is an observation that changes how many readers hear this verse, and it is not a clever trick; it is simple cross-referencing. This exact imagery appears repeatedly in the Old Testament prophets, and in those places it describes the fall of a nation within history. Look up all three:

  • Isaiah 13:10, in the oracle against Babylon: "the stars of the heavens and their constellations will not give their light; the sun will be dark at its rising, and the moon will not shed its light." Babylon fell to the Medes in 539 BC. The sun kept rising.
  • Isaiah 34:4, against Edom: "All the host of heaven shall rot away, and the skies roll up like a scroll." Edom was a small kingdom southeast of the Dead Sea, and it perished. The sky is still there.
  • Ezekiel 32:7, against Egypt and its Pharaoh: "I will cover the heavens and make their stars dark; I will cover the sun with a cloud, and the moon shall not give its light." Egypt was humbled by Babylon. No literal astronomy was disturbed.

So the prophets had a settled poetic convention: when a great power that seemed as fixed as the sky comes down, you say the sky went dark. That is apocalyptic language, a prophetic style that uses cosmic pictures to describe world-shaking events on the ground. If Jesus is speaking that dialect in verse 29, then the darkened sun is the end of the temple order, not the end of the solar system.

Now the counter-consideration, stated fairly, because this course does not win arguments by hiding the other half. First, that a convention can be figurative does not prove it always is; a prophet may also describe the real end, and 2 Peter 3:10-12 speaks of the heavens passing away with a roar and the heavenly bodies being burned up and dissolved, which is not obviously poetry. Second, MacArthur takes exactly this line: he lists the same day-of-the-Lord parallels (Isa. 13, Ezek. 32, Joel 2, Amos 8) and then says "the ultimate fulfillment of these prophecies takes place during the time of the beast's reign" (MacArthur on chapter 24, note on 24:29). He grants the convention and argues the fulfillment is still ahead. That is a real argument, not a dodge. The question the text forces is whether "immediately after the tribulation of those days" lets the fulfillment be two thousand years later.

2. Which direction is the Son of Man travelling in verse 30?

"Then will appear in heaven the sign of the Son of Man, and then all the tribes of the earth will mourn, and they will see the Son of Man coming on the clouds of heaven with power and great glory." Matthew 24:30, ESV

The plain reading, and it is genuinely plain: this is the visible second coming. Jesus descends, everyone sees him, the world mourns. That is how most English readers hear it, and MacArthur reads it that way, adding that "the Son of Man himself is the sign" and pairing the verse with Revelation 19:11-21 (note on 24:30). Nothing in that reading is silly.

But hold the sentence against its source. Son of Man "coming on the clouds" is Daniel 7:13-14, and in Daniel the traffic runs the other way:

"I saw in the night visions, and behold, with the clouds of heaven there came one like a son of man, and he came to the Ancient of Days and was presented before him. And to him was given dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him." Daniel 7:13-14, ESV

The son of man is not coming down to earth. He is coming up to the throne of God to be crowned. Daniel 7 is a coronation scene: the beasts are judged, the Ancient of Days sits, and a human figure receives an everlasting kingdom. If Jesus is quoting that, verse 30 is not a description of his descent but an announcement of his enthronement, and the visible proof of the enthronement is the judgment that falls on the city that rejected him. On this reading the "sign of the Son of Man in heaven" means the sign that the Son of Man has been seated in heaven, and what people "see" is the vindication, the way the whole world "saw" that Rome ruled when Jerusalem burned.

Two smaller notes that matter. Jesus himself uses this Daniel line one chapter later, to Caiaphas: "from now on you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven" (26:64). "From now on," said to men who would be dead within forty years. And "all the tribes of the earth" translates a phrase whose noun can equally mean land: all the tribes of the land, that is, of Israel. Zechariah 12:10-12, which MacArthur rightly cites here, is precisely about the tribes of the land mourning for the one they pierced. That single translation choice tilts the whole verse toward Israel or toward the globe, and honest translators differ.

3. Who are the angels and what is the trumpet in verse 31?

"And he will send out his angels with a loud trumpet call, and they will gather his elect from the four winds, from one end of heaven to the other." Matthew 24:31, ESV

Plain reading first: this is the final gathering, angels sent at the last day, the resurrection and the assembling of all God's people. That reading has 1 Thessalonians 4:16 behind it and MacArthur states it flatly, calling it "the culmination of world history" (note on 24:31).

The other reading rests on two textual facts. First, the Greek word rendered "angels" is angelos, which simply means messenger, and Matthew has already used it of a man: John the Baptist is God's angelos in 11:10. So "he will send out his messengers" can describe the apostolic mission. Second, trumpet-and-gathering is standard Old Testament ingathering language: "in that day a great trumpet will be blown, and those who were perishing . . . will come and worship the LORD" (Isaiah 27:13). On that reading verse 31 describes the worldwide gospel mission gathering the elect out of every nation, which is exactly what verse 14 promised and exactly what began in the decades after the cross. Weigh both. The vocabulary genuinely permits either, and "from one end of heaven to the other" is a strong phrase either way.

4. The hinge: what does "this generation" mean in verse 34?

"Truly, I say to you, this generation will not pass away until all these things take place." Matthew 24:34, ESV

Everything above funnels here. Four proposals, each with its best argument and its real weakness:

  • (a) The generation then living. Best argument: this is what the word means everywhere else in Matthew, without exception, and always of the contemporaries of Jesus (11:16, 12:39-45, 16:4, 17:17, 23:36). Two lessons ago you read 23:36, "all these things will come upon this generation," said of the same judgment, in the same week, in the same building complex. Weakness: it must then explain verses 29-31 in non-literal terms, which some readers find forced.
  • (b) "Generation" means race or nation: the Jewish people will not disappear before the end. Best argument: the Greek word can, in rare contexts, shade toward kind or stock. Weakness: it makes the promise nearly empty (of course a people survives), it never carries that sense elsewhere in Matthew, and Matthew has a perfectly ordinary word for nation available. Most careful commentators on every side now treat it as strained.
  • (c) The future generation that sees the signs begin. This is MacArthur's reading: "this cannot refer to the generation living at that time of Christ, for 'all these things' . . . did not 'take place' in their lifetime," so it must mean "the generation alive at the time when those final hard labor pains begin" (note on 24:34). Best argument: it takes verses 29-31 at face value and fits the fig tree's point about a short span. Weakness: it requires "this" to point at a generation not yet born and not previously mentioned, which is not how the demonstrative normally works, and the whole discourse is addressed to "you."
  • (d) "All these things" covers only the signs, not the coming. Best argument: verse 33 uses the same phrase for what "you see" before he is near, so the phrase may be a technical label for the preliminaries. Weakness: verse 34 also says "all," and the natural antecedent is everything just described, verses 4-31 included.

Where this course lands, and why. Reading (a). Three textual reasons, none of them a label. First, "this generation" means in 24:34 what it means in 23:36 and in every other Matthean use, and a word should not change meaning inside one week of teaching without a signal in the text. Second, "immediately after" in verse 29 ties the cosmic language to the tribulation just described, and the flight-from-Judea instructions of verses 16-20 are useless advice to anyone but first-century Judeans. Third, 16:28 already said something of the same shape: "there are some standing here who will not taste death until they see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom." So verses 4-35 concern the judgment that fell on Jerusalem and the temple in AD 70, the Son of Man vindicated and enthroned exactly as Daniel 7 said. And then verse 36 pivots: "But concerning that day and hour no one knows." A day nobody can date is a different subject from events with visible signs, and that subject is the bodily return still ahead of us. That is the historic postmillennial and partial-preterist reading, and it is this course's home lens.

Now hear the other side at full strength. The MacArthur Study Bible argues the futurist case throughout chapter 24, and its Introduction does not treat this as a minor disagreement: it calls the preterist conclusion "a serious interpretive blunder, forcing the interpreter to read into these passages spiritualized, allegorical meanings unwarranted by normal exegetical methods." That is a strong charge from a careful man, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a shrug. The answer is that reading Isaiah's darkened sun over Babylon as figurative is not allegory imposed from outside; it is the prophets' own usage, established from their own contexts. But you should be able to feel the force of his objection: once you start reading vivid language figuratively, you need a principled stopping point, and the discipline of asking "what does the Old Testament do with this exact image" is that stopping point. Amillennial readers, James White among them, often take a third path, hearing both a near fulfillment in AD 70 and a final fulfillment at the last day in the same words. Faithful, orthodox, Bible-believing Christians hold each of these positions.

The boundary that is not negotiable
Partial preterism says much of Matthew 24 was fulfilled in AD 70 and affirms without qualification a future, bodily, visible return of Christ, a general resurrection of the dead, and a final judgment. That is inside historic Christian orthodoxy and is what this course teaches. Full preterism (sometimes "hyper-preterism") says everything, including the second coming, the resurrection, and the judgment, already happened in AD 70 and nothing remains. That denies articles the church has confessed since the beginning, contradicts 1 Corinthians 15 and Acts 1:11, and is outside orthodoxy. The line between them is not a matter of emphasis. Know which side of it you are on, and know that a partial preterist and a futurist are brothers, while full preterism is a different religion at the point of the resurrection.

Application

  • About God He keeps appointments. Whatever verse 34 means, it means Jesus staked his credibility on a timetable, and 23:38 to 24:2 to AD 70 is the record of a prophecy that landed. The God who kept that word is keeping the one still outstanding.
  • About you You now hold a conviction on a passage that divides sincere Christians. Test yourself: can you state MacArthur's futurist reading in a form he would recognize and call fair? If you cannot, you do not yet hold your own view honestly; you hold a caricature of his.
  • Do Today, open your ESV and read Isaiah 13:1-13 and Ezekiel 32:1-8 straight through, ten minutes, noticing the headings that tell you which nation each concerns. Seeing the convention in its own habitat is worth more than any argument in this lesson. Then write one sentence: what you now think verse 29 describes, and one thing that would change your mind.
Memory verse 9: Matthew 24:35
"Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away." Matthew 24:35, ESV
Read it aloud 10 times, then say it from memory 5 times, then once more tonight before bed. Sit on the claim inside it. Heaven and earth are the most permanent things a person can point at; a Jewish teacher would say "as long as heaven and earth endure" to mean forever. Jesus takes the most durable thing in creation, puts it on the perishable side of the sentence, and puts his own words on the imperishable side. Isaiah 40:8 says that about the word of the LORD. A man saying it about his own sayings is either deluded or divine. Note where this verse sits: immediately after the hardest promise in the discourse, as its guarantee.

Lesson 0074 · Unit 8: Chapters 26-28

Matthew 27:27-44: The Crown of Thorns

~25 min · Passage: Matthew 27:27-44 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not read Matthew 27:27-44 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations. Watch especially for every word in these verses that belongs to kings and kingship, and for how many separate groups speak, and what each one asks for.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

The key that opens the passage

Read this scene as a coronation and everything in it lines up. Matthew has argued from his first sentence that Jesus is the King (1:1). Here, in the hands of men who despise him, Jesus is robed, crowned, handed a scepter, knelt before, hailed by title, and finally has his royal title posted in writing above his head for the whole city to read. Every element is present. The soldiers stage it as theater. Matthew records it as truth. Nobody in the passage means a word of it, and every word of it is right.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • The whole battalion is assembled (v. 27). "They gathered the whole battalion before him," a cohort of roughly six hundred men, in the governor's headquarters. This is not a few bored guards; it is a formal gathering, which is what a coronation requires.
  • Robe, crown, scepter, in that order (vv. 28-29). "A scarlet robe," "a crown of thorns," "a reed in his right hand." Mark and John call the robe purple; Matthew's scarlet was the color of a Roman military cloak, the nearest thing in that room to royal dress.
  • They kneel and they acclaim (v. 29). "And kneeling before him, they mocked him, saying, 'Hail, King of the Jews!'" Homage and acclamation, the last two acts of an enthronement, performed by men who are laughing.
  • The scepter becomes a club (v. 30). "They spit on him and took the reed and struck him on the head." Isaiah 50:6 had written this line centuries early: "I gave my back to those who strike, and my cheeks to those who pull out the beard."
  • Matthew refuses to describe the crucifixion (v. 35). Five English words: "And when they had crucified him." No detail, no anatomy, no lingering. His first readers had seen crosses on the roads; they needed no explanation, and Matthew gives none.
  • A stranger carries the cross (v. 32). "They found a man of Cyrene, Simon by name. They compelled this man to carry his cross." Cyrene is in north Africa. He is conscripted, not volunteering, and he is not one of the Twelve.
  • He refuses the drugged wine (v. 34). "When he tasted it, he would not drink it." He tastes, identifies, and declines.
  • The garments are divided by lot (v. 35). Executioners took the clothes as spoils. Psalm 22:18 had said it: "they divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots."
  • The charge is a royal declaration (v. 37). "This is Jesus, the King of the Jews." Rome posts it as the legal indictment and as a joke about a conquered people. It is the third time in eleven verses that Jesus is called King.
  • Three waves of mockery, one demand (vv. 39-44). Passersby (v. 39), then the chief priests with the scribes and elders (v. 41), then the robbers crucified beside him (v. 44). Different mouths, identical content: save yourself, come down.

Interpretation

Why does Matthew let the mockery run so long?

"And they stripped him and put a scarlet robe on him, and twisting together a crown of thorns, they put it on his head and put a reed in his right hand. And kneeling before him, they mocked him, saying, 'Hail, King of the Jews!'" Matthew 27:28-29, ESV

Because it is not only mockery. Set the elements beside a real enthronement and count: the robe of state, the crown, the scepter in the right hand, the subjects on their knees, the shouted acclamation of the title. Rome knew how to crown a man; these soldiers were parodying a ceremony they had watched. And every single item they chose in contempt is an item Matthew's Gospel has been claiming for Jesus since chapter 1. MacArthur on chapter 27 notes the deliberate cruelty of the details, that they "purposely chose something flimsy-looking" for the scepter and that a reed stiff enough to serve as one was stiff enough to hurt. The soldiers picked each object to say you are not a king. Matthew writes each object down because he is.

This pattern has a name worth learning: irony in the biblical sense, where a speaker says something as ridicule and the narrator knows it is literally true. Matthew uses it three times in this passage: the soldiers' "Hail, King of the Jews" (v. 29), the placard's "This is Jesus, the King of the Jews" (v. 37), and the priests' "He is the King of Israel" (v. 42). Three declarations of the kingship of Christ, all from his enemies, all accurate, none of them meant.

And then the thorns. Do not push this further than the text allows, but do not miss it either. When the ground was cursed for Adam's sin, the LORD said it would bring forth "thorns and thistles" (Genesis 3:18). Thorns are Scripture's oldest image of a world under curse. The Last Adam goes to the cross wearing that curse on his head, and Paul later says exactly what that means: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13). The soldiers were reaching for something painful and available. They handed him the crown that fit.

Why does Matthew say so little about the crucifixion itself?

"And when they had crucified him, they divided his garments among them by casting lots." Matthew 27:35, ESV

The most violent event in Scripture is reported in a subordinate clause. Matthew, who spent seventeen verses on a genealogy, spends five words on the nails. Part of the reason is simply that his readers had seen crucifixions; describing one to a first-century Judean would be like explaining to you what a car crash looks like. But part of it is restraint on purpose. Matthew does not want your emotions worked on by the physical horror. He wants your attention on who is dying and why, and he keeps steering you there: to the title over the head, to the psalm being fulfilled at the foot of the cross, to the words coming out of the crowd. Follow him in this. It is possible to be moved by the suffering of Jesus and never once reckon with the sin that put him there.

Two details in the middle of it deserve a moment. First, Simon of Cyrene. He is a stranger, in town for Passover, seized off the road by soldiers, and he ends up carrying the cross of Christ. Now remember who is not in the frame. Men had said, on this very night, "Even if I must die with you, I will not deny you" (26:35). The one physically bearing the cross beside Jesus is a foreigner under compulsion, and it is hard to read that without hearing 16:24, "let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me."

Second, the wine mixed with gall (v. 34). Jewish custom, reading Proverbs 31:6 mercifully, offered the condemned a drugged wine to dull the pain. Mark identifies the additive as myrrh, a narcotic. Jesus tastes it, recognizes it, and refuses. MacArthur's note here is worth keeping: "Christ, though thirsty, 'would not drink,' lest it dull his senses before he completed his work." He had hours left of speaking, of praying, of committing his mother into another's care, of promising paradise to a dying criminal. He chose to go through it undulled and awake. This is the same will that prayed "not as I will, but as you will" in the garden, still holding.

"He saved others; he cannot save himself." Were they right?

"So also the chief priests, with the scribes and elders, mocked him, saying, 'He saved others; he cannot save himself. He is the King of Israel; let him come down now from the cross, and we will believe in him.'" Matthew 27:41-42, ESV

Yes, and in a sense they never intended. Notice first that they concede the first half: he saved others. Nobody in Jerusalem disputed the miracles; the argument was always about the source of the power (12:24). Then comes the taunt: a savior who cannot save himself is no savior at all. But the sentence is exactly true, and it is true because of the gospel, not against it. He could not save himself and save others. Those were the only two options on the table, and saving others is precisely the reason he would not come down. What they offered as proof of his weakness is the mechanism of the atonement.

Their bargain, "let him come down now from the cross, and we will believe in him," is worth taking seriously for a moment, because people still make it. It sounds like an honest request for evidence. It is not. These are men who had watched him raise the dead (compare their own reaction in John 11:47-53) and had asked for a sign twice already (12:38; 16:1). Unbelief that has explained away the last sign will explain away the next one. The empty tomb three days later did not convert the Sanhedrin; it produced a bribe and a cover story (28:11-15).

Now hear the other refrain, because it is the deepest thing in the passage:

"You who would destroy the temple and rebuild it in three days, save yourself! If you are the Son of God, come down from the cross." Matthew 27:40, ESV

"If you are the Son of God." You have read those words before, in this same Gospel, in the mouth of the devil: "If you are the Son of God, command these stones to become loaves of bread" (4:3), and "If you are the Son of God, throw yourself down" (4:6). The wilderness temptation and the cross temptation are the same temptation, word for word: use your power to get out of the Father's path. It came at the beginning of his ministry with bread and a parapet, and it comes at the end with nails and a crowd, and both times the answer is silence and obedience. Whoever is really speaking through those passersby, the strategy has not changed in three years.

One more echo, easy to miss. Verse 43, "He trusts in God; let God deliver him now, if he desires him," is almost a quotation of Psalm 22:8: "He trusts in the LORD; let him deliver him." The priests are unknowingly reciting the script of the very psalm this crucifixion is fulfilling, the same psalm whose eighteenth verse the soldiers acted out with the clothing eight verses earlier, and whose first line Jesus will cry aloud in tomorrow's passage. This is typology and prophecy converging: the mockers are supplying the lines.

Application

  • About God: he is not embarrassed by the mockery of his Son; he governs it. The robe, the crown, the scepter, and the placard were chosen by men who meant to humiliate a pretender, and each one told the truth. God is able to make even contempt for Christ testify to Christ.
  • About you: "save yourself" is the natural religion of the human heart, and it is the one thing Jesus would not do. If he had saved himself, you would have no Savior. Every time you are tempted to prove yourself, spare yourself, or come down off some cross you have been given, you are hearing an old voice with a long record.
  • Do: take ten minutes today, read verses 27-44 aloud slowly, and stop at every occurrence of the word "king." Say out loud after each one, "and he is." Then do the one thing this week you have been avoiding because it costs you something and nobody will see it: the apology, the visit, the money, the confession.

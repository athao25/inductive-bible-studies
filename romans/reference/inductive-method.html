The Inductive Study Method

The one-page cheat sheet for every daily session. Print this. Lesson 0001 teaches it; every lesson after that uses it.

Inductive means you start with the text itself and draw conclusions out of it, rather than coming to the text to confirm what you already think. Three steps, always in order:

1. Observation: "What does it say?"

Slow down. Read the passage at least twice. You are gathering facts, not meanings yet.

  • Who is speaking? To whom? Who is acted upon? In a letter this matters constantly: Paul writes to a real congregation with real fault lines.
  • What is asserted? What words repeat? What is compared or contrasted?
  • Connectors first. In Romans the little words carry the argument. Circle every therefore, but, for, because, so that, much more, if… then. A paragraph of Romans is a chain of inferences, and the connectors are the links.
  • Diatribe markers. Paul argues with an imagined objector: "What shall we say then?", "By no means!", "But someone will say…". Mark who is speaking each line. Mistaking the objector for Paul is the single most common misreading of this book.
  • Quotations. Romans is stitched with the Old Testament, usually flagged with "as it is written". Note every one; you will chase them in interpretation.
Rule of thumb
If you have fewer than five written observations, you have not looked long enough. Romans rewards the reader who can say what a paragraph argues, not just what it mentions. Try writing each paragraph as a single sentence with its connector intact: "Because x, therefore y."

2. Interpretation: "What does it mean?"

One question rules this step: what did the author intend the original audience to understand? This is called the grammatical-historical method: meaning comes from the grammar of the words and the history of the situation, not from what a verse "means to me".

  • Context is king. Read the paragraph before and after. Then ask where the passage sits in the argument of the whole letter (use the book map). Romans is one continuous case; no paragraph in it is a free-standing motto.
  • Follow the argument, not the verse numbers. Chapter and verse divisions were added centuries later, and in Romans a few of them fall in awkward places (3:20/3:21, 7:25/8:1, 11:36/12:1). Let the logic set the boundaries.
  • Scripture interprets Scripture. The clear governs the unclear. Paul's own quotations are your first cross-references: read Genesis 15 behind Romans 4, Psalm 32 behind 4:6-8, Habakkuk 2:4 behind 1:17, Exodus 33 behind 9:15.
  • Define the terms Paul defines. Righteousness, justify, propitiation, flesh, law, grace, glory. He uses them with precision; the glossary holds the working definitions this course uses.
  • Only now open helps. After you have a working answer, check the MacArthur Study Bible note or a commentary. Helps confirm or correct; they never replace your own look at the text.

3. Application: "What must change?"

Interpretation has one right answer; application has many. But application is not optional. Paul spends eleven chapters on what God has done and then says "therefore… present your bodies" (12:1). The doctrine was always headed somewhere.

  • What does this passage reveal about God: his character, his works, his promises?
  • What does it expose in me: sin to confess, truth to believe, command to obey?
  • Is there a promise here that should change how I face today?
Order matters
Never jump from reading straight to application. Application built on a misreading applies the wrong thing. Observe, interpret, then apply. Romans in particular punishes the shortcut: half the errors made with this letter come from lifting an imperative out of the indicative that grounds it.

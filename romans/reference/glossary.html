Reference

Glossary

Every technical term used in the lessons, defined once, used consistently. Romans is the most vocabulary-dense book in the New Testament; keep this open.

Study method

TermDefinition
Inductive studyDrawing conclusions out of the text by observation, rather than bringing conclusions to it. Three ordered steps: observation, interpretation, application.
ExpositionExplaining a passage's God-intended meaning in its context and pressing it on the hearer. "Expositional" study works through a book verse by verse rather than hopping between topics.
Grammatical-historical methodInterpreting a text by its grammar (what the words and sentences say) and its history (what the original author and audience meant and understood).
ContextThe material surrounding a passage: the paragraph, the section, the book, and the whole Bible. The first question of interpretation.
DiatribeA style of argument, common in ancient moral teaching, in which the writer raises objections from an imagined opponent and answers them. Paul uses it throughout Romans: "What shall we say then?" (6:1; 7:7; 9:14), "By no means!" (me genoito), "But you will say to me…" (9:19; 11:19). Identifying who is speaking is half of reading Romans correctly.
EpistleA letter written to be read aloud to a congregation. Romans has a letter's frame (1:1-15; 15:14-16:27) around the most sustained theological argument in Scripture.
Indicative and imperativeThe indicative states what God has done; the imperative commands what you must do. Paul's order never reverses: eleven chapters of indicative, then "therefore… present your bodies" (12:1). Commands rest on accomplished facts, never the other way round.

Paul's vocabulary in Romans

TermDefinition
GospelGreek euangelion, "good news": the announcement that God has acted in the death and resurrection of his Son to save sinners. In Romans it is God's gospel (1:1), promised beforehand (1:2), about his Son (1:3), and the power of God for salvation (1:16). It is news to be believed before it is advice to be followed.
Righteousness of GodThe letter's key phrase (1:17; 3:21-22; 10:3). It carries God's own righteous character and the righteous standing he gives to those who believe. Both senses meet at 3:26: God is "just and the justifier". This course reads it primarily as the status God credits, granted in a way that vindicates his justice.
JustificationGod's legal declaration that a sinner is righteous in his sight, on the basis of Christ's work, received through faith alone (3:24, 28; 5:1). It is a verdict, not a transformation: a change of standing, once for all, not a change of character by degrees. Contrast sanctification.
ImputationTo credit something to another's account. Three imputations frame Romans: Adam's sin to the race (5:12-19), our sin to Christ at the cross (implied at 3:25; stated at 2 Cor 5:21), and Christ's righteousness to believers (4:3-8, 22-25; 5:19). The Greek word behind "counted" or "reckoned" (logizomai) appears eleven times in chapter 4 alone.
PropitiationA sacrifice that satisfies and turns away wrath (3:25). The Greek hilasterion also names the mercy seat, the lid of the ark sprinkled with blood on the Day of Atonement (Lev 16). Contrast expiation, which means only the removal of sin's stain; Romans requires the fuller word, because Romans has real wrath (1:18) to answer.
RedemptionRelease secured by the payment of a price (3:24). The word's home is the slave market and the exodus: God buys his people out of bondage at cost to himself.
GraceGod's free favour toward those who deserve the opposite. In Romans it is strictly opposed to wages: "to the one who works, his wages are not counted as a gift" (4:4), and "if it is by grace, it is no longer on the basis of works" (11:6).
FaithNot a sentiment or a leap, but trust in a person on the basis of his promise, resting on him rather than on oneself (4:20-21). Faith justifies not because it is a virtue but because of the object it clings to. It is itself God's gift (Eph 2:8-9), which is why boasting is excluded (3:27).
LawPaul uses nomos several ways: the Mosaic covenant law; the Old Testament as a whole (3:19); and, by extension, any principle ("the law of faith", 3:27). The law is holy and good (7:12), was never given to justify (3:20), and functions to expose sin and stop mouths.
Works of the lawDeeds done in obedience to the law as a basis for standing before God. Romans 3:20 denies that any such works can justify: they include, but are not limited to, the boundary markers (circumcision, food, days) that separated Jew from Gentile.
FleshGreek sarx. Sometimes simply human descent or the body (1:3; 9:3), but in chapters 7-8 the word for fallen human nature as it operates apart from the Spirit, hostile to God and unable to please him (8:7-8). "In the flesh" and "in the Spirit" name two whole existences, not two moods.
WrathGod's settled, holy, personal opposition to sin. Not a loss of temper but a judicial reality: presently revealed as he gives sinners over to what they choose (1:18-32), and stored up for the day of judgment (2:5).
GloryThe weight and radiance of God's own excellence. To sin is to fall short of it (3:23); to be saved is to be brought to it (5:2; 8:18, 30), conformed to the image of the Son.
RemnantThe believing minority God preserves within a covenant people under judgment (9:27; 11:5). Paul's proof that God's promises have not failed: they were never made to every physical descendant.
FirstfruitsThe first portion of a harvest, offered to God as the pledge that the whole crop belongs to him. Used of the Spirit in believers as the down payment on resurrection (8:23) and of the patriarchs as the pledge over Israel (11:16).

Theology

TermDefinition
Reformed theologyThe doctrinal stream of the Protestant Reformation: Scripture alone as final authority, salvation by grace alone through faith alone in Christ alone, and God's absolute sovereignty in creation and salvation. Romans is its home text, and the book Luther called "the purest gospel".
General revelationWhat God makes plain about himself to everyone through creation and conscience (1:19-20; 2:14-15). Enough to render every person without excuse; never enough to save, which is why preaching is necessary (10:14-17).
Total depravityNot that people are as wicked as possible, but that sin has touched every faculty, so that no part of a person is left neutral toward God. Romans 3:10-18 is its clearest statement, and 8:7-8 its explanation: the mind of the flesh "cannot" submit to God.
Federal headshipThe doctrine that one man represents many in covenant, so that his act is credited to them. Adam represented the race and his sin brought condemnation to all; Christ represents his people and his obedience brings justification to all who are his (5:12-19). The whole gospel depends on the principle Adam established.
Original sinThe guilt and corruption inherited from Adam by the whole human race (5:12). It is why death reigns over those "whose sinning was not like the transgression of Adam" (5:14), including infants.
ElectionGod's choice, before creation and apart from foreseen merit, of those he will save (8:29-30; 9:11-13; 11:5-7). Romans 9 grounds it in his purpose "not because of works but because of him who calls".
Effectual callingThe inward summons by which God brings the elect to faith, distinguished from the general invitation of the gospel preached to all. In 8:30 everyone called is also justified, so the call in view cannot be one that can fail.
Order of salvation (ordo salutis)The logical sequence of God's saving work in an individual. Romans 8:29-30 gives the backbone: foreknew, predestined, called, justified, glorified. Regeneration, faith, adoption, and sanctification fill it in.
ForeknowledgeIn Paul's usage not bare advance information but covenantal fore-loving, as in "the LORD knew Israel" (Amos 3:2). God foreknows people, not facts about them (8:29; 11:2).
RegenerationGod's act of granting new spiritual life to someone dead in sin. Romans speaks of it in its own terms: newness of life (6:4), the Spirit indwelling (8:9-11), the law written where it can be obeyed.
Union with ChristThe believer's real inclusion in Christ's death, burial, and resurrection, so that what happened to him counts as having happened to them (6:3-11). Paul's answer to "why not go on sinning?" is not a rule but this union.
AdoptionGod's act of receiving believers as sons with full legal standing and inheritance (8:15-17). The Spirit's witness makes it felt: "Abba! Father!" Its consummation is the redemption of the body (8:23).
SanctificationThe lifelong work of God's Spirit making a justified sinner actually holy (6:19, 22; 8:13). Distinguished from justification in kind: justification is a declaration made once, sanctification a process worked out over a life, and confusing them wrecks both.
GlorificationThe final act: resurrection bodies and complete conformity to the image of Christ (8:23, 30). Spoken of in the past tense at 8:30 because in God's purpose it is as good as done.
Perseverance of the saintsThe doctrine that those God justifies he also keeps, so that none is finally lost (8:30, 35-39). Not a promise that believers never stumble, but that God never lets go.
Antinomianism"Against law": the error that because salvation is by grace, holiness does not matter. Paul raises it himself at 6:1 and 6:15 and answers it twice with "By no means!"
LegalismSeeking or maintaining standing before God through law-keeping (10:3). Its root is not moral seriousness but the desire to establish a righteousness of one's own.
HardeningGod's judicial act of confirming the rebellious in their rebellion (9:18; 11:7, 25). It never makes anyone sin who did not want to; it withdraws restraint and gives sinners over to themselves (compare 1:24, 26, 28).
Already and not yetThe tension running through Romans 6-8 and 13: believers are already justified, already dead to sin, already sons, and not yet raised, not yet free of the body's weakness, not yet home. Salvation is "nearer to us now than when we first believed" (13:11).
TypologyA real person, event, or institution in the Old Testament designed by God to prefigure a greater reality in Christ. Paul calls Adam "a type of the one who was to come" (5:14).
CovenantA binding relationship established by God with promises and obligations. Romans works with the Abrahamic promise (ch. 4), the Mosaic law (ch. 7), and the new covenant realities of the Spirit (ch. 8).

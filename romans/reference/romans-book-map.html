The whole letter on one page: argument, structure, themes, and where each unit of the course sits. Come back here whenever a paragraph feels stranded.

The one-sentence summary

God has revealed a righteousness of his own, apart from law and through faith in Jesus Christ, by which he justifies guilty Jews and Gentiles alike without compromising his justice, keeps every promise he ever made to Israel, and produces a people whose whole life becomes worship.

Author and setting

Paul wrote from Corinth around AD 57, near the end of his third missionary journey, dictating to a scribe named Tertius (16:22) and hosted by Gaius (16:23). He was about to carry the Gentile churches' collection to Jerusalem (15:25-27) and hoped afterwards to reach Spain by way of Rome (15:23-24, 28). The church in Rome he had never visited (1:10-13); it was founded by neither apostle, probably by ordinary believers travelling to the capital, and by this date it was mixed but tense. Claudius had expelled Jews from Rome around AD 49 (Acts 18:2); when they returned after his death in 54 they found congregations that had become largely Gentile and had learned to live without them. That fracture stands behind chapters 9-11 and 14-15, and it explains why the most systematic letter in the New Testament is also a profoundly pastoral one.

Why this letter is different
Paul usually writes to churches he planted, about crises he knows first-hand. Here he writes ahead of himself, to a congregation that must decide whether to back his mission. So he lays out the gospel he preaches from the ground up. The result is the fullest statement of Christian doctrine ever written, and the book that has repeatedly set the church back on its feet: Augustine converted at 13:13-14, Luther's tower breakthrough at 1:17, Wesley's heart "strangely warmed" at a reading of Luther's preface to it, Barth's commentary detonating on liberal Europe in 1919.

The argument, paragraph by paragraph

Romans is a chain, and the connectors are the links. Read this list aloud once a week and the letter's spine will stay in place:

  • 1:16-17 The gospel is God's power to save, because in it God's righteousness is revealed, received by faith.
  • 1:18 For God's wrath is also revealed. The good news needs the bad news first.
  • 3:19-20 Therefore every mouth is stopped; law brings knowledge of sin, never acquittal.
  • 3:21 But now (the hinge of the letter) a righteousness apart from law has been manifested.
  • 5:1 Therefore, having been justified, we have peace with God. The verdict has consequences you can live in.
  • 6:1 What shall we say then? Grace does not license sin, because you died with Christ.
  • 8:1 Therefore there is now no condemnation. The answer to chapter 7's cry.
  • 8:31 What then shall we say to these things? Everything since 3:21 is gathered into one unanswerable question.
  • 9:6 But it is not as though the word of God has failed. The thesis of chapters 9-11.
  • 11:36 For from him and through him and to him are all things. The doctrine ends in doxology.
  • 12:1 I appeal to you therefore, by the mercies of God ("therefore" reaching back over eleven chapters) present your bodies.

Romans and the Old Testament

Paul quotes the Old Testament roughly sixty times in this letter, more than in all his other letters combined, and the quotations are load-bearing rather than decorative. Habakkuk 2:4 supplies the theme (1:17). A chain of six psalms and Isaiah passages closes the case for universal guilt (3:10-18). Genesis 15:6 anchors the whole of chapter 4, with Psalm 32:1-2 brought alongside to prove that "counted righteous" and "sins not counted" are the same transaction. Exodus 33:19 and 9:16 stand at the centre of chapter 9, with Jeremiah's potter (Jer 18) and Hosea's "not my people" (Hos 1-2) around them. Deuteronomy 30:12-14 is startlingly reapplied at 10:6-8; Isaiah 28:16 and 8:14 combine into the stone that is both foundation and stumbling block. Elijah's despair at Horeb (1 Kgs 19) grounds the remnant doctrine of chapter 11, and a garland of texts about the nations rejoicing closes the argument at 15:9-12. Whenever a lesson meets a quotation, open the source and read its context. Paul assumes you will.

Where your teachers stand in Romans
On the great matters of this letter (justification by faith alone, imputed righteousness, total inability, unconditional election in chapter 9, and the security of the believer in chapter 8) MacArthur, White, and Durbin agree, and the lessons will say so plainly. Romans 9 is James White's specialist ground; his written and spoken debates on it are the sharpest defence available of the reading this course takes. The live checkpoints, where careful believers differ and the lessons will show the options and argue from the text, are these: whether 2:6-16 describes a real class of law-keeping Gentiles or a hypothetical standard; who the "I" of 7:14-25 is (Paul as a believer, Paul before conversion, or Israel under law dramatized); whether 8:29's "foreknew" means fore-loved or foresaw faith; what "all Israel will be saved" (11:26) includes and when; how far the submission of 13:1-7 extends when rulers command sin; and whether the disputed matters of chapter 14 have modern equivalents. Where MacArthur's dispensational framework shapes his reading of 11:25-27 more than the others', the lessons say so rather than pretending the difference away.

Lesson 0006 · Unit 1: Chapters 1:1-3:20

Romans 1:24-32: God Gave Them Up

~30 min · Passage: Romans 1:24-32 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 1:24-32 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the phrase that repeats three times, what follows each one, the two further "exchanges", how the vice list is arranged, and what verse 32 adds that is worse than doing the things themselves.

Recall first

Observation check

  • Three times: "God gave them up" (vv. 24, 26, 28). The paragraph is built on that refrain. Each occurrence follows a statement of what they did, and each introduces what they were handed over to.
  • Each handing-over follows an exchange. They exchanged the glory of God for images (v. 23) → given up to impurity (v. 24). They exchanged the truth for a lie and worshipped the creature (v. 25) → given up to dishonourable passions (v. 26). They did not see fit to acknowledge God (v. 28) → given up to a debased mind (v. 28).
  • The punishment fits the crime, exactly. They dishonoured God, so their bodies are dishonoured (v. 24). They exchanged the natural for the unnatural in worship, and then in desire. God's judgment here is not an extra penalty attached from outside; it is the sin allowed to become itself.
  • A doxology in the middle of it (v. 25). "The Creator, who is blessed forever! Amen." Paul cannot mention the exchange without blessing the one who was traded away.
  • "Debased mind" is a wordplay (v. 28). They did not see fit (edokimasan) to acknowledge God, so God gave them up to an unfit (adokimon) mind. The verb and the adjective are the same root: they rejected, so they were rejected.
  • The vice list (vv. 29-31) is not exotic. Alongside murder and malice stand envy, gossip, boasting, disobedience to parents, heartlessness. Sins anyone would host at dinner sit in the same list as sins nobody would name in public.
  • "Though they know God's righteous decree" (v. 32). Knowledge again, as in verse 21. Conscience has not been erased.
  • The last line is the sharpest (v. 32). Not only doing them "but give approval to those who practice them". Applause outranks action in the indictment, because approval spreads what action only performs.

Interpretation

What does "God gave them up" mean?

Therefore God gave them up in the lusts of their hearts to impurity, to the dishonoring of their bodies among themselves. Romans 1:24, ESV

The verb paradidomi is a judicial handing-over: the word used of a prisoner delivered to the authorities. It is active: God does something. But notice what he does. He does not inject desires that were not there; verse 24 says he gave them up "in the lusts of their hearts", desires already present and already theirs. What is withdrawn is restraint.

This is the shape of present wrath announced in verse 18, and it is more frightening than fire from heaven, because it feels like freedom. A society being handed over does not experience judgment; it experiences liberation. The tightening spiral looks like progress from the inside. Which means the most terrifying thing God can do to a person short of hell is to stop interfering.

Two guardrails for reading this passage
  • It is a diagnosis of the human race, not a description of "them" over there. Chapter 2 opens with "Therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges": the trap is deliberate. Anyone who reads 1:24-32 with satisfaction has walked into it. Paul builds the case against the pagan world so that the religious reader will nod, and then he turns the whole indictment around.
  • It is about a culture's trajectory, not an individual's biography. Paul is describing what happens to humanity in the aggregate when God is refused, tracing a pattern from Genesis 3 through the golden calf to the Roman street. It is not a claim that every idolater commits every item on the list, or that a given person's particular sin proves a particular history.

Verses 26-27, honestly

For this reason God gave them up to dishonorable passions. For their women exchanged natural relations for those that are contrary to nature; and the men likewise gave up natural relations with women and were consumed with passion for one another. Romans 1:26-27, ESV

Two things must be said, and this course will say both. First, what the text says: Paul presents homosexual practice as one of the clearest examples of the exchange he has been describing, because it visibly inverts the created order given in Genesis 1-2, and he sets it in a chain where the vertical exchange (worshipping the creature rather than the Creator) produces a horizontal one. The logic is creation: "contrary to nature" means contrary to the way God made things, which is why the argument does not depend on cultural convention and cannot be dissolved by pointing out that ancient exploitative practices differed from modern relationships. Attempts to limit the passage to temple prostitution or pederasty run aground on verse 26, which speaks of women, and on the appeal to nature rather than to custom.

Second, what the paragraph does with it. Paul does not stop at verse 27, and neither may we. He runs straight into a list containing envy, gossip, boasting, and heartlessness, and then into chapter 2, where the person nodding along is arraigned. The passage is designed to remove every reader's platform. Anyone who uses verses 26-27 as a weapon has misread the paragraph they came from as thoroughly as anyone who denies what those verses say. The tone to learn here is Paul's own: unflinching about the diagnosis, and unable to speak of it without remembering that "such were some of you… but you were washed" (1 Cor 6:9-11). This is a passage about idolatry's consequences that ends by convicting the respectable, and the whole argument exists to make 3:21-26 necessary for everyone.

Why the vice list looks so domestic

Read verses 29-31 slowly and notice the company that murder keeps. Gossips and slanderers. Boastful, inventors of evil, disobedient to parents. Heartless, ruthless. Paul mixes the spectacular with the ordinary on purpose, because his goal is not to rank sins but to close every exit. If the list held only atrocities, most readers would be safe. It holds envy, so nobody is.

Then verse 32 lands: they know the decree, "yet not only do they do them but give approval to those who practice them." That is a further step down. To sin under conviction is one thing; to build a culture that celebrates it is to enlist others in the ruin. It is also, uncomfortably, the easiest sin on the list to commit from a sofa.

Application

  • About God His restraint is a kindness we never notice. Every day the world is not what it could be is a day he has not yet let go. Present mercy is largely invisible, which is why gratitude has to be deliberate.
  • About you Read verses 29-31 again with a pen and mark the ones nobody would confront you about. Those are the ones to confess today. The list is arranged to catch the respectable.
  • Do Where have you given approval (laughed, shared, stayed silent) at something you know God calls sin? Name one instance, confess it, and if someone was harmed, repair it this week.

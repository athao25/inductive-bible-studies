Lesson 0047 · Unit 5: Chapters 12-16

Romans 14:13-23: Do Not Destroy His Work

~25 min · Passage: Romans 14:13-23 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 14:13-23 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: what Paul says about whether anything is unclean, what makes it unclean for a person, the definition of the kingdom in verse 17, what is not to be destroyed and why, the principle stated in verse 22, and the definition of sin in the final verse.

Recall first

Observation check

  • A redirected verdict (v. 13). "Let us not pass judgment on one another any longer, but rather decide never to put a stumbling block…" The same verb: stop judging others, and pass judgment on your own conduct instead.
  • Paul takes the strong side on the facts (v. 14). "I know and am persuaded in the Lord Jesus that nothing is unclean in itself." He agrees with the strong about the meat.
  • And then relativises it (v. 14). "But it is unclean for anyone who thinks it unclean." Conscience is not infallible, but it is binding on the one who holds it.
  • Love overrides liberty (v. 15). "If your brother is grieved by what you eat, you are no longer walking in love."
  • The stakes named starkly (v. 15). "Do not destroy the one for whom Christ died." Meat set against the cross.
  • "Do not let what you eat cause the loss of one for whom Christ died" (v. 15). And again at verse 20: "do not destroy the work of God for the sake of food."
  • The kingdom defined (v. 17). "Not a matter of eating and drinking but of righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit."
  • Two aims (v. 19). "Pursue what makes for peace and for mutual upbuilding." The same verb as 9:30-31: pursue, the runner's word.
  • Everything is clean, and (v. 20). "Everything is indeed clean, but it is wrong to make another stumble by what you eat." The freedom is granted and then voluntarily limited.
  • The positive form of the principle (v. 21). "It is good not to eat meat or drink wine or do anything that causes your brother to stumble."
  • Keep your conviction between yourself and God (v. 22). "The faith that you have, keep between yourself and God." Not concealment of doctrine but discretion about liberty.
  • A definition of sin (v. 23). "Whoever has doubts is condemned if he eats, because the eating is not from faith. For whatever does not proceed from faith is sin."

Interpretation

Right about the thing, wrong about the person

I know and am persuaded in the Lord Jesus that nothing is unclean in itself, but it is unclean for anyone who thinks it unclean. Romans 14:14, ESV

Paul does not sit on the fence. He states his own position clearly: nothing is unclean in itself, so the food laws no longer bind, and the strong are correct. He says "everything is indeed clean" again at verse 20, and calls the scrupulous "weak in faith". So this chapter is not relativism about the facts.

What it does is separate two questions that we habitually merge: am I right? and what does love require? Being right about a disputed matter does not settle what to do. The strong may eat, and if eating in front of a brother wounds him, "you are no longer walking in love" (v. 15). Correct theology exercised without love is a failure of obedience, not a triumph of it.

Then note the weight Paul puts on the other side of the scale. Verse 15: "do not destroy the one for whom Christ died." Verse 20: "do not destroy the work of God for the sake of food." He deliberately sets the trivial thing beside the most expensive thing in the universe. Whatever "destroy" means at its outer edge (and the strong verb should not be softened) the argument is one of proportion: you are risking damage to a person Christ died for, over dinner.

Doctrine checkpoint: conscience, and how it works
Romans 14 is the New Testament's fullest treatment of conscience. Five conclusions the chapter supports:
  • Conscience is not infallible. The weak believer thinks the meat is unclean, and Paul says it is not (v. 14). A conscience can be mistaken about the facts.
  • Conscience is binding anyway. To act against your conviction is to act without faith, and "whatever does not proceed from faith is sin" (v. 23). So violating a mistaken conscience is still sin, not because the act is wrong but because you did it against your own conviction before God.
  • Therefore conscience should be educated, not overridden. The right course for a weak conscience is instruction from Scripture until it is "fully convinced" (14:5), never pressure from the strong to act against it.
  • And liberty is to be limited voluntarily. "It is good not to eat meat or drink wine or do anything that causes your brother to stumble" (v. 21). Freedom includes the freedom not to use your freedom, which is exactly what Christ did (15:3).
  • Discretion is commanded, not silence. "The faith that you have, keep between yourself and God" (v. 22) is about not parading a liberty, not about hiding convictions when they are asked for.
A practical corollary worth naming: this chapter cuts against the modern instinct to "help" scrupulous believers by pressing them to do the thing they fear. Paul's method is teaching and patience, and in the meantime the stronger party adjusts.

What the kingdom is about

For the kingdom of God is not a matter of eating and drinking but of righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit. Romans 14:17, ESV

One of the great clarifying sentences in Paul. The kingdom is not constituted by diet, calendar, or any of the visible markers that congregations divide over. It is righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit, and all three of those, note, are things Romans has already established: righteousness in chapters 3-5, peace at 5:1, joy and the Spirit at 5:2-5 and chapter 8.

So the argument is not "these things are too small to matter". It is "these things are not what the kingdom consists of, and you are letting them displace what it does consist of". A congregation that has sacrificed peace over food has traded a kingdom reality for a non-kingdom one.

Then verse 19 supplies the direction of travel: "pursue what makes for peace and for mutual upbuilding." Peace is not a mood that settles when arguments stop; it is something you chase. And "upbuilding" (construction language) sets the criterion for decisions in disputed areas: not "may I?" but "does this build up?"

"Whatever does not proceed from faith is sin"

The chapter's last sentence is one of the most searching definitions of sin in Scripture, and its immediate application is narrow: a person who eats while doubting is condemned, because the eating did not come from faith. But the principle as stated is general, and it is worth taking generally. An act that does not proceed from trust in God (however outwardly correct) is not obedience.

That cuts in a direction most of us do not expect. It means two people can do the identical thing and only one of them is obeying. It also means that a great deal of respectable religious activity, done out of habit, fear, or social pressure rather than faith, falls under this sentence. Romans has said from 1:17 onward that everything in the Christian life runs on faith; here it turns out that even a dinner does.

Application

  • About God He counts the wounding of a weak believer as a serious matter: serious enough to be measured against the death of his Son.
  • About you Where have you been right and unloving? Verse 15 says that combination is not a partial success but a failure to walk in love.
  • Do Identify one liberty you could set aside this month for someone else's sake, and do it without telling them. That is verse 22 and verse 21 together.

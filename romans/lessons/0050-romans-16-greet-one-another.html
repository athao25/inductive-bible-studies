Lesson 0050 · Unit 5: Chapters 12-16

Romans 16: Greet One Another

~30 min · Passage: Romans 16 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 16 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now: the whole chapter, names included. Write at least five observations: who carries the letter and how she is described, roughly how many people are named and how many are women, what is said about Prisca and Aquila, the warning in verses 17-20 and its promise, who Tertius is, and the shape of the closing doxology.

Recall first

Observation check

  • Phoebe first (vv. 1-2). "Our sister… a servant of the church at Cenchreae": the Greek is diakonos. She is almost certainly the letter's courier, and Paul asks the Romans to help her "in whatever she may need from you".
  • She is called a "patron" (v. 2). Prostatis: a benefactor or protector, a term of standing in the Roman world. She had supported Paul himself.
  • Twenty-six individuals greeted by name, plus households. For a church Paul had never visited, the list is remarkable, and it proves how well connected the early churches were.
  • At least nine of them are women. Prisca, Mary, Junia, Tryphaena, Tryphosa, Persis, Julia, the mother of Rufus, the sister of Nereus, along with Phoebe.
  • Prisca before Aquila (v. 3). The wife is named first, as in Acts 18:18, 26. They "risked their necks" for Paul and host a church in their house.
  • Named house churches (vv. 5, 14, 15). Several separate gatherings, which explains how a divided congregation could stay divided: they met in different homes.
  • Andronicus and Junia (v. 7). "My kinsmen and my fellow prisoners… well known to the apostles, and they were in Christ before me."
  • Descriptions are personal, not formulaic. "Worked hard in the Lord", "my beloved", "the first convert in Asia", "approved in Christ", "chosen in the Lord". Paul remembers specifics.
  • Social range. Likely slave names (Ampliatus, Urbanus, Hermes), a possible aristocratic household ("those who belong to the family of Aristobulus"), and Rufus' mother: a whole social cross-section in one room.
  • "Greet one another with a holy kiss" (v. 16). A command aimed at the same congregation that had been despising and judging each other in chapter 14.
  • A sharp warning (vv. 17-18). "Watch out for those who cause divisions… by smooth talk and flattery they deceive the hearts of the naive." Not disputable matters this time but false teaching.
  • An Eden promise (v. 20). "The God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet": Genesis 3:15 applied to the church.
  • Tertius signs (v. 22). The scribe greets them in his own name, the only place in Scripture where an amanuensis does so.
  • The doxology (vv. 25-27). The mystery now disclosed, the prophetic writings, all nations, "the obedience of faith" (the same phrase as 1:5) and "to the only wise God be glory forevermore through Jesus Christ! Amen."

Interpretation

Why the names are not filler

It is tempting to skim this chapter, and skimming it loses something Paul thought worth the parchment. Consider what the list demonstrates.

It grounds the letter in real people. Sixteen chapters of the profoundest theology ever written end with two dozen names, several house addresses, and a request to look after a woman travelling from Corinth. Romans is not an abstraction; it was carried by someone, read aloud in someone's living room, and applied to people whose quarrels Paul knew about.

It shows a church that crossed every social line in Rome. Slaves and free, Jews and Gentiles, women and men, a possible member of an aristocratic household beside people with names common among the enslaved. That is precisely the congregation chapters 9-11 and 14-15 were written for, and its very membership list is an argument that the gospel had done what Paul said it did.

It shows how much of the work was done by women. Phoebe is a diakonos of her church and a patron of Paul's; Prisca is named before her husband and taught Apollos (Acts 18:26); Mary, Tryphaena, Tryphosa and Persis "worked hard in the Lord", a phrase Paul uses of his own labour. Whatever conclusions a reader reaches about church office from 1 Timothy 2 and elsewhere, this chapter makes it impossible to describe the apostolic churches as places where women were passive.

Doctrine checkpoint: Junia, and reading a hard verse honestly
Verse 7 greets "Andronicus and Junia… well known to the apostles" (ESV), and two questions are debated.
  • Is Junia a woman? Almost certainly yes. The name is well attested for women in Latin inscriptions, the masculine "Junias" is not attested at all, and the Greek fathers (Chrysostom among them) read her as a woman.
  • Is she called an apostle? The Greek phrase can mean "well known to the apostles" (ESV) or "outstanding among the apostles" (as many translations render it). Both are grammatically possible, and scholars divide.
  • And "apostle" has a range. The word means "sent one". It designates the Twelve and Paul in the strict sense, and elsewhere it covers commissioned messengers of churches (2 Cor 8:23; Phil 2:25). If Junia is called an apostle, the second sense is available.
  • How to handle it. Do not settle a disputed translation by which answer suits your position, and do not build a doctrine of office on a single ambiguous phrase. What the verse certainly says is that a woman was Paul's kinswoman, was imprisoned for the gospel, and was in Christ before he was. That is not a small thing to be told about someone.
This is a good place to practise the method the course has taught: state what is certain, name what is disputed, give the evidence on each side, and refuse to pretend the difficulty away.

A warning, and why it belongs here

I appeal to you, brothers, to watch out for those who cause divisions and create obstacles contrary to the doctrine that you have been taught; avoid them. Romans 16:17, ESV

After all the welcoming of chapter 14, verses 17-18 look abrupt: watch out, avoid them. But the difference in category is exact and it is the key to both passages. Chapter 14 concerned believers who differed over food and days, and the command was to welcome. Chapter 16 concerns people who teach "contrary to the doctrine that you have been taught", who serve "their own appetites", and who work "by smooth talk and flattery". The first group are brothers with scruples; the second are wolves with charm.

Note the diagnostic Paul supplies: smooth talk, flattery, and self-service. False teachers in the New Testament are rarely identified first by exotic doctrine; they are identified by what they are after and how they operate on people. And the naive are their target, which is a reason for ordinary believers to know their doctrine well enough not to be flattered out of it. Sixteen chapters of Romans is a decent inoculation.

Then verse 20: "the God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet." Paul takes Genesis 3:15 (the serpent-crusher promised in Eden) and applies its outcome to this congregation. The letter that began with humanity handed over to its idols ends with the church's foot on the serpent's head.

The doxology, and the phrase that closes the circle

to bring about the obedience of faith: to the only wise God be glory forevermore through Jesus Christ! Amen. Romans 16:26-27, ESV

Read 1:1-5 and 16:25-27 side by side and you will see Paul close his own circle. Both speak of the gospel, of what was promised through the prophets in the Scriptures, of all the nations, and of "the obedience of faith". The letter is bracketed by the same phrase, which is as clear a statement of authorial intent as any book of Scripture provides: Romans exists to produce faith that obeys, among all the nations, to the glory of God.

And notice the last words. Not a summary, not an exhortation: glory to God through Jesus Christ, and Amen. The second doxology of the letter (after 11:36), and the same destination. Sixteen chapters of the hardest thinking in Scripture end where chapter 11 ended, and where the reader is meant to end: worshipping.

Application

  • About God He builds his church out of named individuals (a courier, a tentmaking couple, a scribe, someone's mother) and their names are in Scripture forever.
  • About you Paul remembered specifics about people he had never met. Could you say something true and particular about twenty-six people in your congregation? That is a measure of love, not memory.
  • Do Write down five people in your church and one specific thing each has done for the Lord. Then tell at least one of them this week. Romans 16 is a model of that habit, and it is nearly a lost art.

Lesson 0015 · Unit 2: Chapters 3:21-5:21

Romans 4:9-17: Father of Us All

~25 min · Passage: Romans 4:9-17 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 4:9-17 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the question about timing in verse 10, what circumcision is called in verse 11, the two groups Abraham fathers, why the promise could not come through the law, what the law brings according to verse 15, and the two things God is said to do in verse 17.

Recall first

Observation check

  • The question of timing (vv. 9-10). "Was it before or after he had been circumcised?" Paul's whole argument rests on a date, and he answers his own question: "It was not after, but before."
  • The chronology is decisive. Genesis 15:6 records the counting; Genesis 17 records the circumcision. On the traditional reckoning, at least fourteen years separate them: Ishmael is born in between, and Abraham is 86 in Genesis 16 and 99 in Genesis 17.
  • Circumcision is defined (v. 11). "A sign… a seal of the righteousness that he had by faith while he was still uncircumcised." A sign points; a seal certifies. Neither creates.
  • Two fatherhoods, one condition (vv. 11-12). Father of all who believe without being circumcised, and father of the circumcised who "also walk in the footsteps of the faith". Ancestry is redefined by the footsteps, not the flesh.
  • "The promise… did not come through the law" (v. 13). Another chronological argument: the promise preceded Sinai by 430 years (Gal 3:17).
  • "Heir of the world" (v. 13). Not "heir of Canaan". Paul reads the land promise as pointing beyond itself, an idea he will develop at 8:17-21.
  • If law, then faith is void (v. 14). Two systems cannot both be operative; making the promise depend on law would nullify both faith and promise.
  • "The law brings wrath" (v. 15). With a striking corollary: "where there is no law there is no transgression." Sin exists without law; transgression (the crossing of a stated line) requires one.
  • "That is why it depends on faith, in order that the promise may rest on grace" (v. 16). The purpose clause names the reason for the whole design: guarantee. Faith is the route so that the promise can be secure.
  • God defined by two acts (v. 17). He "gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist." Resurrection and creation, and both will matter in the next paragraph.

Interpretation

An argument that turns on a calendar

How then was it counted to him? Was it before or after he had been circumcised? It was not after, but before he was circumcised. Romans 4:10, ESV

This is one of the most quietly devastating arguments in the New Testament, and it depends entirely on careful reading of the Genesis narrative. If Abraham had been declared righteous after receiving the covenant sign, the sign could plausibly be a condition. Because the declaration came first (by years) circumcision cannot be the ground of anything. It was added later as a seal: an official certification of a status already granted, like a stamp on a document that was already valid.

The pastoral payoff arrives in verses 11-12 and it is aimed straight at Rome. Abraham is the father of two groups: those who believe without the sign, and those who have the sign and walk in the footsteps of his faith. Note carefully what that second clause does. It does not say "the circumcised". It says the circumcised who also believe. Physical descent from Abraham without Abraham's faith produces no relationship at all: a point that will become the thesis of chapter 9.

The modern equivalents are easy to name and uncomfortable to apply. Baptism, church membership, a Christian upbringing, a signed card: all of these can be genuine signs and seals of a real faith, and none of them can substitute for the faith they signify. Ask any of them the Romans 4 question: did this come before or after believing?, and the answer settles what it can and cannot do.

Doctrine checkpoint: "where there is no law there is no transgression" (4:15)
This sentence is easy to misuse, so state it carefully.
  • It does not mean sin does not exist without law. Romans 5:13-14 says explicitly that "sin indeed was in the world before the law was given", and death reigned from Adam to Moses over people whose sinning was not like Adam's transgression. Sin is real; God's standard is not created by its publication.
  • It means transgression (the crossing of an explicitly drawn line) requires the line. The law converts sin into documented rebellion, which is why 5:20 says the law "came in to increase the trespass" and 7:7-13 will describe the mechanism in personal terms.
  • Hence "the law brings wrath". Attaching the promise to law-keeping would guarantee failure, because the very instrument would multiply the offence. That is why verse 16 says the promise depends on faith "in order that" it may rest on grace and be guaranteed.
Notice the logic of verse 16: faith is not chosen as the route because it is a nobler quality than obedience. It is chosen because it is the only route on which the promise can be certain. Any condition we contribute makes the outcome as reliable as we are.

The God who raises the dead and calls things into existence

in the presence of the God in whom he believed, who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist. Romans 4:17, ESV

Paul defines the object of Abraham's faith, and the definition is chosen for the story. Abraham was asked to believe a promise about offspring when his body was "as good as dead" (v. 19) and Sarah's womb was barren. So the God he trusted had to be one who gives life to the dead and speaks non-existent things into being. Both descriptions come straight from Genesis: creation by word in chapter 1, and the son given from two dead bodies in chapter 21.

Two lines run out of this verse into the rest of Romans. First, faith is not a leap in the dark but a response to a promise, and its strength is entirely a function of who made the promise. Abraham's faith is commended not because it was intense but because it was accurately aimed. Second, the God described here is precisely the God of the gospel: verse 24 will say we believe "in him who raised from the dead Jesus our Lord". The pattern is the same in both testaments: God speaks into deadness, and life follows.

There is a third implication worth carrying with you. If God calls into existence the things that do not exist, then the standing he grants is not a recognition of something already there. He does not credit righteousness because he detects a trace of it. He speaks and it is so, which is the only kind of God who could justify the ungodly.

Application

  • About God He arranged the sequence (promise before law, faith before sign) so that the outcome would be guaranteed rather than contingent. His design is aimed at your assurance.
  • About you Which of your signs and seals do you quietly treat as the substance? Name it, thank God for it, and then put it back in the category of verse 11.
  • Do Read Genesis 15, 16 and 17:1-14 today with an eye on the calendar. Note Abraham's age in 16:16 and 17:1, and let the gap between the counting and the cutting do its work.

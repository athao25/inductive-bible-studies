Lesson 0019 · Unit 2: Chapters 3:21-5:21

Romans 5:12-21: Adam and Christ

~35 min · Passage: Romans 5:12-21 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 5:12-21 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: how sin and death entered the world and how far they spread, what verse 12 says about "because all sinned", the argument of verses 13-14 about the period before Moses, the word applied to Adam in verse 14, the repeated "much more", and the two reigns contrasted in verse 21.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "Therefore, just as…" (v. 12). Paul begins a comparison and then breaks off. The "so also" does not arrive until verse 18, after a long parenthesis. Mark the interruption; it is why this paragraph feels hard.
  • The sequence (v. 12). Sin entered through one man; death through sin; death spread to all "because all sinned". Four claims in one sentence.
  • The tense of "all sinned" (v. 12). Aorist: a completed past act, not "all go on sinning". Paul is pointing to a single event in which all were involved.
  • The parenthesis (vv. 13-14). Sin was in the world before the law, but is not "counted where there is no law". Yet death reigned from Adam to Moses, even over those whose sinning was not like Adam's transgression.
  • The evidence is death itself. If death reigned over people who had no explicit command to break, they must have been implicated in someone else's breach.
  • "Adam, who was a type of the one who was to come" (v. 14). The word is typos: a pattern or mould. Adam's structure prefigures Christ's.
  • "But the free gift is not like the trespass" (v. 15). Similarity and dissimilarity together: the pattern matches, the scale does not.
  • Two asymmetries (vv. 15-17). One trespass brought condemnation; the gift followed many trespasses and brought justification. And where death "reigned", the recipients of grace "reign in life": persons on the throne, not just a power.
  • The comparison finally completes (vv. 18-19). "As one trespass led to condemnation for all men, so one act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all men." And "by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous."
  • The law's role (v. 20). "The law came in to increase the trespass": a purpose clause that will need chapter 7 to unpack.
  • "Grace abounded all the more" (v. 20). The Greek intensifies: super-abounded. Sin has a ceiling; grace does not.
  • Two reigns (v. 21). "Sin reigned in death"; "grace also might reign through righteousness leading to eternal life." Every human being lives under one of the two.

Interpretation

Why the whole race is involved in one man's act

Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all sinned: Romans 5:12, ESV

The debated phrase is the last one. Does "because all sinned" mean each person is condemned for their own personal sins, or that all sinned in Adam when he sinned? Three considerations settle it in favour of the second.

  • The tense. "All sinned" is a completed past action, not a continuing habit. Paul is pointing at one moment.
  • The parenthesis exists to make this point. Verses 13-14 argue that death reigned over people between Adam and Moses whose sinning "was not like the transgression of Adam": people with no explicit command to break. If death is the wage of personal transgression only, their deaths are unexplained. The fact that they died proves they were implicated in a breach not their own.
  • The comparison requires it. The whole passage runs on a parallel: as we are condemned in Adam, so we are justified in Christ. If our condemnation rests on our own acts, then by the logic of the parallel our justification rests on our own acts too, and the entire argument since 3:21 collapses.

This is the doctrine of federal headship: one man represents many in covenant, so that his act counts for them. Adam was not merely the first sinner but the appointed head of the race, and his fall was ours. Modern readers instinctively protest that this is unfair. Two replies are worth weighing. First, the arrangement is the only one under which salvation by a representative is possible: you cannot object to being represented by Adam and then ask to be saved by the obedience of another. Second, no one is condemned for Adam's sin alone; we ratify it constantly, which is what chapters 1-3 documented at length.

Doctrine checkpoint: "justification and life for all men" (5:18)
Verse 18 says one act of righteousness leads to "justification and life for all men", and verse 19 says "the many will be made righteous". Does this teach universal salvation?
  • No, and verse 17 shows why. The gift is received: "those who receive the abundant provision of grace and the free gift of righteousness". The whole letter has said the righteousness is "for all who believe" (3:22).
  • The "all" in each half is defined by its head. All who are in Adam are condemned; all who are in Christ are justified. The parallel is between two representative humanities, not between two identical head counts. Paul is contrasting the manner of the two transactions, not asserting the same membership for both.
  • Read alongside the rest of the letter. Romans 2:5-8 describes wrath on the day of judgment; 9:22-23 speaks of vessels of wrath and vessels of mercy. A universalist reading of 5:18 would contradict the argument on either side of it.
Keep the asymmetry Paul emphasises. His point is not that the two groups are the same size but that the second transaction is greater in every respect: one trespass produced condemnation, while grace came after many trespasses and produced acquittal; death reigned, but the saved reign in life.

The gift is not like the trespass

For if, because of one man's trespass, death reigned through that one man, much more will those who receive the abundance of grace and the free gift of righteousness reign in life through the one man Jesus Christ. Romans 5:17, ESV

Paul builds the parallel and then keeps breaking it in one direction. Notice the asymmetries: the trespass was one, the gift answers many trespasses (v. 16); the verdict from one sin was condemnation, the verdict after countless sins is justification (v. 16); death reigned over us, but we reign in life (v. 17). Every comparison tilts toward grace, which is why the paragraph's refrain is "much more".

Verse 17 contains the sharpest turn. The symmetrical thing to say would be "so also life will reign through Christ". Paul says something better: the recipients of grace "reign in life". Under Adam we were subjects of a tyrant; under Christ we are not merely rescued subjects of a kinder king but co-heirs installed on a throne (8:17). Salvation restores more than Adam lost.

Then verse 20 answers a question you may not have thought to ask: if the fall was through Adam and the remedy is through Christ, why the law at all? "The law came in to increase the trespass." Not to fix the problem but to make it undeniable: to turn sin into documented transgression so that no one could mistake the situation for something a little discipline might handle. And immediately: "but where sin increased, grace abounded all the more." Sin is finite. Grace is not. That is the last word of the unit, and it is also the sentence that provokes the objection Paul takes up in 6:1.

Application

  • About God He deals with humanity through representatives, and the second representative is better than the first was ruinous. That is a design, not a repair.
  • About you You are in one of two men. There is no third category and no neutral ground: a thought that clarifies most questions about your standing.
  • Do Read Genesis 3 today alongside this paragraph, and write one sentence on what Adam was given, what he was told, and what he did. Then say verse 21 aloud and note which reign you live under.

In your MacArthur Study Bible, read the notes on 5:12 (because all sinned), 5:14 (type), and 5:18-19 (the two "all"s). This is a paragraph where a good study note earns its place, after you have wrestled with the text yourself.
Church prompt
Ask someone whether they think it is fair to be counted guilty in Adam. Then ask whether they would like to be counted righteous in Christ. Holding the two questions together is the whole lesson.

Questions welcome, always: "does 5:18 teach universal salvation?", "why did God let Adam represent us?", "what about infants who die?" Ask your teacher; on the third, note that 5:14 says death reigned even over those who did not transgress as Adam did, which is exactly why the doctrine matters pastorally.

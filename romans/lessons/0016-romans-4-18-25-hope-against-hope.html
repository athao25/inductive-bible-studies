Lesson 0016 · Unit 2: Chapters 3:21-5:21

Romans 4:18-25: Hope Against Hope

~25 min · Passage: Romans 4:18-25 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 4:18-25 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: what Abraham hoped against and in what, the two dead things he considered, what he did instead of wavering, what "fully convinced" is convinced about, the shift from "him alone" to "us also" in verses 23-24, and the two clauses of verse 25.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "In hope he believed against hope" (v. 18). A deliberate paradox: against every natural ground for hope, on the ground of a word.
  • The promise is quoted (v. 18). "So shall your offspring be": Genesis 15:5, spoken under the stars. The content of his faith was a specific sentence God had said.
  • "He did not weaken in faith when he considered his own body" (v. 19). Faith here is not denial. He considered the facts: a hundred-year-old body and Sarah's dead womb.
  • "No unbelief made him waver" (v. 20). Yet Genesis records laughter (17:17), and Sarah's laughter too (18:12), and the Hagar detour of chapter 16. Paul is describing the settled direction of a life, not a flawless record.
  • "He grew strong in his faith as he gave glory to God" (v. 20). Strength came through worship. The connection is causal, not decorative.
  • "Fully convinced that God was able to do what he had promised" (v. 21). The content of the conviction is God's power and reliability, not Abraham's own resources or feelings.
  • "That is why his faith was counted to him as righteousness" (v. 22). The chapter's thesis restated after the demonstration.
  • The pivot to us (vv. 23-24). "The words 'it was counted to him' were not written for his sake alone, but for ours also." The Old Testament was written with later readers in view.
  • The object of our faith (v. 24). "Him who raised from the dead Jesus our Lord." The same God as verse 17, now identified by a specific act in history.
  • The two clauses of verse 25. "Delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification." Two events, two purposes, joined by a parallel that is worth a lesson on its own.

Interpretation

What faith actually is, described from life

No unbelief made him waver concerning the promise of God, but he grew strong in his faith as he gave glory to God, fully convinced that God was able to do what he had promised. Romans 4:20-21, ESV

Chapter 4 has argued that faith is not a work. Now Paul shows what it is instead, and three things stand out.

Faith looks at the facts. Verse 19 says Abraham "considered his own body, which was as good as dead". This is not positive thinking or a refusal to acknowledge difficulty. He counted the obstacles accurately and then weighed them against a promise, and found the promise heavier. Faith that requires you to pretend the situation is fine is not the biblical article; it is a technique, and it collapses when the facts assert themselves.

Faith rests on a word. The content of his conviction was "that God was able to do what he had promised". Not a general optimism about how things tend to work out, but confidence attached to a specific sentence spoken by a specific God. That is why the strength of your faith is largely a question of how well you know what God has actually said.

Faith grows by worship. "He grew strong in his faith as he gave glory to God." The order in that clause is worth noticing: giving glory is the means, growing strong the result. We usually try to reverse it, waiting to feel confident before we worship. Abraham's route ran the other way, and it is available on any morning when the facts look like a dead body and a barren womb.

And a pastoral note on verse 20, because honest readers stumble here. Genesis says Abraham laughed and Sarah laughed, and the Hagar episode was a serious failure of trust. Paul is not rewriting the history; he is characterising a life. The New Testament regularly evaluates believers by the direction of their faith rather than by its worst moments: Hebrews 11 does the same for Samson and Jephthah. If your assurance depends on never having wavered, it was never resting where Abraham's rested.

Doctrine checkpoint: "raised for our justification" (4:25)
"Delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification." The parallel is tight, and it raises a question: if the cross paid for sin, what does the resurrection contribute to justification?
  • The resurrection is God's receipt. If Christ had stayed in the tomb, the payment would have been unproven, indeed disproven, since death holding him would mean sin still held him. His release is the public evidence that the debt was accepted as paid. Paul says as much elsewhere: "if Christ has not been raised… you are still in your sins" (1 Cor 15:17).
  • The two clauses are not a division of labour. Paul is not saying the cross handled trespasses and the resurrection handled justification as separate transactions. Hebrew parallelism holds them together: one saving act with two moments, and the whole of it accomplishes the whole of our salvation.
  • The living Christ is the justified one, and we are in him. Chapter 5 will show that his obedience is credited to many; chapter 6 will show that we were united to both his death and his rising; chapter 8 will point to him at God's right hand interceding. The verdict is not filed in an archive but held by a living person.
Practically: when assurance wobbles, Romans 4:25 sends you to look not at your faith but at an empty tomb. The evidence that your sins were paid for is located outside you, in an event with a date.

Written for us also

But the words "it was counted to him" were not written for his sake alone, but for ours also. It will be counted to us who believe in him who raised from the dead Jesus our Lord. Romans 4:23-24, ESV

This is a statement about how to read the Old Testament, and it is worth pausing on. Paul says Genesis 15:6 was recorded with future readers in view. Not merely that it happens to be applicable, but that God intended it to instruct people who would live millennia later: the same conviction he states plainly at 15:4, "whatever was written in former days was written for our instruction."

Notice too the exact parallel Paul draws between Abraham's faith and ours. Abraham believed in the God who gives life to the dead and calls into being what does not exist (v. 17); we believe in the God who raised Jesus from the dead (v. 24). Same God, same kind of promise, same kind of trust, only now the promise has a name and a date. Old and New Testament believers are not saved by two different systems. There is one gospel, believed in advance by Abraham and in retrospect by us.

Application

  • About God He is able to do what he has promised (v. 21). Not merely willing, not merely well-intentioned: able. Most anxiety is a quiet doubt about that adjective.
  • About you Name the "dead body" in your situation: the fact that makes the promise look absurd. Then do what verse 19 does: consider it honestly, and put the promise on the other side of the scale.
  • Do Practise verse 20's order today. Before you feel strong, give glory to God for one specific thing he has promised and has not yet done. Say it aloud. Strength came that way for Abraham.

Lesson 0045 · Unit 5: Chapters 12-16

Romans 13:8-14: Put On the Lord Jesus

~25 min · Passage: Romans 13:8-14 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 13:8-14 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the one debt that is never paid off, which commandments are quoted and which single command sums them up, the argument from the time of day, the two sets of clothing named, and the final instruction about the flesh.

Recall first

Observation check

  • A bridge from taxes to love (v. 8). "Owe no one anything, except to love each other." The debt language continues directly from 13:7.
  • The one permanent debt. Every other obligation can be discharged; love never is. You do not finish loving your neighbour the way you finish paying a bill.
  • "The one who loves another has fulfilled the law" (v. 8). Fulfilled, not abolished. Compare 8:4 and Matthew 5:17.
  • Four commandments quoted (v. 9). Adultery, murder, theft, coveting: the second table of the Decalogue, all neighbour-directed.
  • "And any other commandment" (v. 9). Paul does not treat the list as exhaustive; he treats it as illustrative of a principle.
  • The summary (v. 9). Leviticus 19:18: "You shall love your neighbour as yourself." The same summary Jesus gives (Matt 22:39).
  • Love's negative test (v. 10). "Love does no wrong to a neighbour." Not a feeling but a floor: whatever else love does, it does not harm.
  • An argument from the clock (v. 11). "Salvation is nearer to us now than when we first believed." Time has passed; the night is further along.
  • "Wake from sleep" (v. 11). Sleep, night, day, waking: an extended metaphor running to verse 13.
  • Two wardrobes (vv. 12-14). "Cast off the works of darkness" and "put on the armour of light"; then, more personally, "put on the Lord Jesus Christ."
  • Six vices in three pairs (v. 13). Orgies and drunkenness, sexual immorality and sensuality, quarrelling and jealousy. Note that the last pair is as serious as the first.
  • The last clause (v. 14). "Make no provision for the flesh, to gratify its desires." A tactical instruction: cut the supply lines.

Interpretation

The debt that never clears

Owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law. Romans 13:8, ESV

Verse 7 said to pay everyone what is owed: taxes, revenue, respect, honour. Verse 8 says to let no debt stand, with one exception. Love is the account you can never close.

Then Paul makes a claim about the law that fits everything the letter has said. He has argued that no one is justified by law (3:20), that believers are released from it (7:6), and that its righteous requirement is fulfilled in those who walk by the Spirit (8:4). Now: the one who loves has fulfilled the law. There is no contradiction here as long as you keep the questions distinct. As a way of gaining standing, the law is finished. As a description of what pleases God, it is fulfilled by love, because every neighbour-directed command it contains describes something love would not do anyway.

That is the force of verse 10's negative formulation: "love does no wrong to a neighbour." Notice how much clearer this makes the commandments. An adulterer, a thief, a murderer, and a coveter are all doing wrong to a neighbour; love rules each out at the root rather than by rule. But note the direction of the logic: love does not replace the commandments so that anything sincere becomes acceptable. Paul quotes the commandments as the content of what love does. Anyone who claims to love while doing what those commands forbid has redefined the word.

Doctrine checkpoint: how love "fulfils" the law
Three ways to get this wrong, and the right one.
  • Wrong: love replaces the law, so specific commands no longer matter. Paul quotes four commandments in the same breath. If love made them irrelevant, citing them would be pointless.
  • Wrong: love is a new law, a simpler code to be kept for merit. That would restart the whole works system Romans 3-4 dismantled, only with one rule instead of six hundred.
  • Wrong: love is a feeling, so intentions settle everything. Verse 10's test is conduct ("does no wrong") not sincerity.
  • Right: love is the substance the commandments describe. They spell out in cases what love does and refuses; love supplies the motive and disposition that the letter of the law can require but never create. Which is exactly 8:4: the Spirit produces in believers what the law demanded and could not generate. Love is not a lower standard than the commandments; it reaches further, since it obliges you to positive good the law could never legislate.

What time it is

Besides this you know the time, that the hour has come for you to wake from sleep. For salvation is nearer to us now than when we first believed. Romans 13:11, ESV

Paul's motive for holiness here is eschatological, and it is worth noticing how ordinary his reasoning is: time has passed since you believed, therefore the end is closer, therefore wake up. The night is far gone; the day is at hand. Christians live in the last hour before dawn, and the appropriate posture is that of someone who knows morning is coming.

This is the already and not yet from chapter 8, applied to behaviour. We have the firstfruits and we groan (8:23); it is night and the day is at hand (13:12). Which means Christian ethics is not a matter of keeping rules until you die but of dressing for the day that is arriving. The clothing image makes that concrete: the works of darkness are night clothes, and you do not go on wearing them once the sky is lightening.

And note the vice list of verse 13. Orgies and drunkenness first, then sexual immorality and sensuality: the sins any respectable reader would condemn. And then, in the same breath and the same list, quarrelling and jealousy. In a congregation split along ethnic lines and arguing about food and days, that placement is not accidental. Church infighting appears in Paul's catalogue of night-time behaviour alongside debauchery, which most of us would never have grouped together.

Two garments, one strategy

But put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh, to gratify its desires. Romans 13:14, ESV

The chapter's final verse is the one that converted Augustine. In a Milan garden in AD 386, hearing a child chanting "take up and read", he opened Paul at this passage, and later wrote that he needed to read no further: "instantly… all the darkness of doubt vanished away."

Two commands, and they work together. Put on the Lord Jesus Christ: a personal, positive move. Not "put on his virtues" but put on him, which fits everything the letter has said about union: you were baptized into him (6:3), you are in him (8:1), nothing separates you from him (8:39). Sanctification is not primarily acquiring qualities but wearing a person.

Make no provision for the flesh: the negative and tactical half. The word means forethought or planning. Do not lay in supplies for sin. Most defeats are arranged in advance: the route walked, the app kept, the evening left empty, the friendship maintained on false terms. Paul's counsel is not to fight temptation at maximum strength but to stop provisioning it. That is the practical end of a letter that began with God's wrath against suppressed truth: the same body that was once presented to impurity (6:19) is now dressed in Christ and starved of what used to rule it.

Application

  • About God He gives commands that describe love rather than substitute for it, and he supplies by his Spirit what the commands could only require.
  • About you Verse 13 puts quarrelling and jealousy in the same list as drunkenness. Which of the six is currently most true of you, and would you have called it a night-time sin?
  • Do Identify one specific provision you are making for the flesh (an access, a habit, a standing arrangement) and remove it today, not tomorrow. Then read Augustine's verse aloud as a prayer.

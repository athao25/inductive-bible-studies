Lesson 0049 · Unit 5: Chapters 12-16

Romans 15:14-33: A Priest of the Gospel

~25 min · Passage: Romans 15:14-33 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 15:14-33 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: how Paul describes his own ministry in priestly terms, his stated ambition about where he preaches, the reason he has not come to Rome sooner, his three-stage travel plan, why he calls the collection a debt, and the three requests in his closing prayer.

Recall first

Observation check

  • A gracious framing (v. 14). "I myself am satisfied about you… that you yourselves are full of goodness, filled with all knowledge and able to instruct one another." After fifteen chapters of instruction, he affirms them.
  • "By way of reminder" (v. 15). Paul presents the whole letter as a reminder rather than a novelty.
  • Priestly language (v. 16). "To be a minister of Christ Jesus to the Gentiles in the priestly service of the gospel, so that the offering of the Gentiles may be acceptable, sanctified by the Holy Spirit."
  • The Gentiles are the offering. Not Paul's sacrifice for them but them, presented, which links back to 12:1's living sacrifice.
  • Boasting relocated again (v. 17). "In Christ Jesus, then, I have reason to be proud of my work for God", and then verse 18 immediately grounds it in "what Christ has accomplished through me".
  • The scope of the mission (v. 19). "From Jerusalem all the way to Illyricum": the north-western edge of Greece, the frontier of the eastern empire.
  • The stated ambition (v. 20). "To preach the gospel where Christ has not already been named, lest I build on someone else's foundation." A pioneer's calling, not a general rule for everyone.
  • Isaiah 52:15 quoted (v. 21). "Those who have never been told of him will see." The Servant song again, as at 10:15-16.
  • Why the delay (vv. 22-23). Not indifference but a full workload; now "since I no longer have any room for work in these regions", Rome is possible.
  • Three stages (vv. 25-28). Jerusalem with the collection, then Rome, then Spain. Rome is a waypoint, not the destination.
  • The collection as a debt (v. 27). "They owe it to them. For if the Gentiles have come to share in their spiritual blessings, they ought also to be of service to them in material blessings."
  • Three prayer requests (vv. 30-32). Rescue from unbelievers in Judea, that the offering be acceptable to the saints, and that he may come to Rome with joy.
  • How it turned out. Acts 21-28: Paul was arrested in Jerusalem and reached Rome as a prisoner, after a shipwreck. Prayer answered, route unrecognisable.

Interpretation

Mission as priestly service

to be a minister of Christ Jesus to the Gentiles in the priestly service of the gospel, so that the offering of the Gentiles may be acceptable, sanctified by the Holy Spirit. Romans 15:16, ESV

Paul reaches for temple vocabulary to describe evangelism, and every term is chosen. He is a leitourgos (the same word used of the magistrate at 13:6) performing "priestly service". The altar is the gospel. And the offering he presents is not an animal but people: Gentiles, brought and laid before God, "sanctified by the Holy Spirit".

Hold this beside 12:1, where believers present their own bodies as a living sacrifice. The two images fit together: the evangelist brings people to the altar, and they present themselves there. Both are temple service performed away from any temple, which is exactly what the New Testament does with sacrificial language: the categories are kept and relocated.

Then the honesty of verses 17-18. Paul says he has "reason to be proud" of his work, and immediately specifies: "I will not venture to speak of anything except what Christ has accomplished through me." That is the pattern of boasting-relocated that has run through the letter since 3:27: pride is not extinguished, it is redirected to the one who did the work.

Doctrine checkpoint: the collection, and why Paul calls it a debt
Verses 25-28 concern the offering Paul is carrying from Macedonia and Achaia to the poor believers in Jerusalem, and his reasoning about it is worth pausing on.
  • It is voluntary and it is owed. "They were pleased to do it, and indeed they owe it to them" (v. 27). Both at once: gladly given, genuinely due.
  • The logic is spiritual-to-material. Gentiles received spiritual blessings through Jewish believers (the Scriptures, the Messiah, the first apostles) so material service in return is simple equity.
  • It is the pastoral programme of chapters 9-11 in cash. Everything Paul has argued about one olive tree and one people is being enacted by Gentile churches sending money to Jewish believers they had never met. Doctrine issuing in a bank transfer.
  • He anticipated it might be refused. Verse 31 asks the Romans to pray "that my service for Jerusalem may be acceptable to the saints": the Jewish church might not want Gentile money from a controversial apostle. Paul carried it anyway, and Acts 21 shows the tension was real.
Note also the striking phrase at verse 24: Paul hopes "to be helped on my journey" by the Romans. The letter with the deepest theology in Scripture was written partly to raise support for a mission to Spain. Doctrine and logistics were never separate departments for Paul.

Plans, prayers, and how they were answered

I appeal to you, brothers, by our Lord Jesus Christ and by the love of the Spirit, to strive together with me in your prayers to God on my behalf, that I may be delivered from the unbelievers in Judea… Romans 15:30-31, ESV

This is one of the most instructive passages in the New Testament about prayer and providence, because we know the outcome. Paul asked for three things: rescue from hostile unbelievers in Judea, acceptance of the offering by the Jerusalem church, and a joyful arrival in Rome.

What happened, per Acts 21-28: he was seized in the temple by a mob, nearly beaten to death, rescued by Roman soldiers, imprisoned for two years in Caesarea, appealed to Caesar, was shipwrecked off Malta, and finally reached Rome in chains, where he spent two years under house arrest, preaching "with all boldness" (Acts 28:31).

So was the prayer answered? He was delivered from the unbelievers: by Roman custody. He did reach Rome, as a prisoner, and at imperial expense. And the letter he wrote to prepare for a visit outlived the visit, the mission, and the empire. This is 8:28 in biography: all things working together for good, and the good looking nothing like the request.

Two things to take from it. First, Paul made concrete plans and prayed about them specifically: no fatalism, and no vagueness. Second, he held them loosely enough that God's rerouting did not destroy him. Note the closing words of his request: "that by God's will I may come to you with joy" (v. 32). The qualifier was there from the start.

Application

  • About God He answers prayers along routes we would not have chosen, and the detour is often the point (compare 11:11).
  • About you Paul's ambition was to go where Christ was not named. Not everyone is called to that, but everyone can ask: is any part of my life aimed at people who have not heard?
  • Do Write down one specific request you have been praying, and beside it the words "by God's will" (v. 32). Then give something material this month to believers you will never meet: that is verse 27 applied.

Primary source
In your MacArthur Study Bible, read the notes on 15:16 (priestly service), 15:20 (ambition), and 15:25-27 (the collection). Then read Acts 21:17-36 to see how verse 31 was answered.
Church prompt
Ask someone about a prayer that was answered in a way they would never have chosen. Romans 15:30-32 with Acts 21-28 is the apostolic example, and hearing an ordinary one alongside it helps.

Questions welcome, always: "did Paul ever reach Spain?", "why call the collection a debt?", "was his prayer answered?" Ask your teacher; on the first, Scripture does not say, and early tradition is divided.

Lesson 0023 · Unit 3: Chapters 6-8

Romans 7:1-6: Released from the Law

~25 min · Passage: Romans 7:1-6 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 7:1-6 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: who Paul says he is addressing, the principle stated in verse 1, the marriage illustration and precisely who dies in it, how the illustration is applied in verse 4, what the law did according to verse 5, and the two ways of serving contrasted in verse 6.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "I am speaking to those who know the law" (v. 1). Paul addresses the part of his audience for whom this is live. The chapter's whole discussion assumes readers who take the Mosaic law seriously.
  • The principle (v. 1). "The law is binding on a person only as long as he lives." Death dissolves legal obligation: the same logic as 6:7.
  • The illustration (vv. 2-3). A married woman is bound to her husband while he lives; if he dies she is free to marry another without being an adulteress.
  • The illustration does not map neatly, and that is deliberate (v. 4). In the story the husband dies; in the application we die: "you also have died to the law through the body of Christ." Paul is illustrating the principle (death ends obligation), not building an allegory.
  • Purpose stated twice (v. 4). "So that you may belong to another… in order that we may bear fruit for God." Release is for remarriage, not for independence.
  • "Our sinful passions, aroused by the law" (v. 5). An extraordinary claim that will need the rest of the chapter to defend: the law stirred sin up.
  • "Bore fruit for death" (v. 5). Fruit imagery in both directions, matching 6:21-22.
  • "But now we are released from the law, having died to that which held us captive" (v. 6). "But now" again, the letter's signature reversal.
  • Two services contrasted (v. 6). "In the new way of the Spirit and not in the old way of the written code." Not service versus freedom: two kinds of service.
  • The Spirit reappears (v. 6). Absent since 5:5, mentioned here in passing, and about to dominate chapter 8.

Interpretation

Why the illustration limps, and why that is fine

Likewise, my brothers, you also have died to the law through the body of Christ, so that you may belong to another, to him who has been raised from the dead, in order that we may bear fruit for God. Romans 7:4, ESV

Readers often get tangled here. In the illustration, the husband dies and the wife is freed. In the application, we die, and yet we are the ones freed to remarry. If you press the picture as an allegory, you have a woman who dies and then marries someone else.

The confusion dissolves once you see that Paul is illustrating a principle, not encoding a cipher. The principle is stated flatly in verse 1: death ends legal obligation. The marriage case is simply the clearest everyday example of it that his readers know. Then he applies the principle to a different situation, where the death that ends the obligation is ours-in-Christ. The illustration proves the rule; the application uses the rule.

And notice what the release is for. Verse 4 gives two purpose clauses, and neither is autonomy: "so that you may belong to another" and "in order that we may bear fruit for God". This is the same move as chapter 6, where dying to sin led to presenting yourself to God. Paul never releases anyone into ownerlessness. The woman is not freed from marriage; she is freed to marry the risen Christ. Freedom in Romans always means changing masters, never having none.

Doctrine checkpoint: in what sense are Christians "released from the law"?
Verse 6 is easily misused in both directions, so hold four statements together.
  • Released from the law as a way of gaining standing. This is the letter's constant refrain: no one is justified by works of the law (3:20, 28). That door is not merely difficult; it is shut.
  • Released from the law as a condemning power. The law pronounced sentence, and the sentence has been executed in Christ (8:1-3). It has nothing left to say against those in him.
  • Released from the Mosaic covenant as an administration. The believer does not live under Sinai's covenant order: its sacrifices, its food laws, its calendar. Chapter 14 will treat those as matters of conscience precisely because they are no longer binding.
  • Not released from God's moral will. Paul will call the law "holy and righteous and good" (7:12), say the Spirit fulfils "the righteous requirement of the law" in us (8:4), and quote the commandments approvingly at 13:8-10. The imperatives of chapters 12-15 are not optional.
So the contrast in verse 6 is not law versus lawlessness; it is "the old way of the written code" versus "the new way of the Spirit". Same God, same moral character, different mechanism: a code outside you demanding obedience, or the Spirit inside you producing it, which is exactly what Jeremiah 31:33 and Ezekiel 36:27 promised.

"Aroused by the law"

For while we were living in the flesh, our sinful passions, aroused by the law, were at work in our members to bear fruit for death. Romans 7:5, ESV

This is the sentence that makes the rest of chapter 7 necessary. Paul has already said the law increases the trespass (5:20) and brings wrath (4:15). Now he says something more personal: the law arouses sinful passions. Any reader who honours the law of God (and every Jewish reader did) will immediately want to protest, which is exactly why verse 7 opens with "What then shall we say? That the law is sin? By no means!"

The mechanism will be explained tomorrow, but the phenomenon is familiar enough to name now. A prohibition, met by a rebellious heart, functions as an advertisement. Augustine's pears, stolen not because he wanted pears but because the theft was forbidden, is the classic literary case; every parent has watched a smaller version. The problem is not the command, which is good; it is the material it lands on.

Notice the qualifier that opens the verse: "while we were living in the flesh". Paul locates the arousal in a past condition, not the believer's present one. Verse 6 answers it with the new way of the Spirit. The chapter is not saying that God's law is a hostile force; it is saying that a good law over an unchanged heart produces exactly the opposite of what it commands, and that something more than law was always going to be required.

Application

  • About God He did not simply lower the demand. He killed the old relationship and married us to his risen Son, so that obedience could come from union rather than obligation.
  • About you Which mode do you actually live in: the written code or the Spirit? A useful test: when you obey, is it mainly to avoid guilt, or does it flow from belonging to someone?
  • Do Read Jeremiah 31:31-34 and Ezekiel 36:25-27 today, and mark what God promises to do inside people. Then reread 7:6 and note that Paul thinks this has already happened to you.

Church prompt

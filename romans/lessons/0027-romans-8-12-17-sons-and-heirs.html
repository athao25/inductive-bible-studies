Lesson 0027 · Unit 3: Chapters 6-8

Romans 8:12-17: Sons and Heirs

~25 min · Passage: Romans 8:12-17 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 8:12-17 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: what we are said to be debtors to and what we are not, the two outcomes set out in verse 13, what "led by the Spirit" is offered as evidence of, the two spirits contrasted in verse 15, the cry "Abba! Father!" and who prompts it, and the condition attached at the close of verse 17.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "So then, brothers, we are debtors" (v. 12). The sentence is incomplete in a striking way: we are debtors, "not to the flesh". Paul never says what we are debtors to, though the whole chapter implies it.
  • Two outcomes (v. 13). "If you live according to the flesh you will die, but if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live." Life and death, set as alternatives to Christians.
  • "By the Spirit you put to death" (v. 13). Both agents are named. Not the Spirit instead of you, and not you instead of the Spirit.
  • "Led by the Spirit" defines sonship (v. 14). "For all who are led by the Spirit of God are sons of God." Evidence, not entrance requirement.
  • Two spirits (v. 15). "You did not receive the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear but you have received the Spirit of adoption as sons."
  • "Abba! Father!" (v. 15). An Aramaic word retained in Greek: the family word Jesus himself used in Gethsemane (Mark 14:36), and preserved in Galatians 4:6 as well.
  • Double witness (v. 16). "The Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God." Two testimonies, as the law required.
  • Note the shift in vocabulary (vv. 14-17). "Sons" (a legal, inheritance term) and then "children" (a family term). Paul uses both, and both are needed.
  • Heirs, twice over (v. 17). "Heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ." The inheritance is not a possession God gives but God himself.
  • The condition (v. 17). "Provided we suffer with him in order that we may also be glorified with him." Suffering appears exactly where the glory does, and the next paragraph will develop it.

Interpretation

Putting to death, and who does it

For if you live according to the flesh you will die, but if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live. Romans 8:13, ESV

This verse holds together two things that are often separated. The action is genuinely yours: "you put to death", an imperative with real content and real effort. The power is genuinely the Spirit's: "by the Spirit". Remove the first and you get passivity, waiting for holiness to happen. Remove the second and you get chapter 7, where a man tries with everything he has and is defeated in the trying.

The verb is violent on purpose. Mortification is not managing a habit or reducing its frequency; it is killing something. The Puritan John Owen's line is the classic summary: "Be killing sin or it will be killing you." And note the object: "the deeds of the body", specific practices rather than the body itself. Paul is not recommending asceticism; the body is to be presented to God (6:13; 12:1), not punished.

The conditional form of the verse alarms some readers: does salvation depend on my performance? Read it alongside the next verse, and the logic clarifies. Verse 14 says all who are led by the Spirit are sons: the leading is the evidence of a status already held, not a wage paid for it. Paul is describing what a living faith looks like, exactly as he did at 6:16. Where the Spirit indwells, war on sin follows. Where there is no war, the claim to sonship has nothing under it.

Doctrine checkpoint: adoption, and why Paul needed a second word
Justification makes you righteous in God's court. Adoption makes you his child in his family. They are distinct, and Christians who have only the first tend to live like acquitted defendants rather than sons.
  • It is legal. Roman adoption transferred a person permanently into a new family with full inheritance rights, and the old debts were cancelled. Paul's readers in the capital knew exactly how binding it was: an adopted son could not be disinherited on the strength of his birth.
  • It is relational. The proof is a cry, not a certificate: "Abba! Father!" The Spirit produces in the believer an instinctive address that a slave would never dare use.
  • It is future as well as present. Verse 23 will say we are still "waiting eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies". The status is granted now; the full enjoyment of it arrives with the resurrection.
  • It is the ground of assurance. The double witness of verse 16 (the Spirit with our spirit) is why assurance in Romans is not a matter of introspective bookkeeping. The witness is God's before it is ours.
Note the contrast Paul draws in verse 15: not the spirit of slavery "to fall back into fear". Fear of God as a threatening master is precisely what the gospel removes. Reverence remains; dread does not.

Heirs of God

and if children, then heirs: heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ, provided we suffer with him in order that we may also be glorified with him. Romans 8:17, ESV

The phrase "heirs of God" is more extravagant than it first sounds. An heir of God would normally mean an heir of what God possesses: that is, everything. But Paul's usual pattern in Scripture is that the inheritance is God himself: "the LORD is my chosen portion" (Ps 16:5), "the LORD is my portion" (Lam 3:24). The Levites got no land because they got him. The final inheritance of the Christian is not a place but a person.

And "fellow heirs with Christ" sets the scale. The inheritance is not divided into portions that shrink as more heirs arrive; we inherit jointly with the Son, sharing what is his. That is the answer to 5:17's "reign in life" and to the glory hoped for at 5:2 and lost at 3:23.

Then the condition, which the modern ear resists: "provided we suffer with him in order that we may also be glorified with him." This is not a fee charged for the inheritance. It is a description of the road. To be joined to Christ is to be joined to the one the world crucified, and union with him cannot be selective: his death and his life, his suffering and his glory, come as a single package. Paul says as much elsewhere: "all who desire to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted" (2 Tim 3:12). The next paragraph immediately puts the suffering in perspective: "not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed to us" (v. 18).

Application

  • About God He did not merely acquit; he adopted. And he gave his Spirit so that the relationship would be felt as well as documented.
  • About you Do you relate to God as a pardoned defendant or as a son? The tell is what happens after you sin: a defendant expects re-arrest; a child expects to come home.
  • Do Name one "deed of the body" you have been managing rather than killing. Write down what putting it to death this week would concretely involve (access removed, person told, habit replaced) and do the first step today.

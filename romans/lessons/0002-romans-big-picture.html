Lesson 0002 · Orientation

The Big Picture of Romans

~30 min · Passage: Romans 1:1-17 and 15:14-33 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 1:1-17 and 15:14-33 in your ESV, stop and do that now. Those two passages are the letter's frame: the reason Paul writes and the errand he is on. Write down at least five observations before continuing.

Recall first

Retrieval before new input. Without looking back:

Who wrote it, and when

Paul, from Corinth, around AD 57, at the end of his third missionary journey. He dictated it to a scribe named Tertius, who signs his own name at 16:22; the only place in the New Testament where a secretary greets the readers. Paul's host was Gaius (16:23), and Phoebe, a deacon of the church at Cenchreae, almost certainly carried the letter to Rome (16:1-2). These are not decorations; they tell you the letter arrived in the hands of a named woman and was read aloud to a congregation who knew none of the theological vocabulary you are about to learn.

Paul had never been to Rome (1:10-13). That single fact explains the letter's character. Everywhere else he writes into a crisis he already knows; here he writes ahead of himself, to a church he hopes will send him on to Spain (15:24, 28). So he sets out the gospel he preaches, whole, from the ground up. The most systematic document in the New Testament exists because a missionary needed a support base at the far end of the empire.

Who received it

A mixed church under pressure. In AD 49 the emperor Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome: Luke mentions it in passing at Acts 18:2, which is how Paul met Priscilla and Aquila. For five years the Roman congregations were Gentile-only. When Claudius died in 54 and the Jewish believers came back, they returned to churches that had grown used to their absence, with Gentile leaders, Gentile assumptions, and no particular reverence for the calendar and the food laws.

Hold that picture, because it explains what would otherwise look like a detour. Why does Paul spend three chapters (9-11) on Israel's place in God's plan, ending with a stern warning to Gentiles not to be arrogant toward the natural branches (11:18)? Why two chapters (14-15) on food and days and not despising one another? Because the systematic argument was aimed at a real fracture. Romans is not a theology textbook that happens to be a letter. It is a letter whose pastoral problem could only be solved by theology.

Both things are true
Some readers treat Romans as timeless doctrine with a few local greetings attached. Others treat it as pure situational politics about Jew-Gentile relations, with the doctrine as ammunition. The text resists both reductions. Paul argues eternal truths (guilt, wrath, justification, election, glory) precisely because two groups of believers in one city could not eat together. Big doctrine, small tables. That is how this letter works, and it is why chapters 12-16 are not an appendix.

The argument in seven movements

MovementTextThe question answered
Introduction and theme1:1-17Who is Paul, what is the gospel, why is he unashamed?
Condemnation1:18-3:20Why does anyone need it? The whole world is guilty.
Justification3:21-5:21How can God acquit the guilty and stay just?
Sanctification6:1-8:39If grace is free, what becomes of sin, the law, and the future?
Vindication9:1-11:36If Israel is largely outside, has God's word failed?
Transformation12:1-15:13What does mercy look like in a body, a church, a state?
Conclusion15:14-16:27Plans, co-workers, a warning, and a doxology.

Print the book map and keep it beside you. When a paragraph feels stranded, find it on that table first and ask what it is contributing to the movement it sits in.

The theme verse, and the sentence that made it work

For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith, as it is written, "The righteous shall live by faith." Romans 1:16-17, ESV

Everything from 1:18 to 11:36 unpacks these two verses. Salvation (the goal), power of God (the means), everyone who believes (the scope and the condition), Jew first and also Greek (the order of history and the shape of chapters 9-11), righteousness of God revealed (the theme), by faith (the manner), Habakkuk 2:4 (the proof from Scripture). Learn it today; it is memory verse 1, and it activates now.

Doctrine checkpoint: what makes this letter dangerous
Romans has repeatedly broken people open. Augustine, in a Milan garden in 386, opened it at 13:13-14 and stopped drifting. Luther, wrestling with 1:17 in a tower in Wittenberg, found that God's righteousness was a gift and not only a standard, and the Reformation followed. John Wesley felt his heart "strangely warmed" in 1738 while listening to someone read Luther's preface to Romans. That history should raise your expectations for the next few months and lower your confidence that you already know what the letter says.

What to track from day one

  • Righteousness in all its forms: the noun, the verb "to justify", the adjective "righteous". Over sixty times. Watch whose righteousness is in view each time.
  • Faith and believing. Note the bookends: "the obedience of faith" at 1:5 and again at 16:26. Paul frames the whole letter with the phrase, and it should keep you from ever setting faith against obedience.
  • Jew and Greek together. 1:16; 2:9-11; 3:9, 29-30; 9-11; 15:7-13. The pairing never leaves.
  • The law. Paul will say it cannot justify (3:20), that it is holy (7:12), that we are released from it (7:6), and that love fulfils it (13:10). Those are not contradictions; they are different questions, and the discipline is to notice which question is being asked.
  • Boasting. 2:17, 23; 3:27; 4:2; 5:2-3, 11; 11:18; 15:17. Paul demolishes human boasting and then redirects boasting to God. Follow that thread and you have the ethics of the letter in miniature.
  • The Holy Spirit. Almost silent in chapters 1-7, then some twenty appearances in chapter 8. That silence is deliberate; feel it when you get to chapter 7.

Interpretation: why "condemnation" comes before "justification"

Modern readers often want Paul to start with the love of God. He starts with wrath (1:18), and does not mention love until 5:5-8. That order is a decision, not an accident. A gospel is only good news to someone who knows they are in trouble; a pardon means nothing to a person who does not know they are on trial. Paul spends nearly three chapters establishing guilt because the announcement of 3:21 is worthless to anyone who has not felt the force of 3:20.

This is worth pausing on, because it will shape how you read the whole letter and how you speak about it. The order of Romans is a discipleship strategy: know your condition, then know your Saviour, then live from what he has done. Anyone who begins at chapter 8 and stays there ends up with comfort that has nothing under it.

Application

  • About God He answers a fractured church with the truth about himself. The cure for arrogance between believers turns out to be a right view of God's mercy, not a negotiated settlement.
  • About you Which movement of the letter do you already know well, and which do you tend to skip? Most of us live in chapters 5, 8, and 12 and avoid 1-3 and 9-11. Note your instinct now, and watch it as we go.
  • Do Begin memory verse 1 (1:16-17) today: read it aloud ten times, then say it from memory five times. Tomorrow you start at verse 1 of chapter 1.

Check yourself

Before next lesson
Read Romans 1:1-7 in your ESV, twice, and jot five observations. Watch: how many words Paul spends before the sentence's main verb arrives, what three things he calls himself in verse 1, what the gospel was promised through and when, and the two things said about the Son in verses 3-4. It is one enormous sentence in Greek; try to find its skeleton.
Primary source
In your MacArthur Study Bible, reread the Introduction to Romans outline alongside the seven movements above and note where they differ. Then listen to one sermon from the Romans archive at gty.org this week: the introductory messages on 1:1-7 are a good start.
Church prompt
Ask someone at church what they would say Romans is about, in one sentence. Collect two or three answers over the next weeks and compare them at the capstone. Most people name a chapter they love rather than the argument of the book, which is exactly the habit this course is designed to fix.

Questions welcome, always: "why did Paul want to go to Spain?", "did he ever get there?", "how did a church start in Rome without an apostle?" Ask your teacher; the first two are answered from 15:24 and from silence, and the third from Acts 2:10.

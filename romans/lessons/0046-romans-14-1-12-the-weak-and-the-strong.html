Lesson 0046 · Unit 5: Chapters 12-16

Romans 14:1-12: The Weak and the Strong

~30 min · Passage: Romans 14:1-12 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 14:1-12 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: who is called weak and who strong, the two disputes named, what each side is told not to do, the reason given in verse 3, the argument about whose servant a believer is, and the appeal to the judgment seat. Note carefully whether Paul says the weak are mistaken.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "As for the one who is weak in faith, welcome him" (v. 1). The command falls first on the strong, and "welcome" means receive into full fellowship.
  • "Not to quarrel over opinions" (v. 1). The Greek word covers disputes over matters of judgment: questions on which Scripture has not spoken decisively.
  • The first dispute is food (v. 2). "One person believes he may eat anything, while the weak person eats only vegetables." Likely about meat that may have been offered to idols or not slaughtered properly.
  • Two different sins named (v. 3). The strong must not despise; the weak must not pass judgment. Contempt and censoriousness, one for each side.
  • The reason (v. 3). "For God has welcomed him." The decisive fact about the other person is God's acceptance of them.
  • "Who are you to pass judgment on the servant of another?" (v. 4). The image is a household: criticising someone else's servant is presumption toward the master.
  • "The Lord is able to make him stand" (v. 4). Confidence about the other believer's perseverance, grounded in the Lord rather than in their maturity.
  • The second dispute is days (vv. 5-6). "One person esteems one day as better than another, while another esteems all days alike."
  • "Each one should be fully convinced in his own mind" (v. 5). Not indifference: a settled personal conviction is required, and "mind" recalls 12:2.
  • Both sides give thanks (v. 6). The eater gives thanks; the abstainer gives thanks. Both are honouring the Lord in what they do, which is why both are acceptable.
  • "None of us lives to himself" (vv. 7-9). Living, dying, and belonging to the Lord, grounded in Christ's death and resurrection.
  • The judgment seat (vv. 10-12). Isaiah 45:23 quoted: every knee will bow. "So then each of us will give an account of himself to God."

Interpretation

What counts as an "opinion"

As for the one who is weak in faith, welcome him, but not to quarrel over opinions. Romans 14:1, ESV

Everything in this chapter depends on identifying the category correctly. Paul is not saying doctrine does not matter: this is the same letter that spent eleven chapters on doctrine and will warn against divisive teachers at 16:17. Nor is he flattening every disagreement into preference. He is dealing with a specific class of question: matters on which believers with equal loyalty to Christ can reach different conclusions, where Scripture does not settle the case, and where each can give thanks to God in what they do (v. 6).

The two examples are food and days, and both were live for the Roman church. Jewish believers returning after the expulsion under Claudius had kept the food laws and the Sabbath all their lives; the mostly Gentile congregations had not. Neither practice was sin, and each group found the other's freedom or scruple offensive. That is the situation Paul addresses, and it is why chapter 14 follows chapters 9-11 rather than standing on its own.

Note the labels, and how carefully Paul handles them. He calls one group "weak in faith", so he does think their scruple reflects less freedom than the gospel provides, and 14:14 will say plainly that "nothing is unclean in itself". Yet the first command in the chapter is aimed at the strong, and it is not "correct them" but "welcome him". Being right about a disputed matter confers no authority over the person who differs.

Doctrine checkpoint: which matters are disputable?
A working test, drawn from the chapter itself, plus a caution.
  • Is it something Scripture settles? If it does (the deity of Christ, justification by faith, the sinfulness of adultery) it is not in this category. Paul does not tell the Galatians to welcome each other's views on circumcision-as-necessary-for-salvation; he calls it another gospel, because standing before God was at stake.
  • Can both parties genuinely give thanks to God in what they do (v. 6)? That is Paul's own test. Nobody can give thanks over adultery.
  • Is the conviction held before the Lord (vv. 5-8)? The abstainer is not fussing about a preference; he believes he is honouring God. That is why he must be respected rather than mocked.
  • Caution both ways. The perennial temptation is to treat our convictions as gospel matters and other people's as mere opinions. The chapter cuts against both errors: the strong must not despise, the weak must not condemn.
Modern equivalents exist but must be chosen carefully, and Christians differ on which qualify: alcohol, schooling choices, entertainment, days and calendars, dietary practice, forms of worship. What matters more than the list is the posture. Paul's aim is not to produce a tidy taxonomy but to stop two groups of believers from destroying each other over matters God has not made tests of fellowship.

Whose servant is he?

Who are you to pass judgment on the servant of another? It is before his own master that he stands or falls. And he will be upheld, for the Lord is able to make him stand. Romans 14:4, ESV

The argument is about jurisdiction, and it is the same move as 12:19. There, vengeance belonged to God, so private retaliation was out of bounds. Here, judgment belongs to the Lord, so criticising another believer's conscience is trespass. In a Roman household, correcting another man's slave was an insult to his owner; you do not have standing.

And then the assurance: "he will be upheld, for the Lord is able to make him stand." Read that as a rebuke to a particular anxiety: the fear that if I do not correct this person, their error will ruin them. Paul says the Lord is competent to keep his own servants. It is the pastoral application of the golden chain: the same God who justified them will glorify them, whatever they think about vegetables.

Verses 7-9 push deeper. "None of us lives to himself, and none of us dies to himself… so then, whether we live or whether we die, we are the Lord's." The reason we may not judge each other is that we all belong to the same owner, and the ground of that ownership is Christ's death and resurrection. Christian liberty is not autonomy: nobody in this chapter is free to do as they please. Both parties are servants; they simply have instructions on different matters.

One judgment seat, and your own account

for it is written, "As I live, says the Lord, every knee shall bow to me, and every tongue shall confess to God." So then each of us will give an account of himself to God. Romans 14:11-12, ESV

Paul closes the first half of the chapter by pointing to the judgment seat, and note how he applies it. The natural use of a coming judgment would be to warn your opponent. Paul uses it to sober you: "each of us will give an account of himself". The prospect of standing before God is presented not as leverage against the person across the aisle but as a reason to attend to your own conduct, including your contempt and your censoriousness.

The quotation is from Isaiah 45:23, where every knee bows to Yahweh; Paul applies it in Philippians 2:10-11 to Christ. Here the point is simpler: there is one throne, it is occupied, and you are not on it. Which makes the seating arrangement in a disputed matter quite clear.

Application

  • About God He has welcomed people whose convictions differ from yours, on matters he did not make tests. That is his prerogative and a rebuke to your gatekeeping.
  • About you Which sin is yours: contempt or condemnation? Verse 3 assigns one to each side, and most of us know instinctively which we are prone to.
  • Do Name one believer whose practice on a disputable matter irritates you. Then do what verse 1 says: welcome them, concretely: a meal, an invitation, a conversation that is not about the disputed thing.

Primary source
In your MacArthur Study Bible, read the notes on 14:1 (weak in faith), 14:4, and 14:5-6 (days). Then compare 1 Corinthians 8 and 10:23-33, where Paul handles the same problem for a different church.
Church prompt
Ask someone what they think counts as a "disputable matter" in your congregation, and where the line falls. Comparing your list with theirs is instructive, and disagreeing about the list is itself an exercise in chapter 14.

Questions welcome, always: "does this make all doctrine negotiable?", "who decides what is disputable?", "was Paul saying the Sabbath is optional?" Ask your teacher; on the last, note that verse 5 puts days squarely in the disputable category for this congregation.

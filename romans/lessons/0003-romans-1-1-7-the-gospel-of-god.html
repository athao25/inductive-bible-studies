Romans 1:1-7: The Gospel of God

~25 min · Passage: Romans 1:1-7 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 1:1-7 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write down at least five observations: the three titles Paul gives himself, where the gospel came from and when it was promised, the two statements about the Son, what Paul says he received and for what purpose, and what he calls his readers.

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • It is one sentence (vv. 1-7). In Greek this is a single, enormous period, the longest opening of any Paul letter. The skeleton is simple ("Paul… to all those in Rome… grace to you and peace") and everything between is a description of the gospel. Paul cannot say his own name without stopping to explain the message.
  • Three self-descriptions (v. 1). Servant (literally slave) of Christ Jesus, called as an apostle, set apart for the gospel. Status, commission, purpose. Note the order: slave first.
  • "The gospel of God" (v. 1). Not Paul's gospel, not the church's. God is its author and its owner. He will say "my gospel" later (2:16; 16:25) meaning the message entrusted to him, never the message invented by him.
  • Promised beforehand (v. 2). "Through his prophets in the holy Scriptures." The gospel is not new, and it is not a change of plan. Anyone expecting Paul to pit Christianity against the Old Testament has been contradicted before the greeting ends.
  • Two things about the Son (vv. 3-4). "Descended from David according to the flesh" and "declared to be the Son of God in power according to the Spirit of holiness by his resurrection from the dead." Human lineage and divine declaration, held together in a single clause.
  • "Grace and apostleship" (v. 5). One gift, not two. The commission came as mercy, which is how a man who had hunted the church could receive it.
  • "To bring about the obedience of faith… among all the nations" (v. 5). The purpose clause. Watch that phrase; it comes back as the letter's final word at 16:26.
  • Four things said of the readers (vv. 6-7). Called to belong to Jesus Christ, loved by God, called to be saints, in Rome. Their identity is settled by God before their address is mentioned.
  • "Grace to you and peace" (v. 7). Paul's standard greeting fuses the Greek charis and the Hebrew shalom. The order is never reversed: grace produces peace, not the other way round.

Interpretation

What is a gospel, and whose is it?

Paul, a servant of Christ Jesus, called to be an apostle, set apart for the gospel of God, which he promised beforehand through his prophets in the holy Scriptures Romans 1:1-2, ESV

The word euangelion meant an announcement of good news, and in the Roman world it was imperial vocabulary: the birth of an emperor, a victory at the frontier. A calendar inscription from Asia Minor calls the birthday of Augustus "the beginning of the good news for the world". Paul takes the empire's word and attaches it to a Galilean who was executed by the empire. To a congregation living in the capital, that was audacious in a way we have to work to feel.

But the more important observation is the genitive: it is God's gospel. News is not advice. A gospel is not a set of instructions for improving yourself; it is a report of something that has happened, which you did not do and cannot undo. That is why it can be believed, and why it must be. And because it is God's, its content is not adjustable. Paul is a herald, not an author, which is exactly why he calls himself a slave before he calls himself an apostle.

Doctrine checkpoint: does 1:4 mean Jesus became the Son at the resurrection?
"Declared to be the Son of God in power" (ESV) translates a verb, horizo, that can mean appointed or designated, and some translations render it "appointed the Son of God in power". Does that make Jesus' sonship begin at Easter?
  • No, and the sentence itself shows why. Verse 3 says the gospel is "concerning his Son, who was descended from David": he is already the Son before the descent from David is mentioned. Sonship precedes the incarnation, let alone the resurrection.
  • The phrase "in power" is doing the work. The contrast is not son / not-son but the Son in weakness and the Son in power. Eternal sonship, publicly enthroned. Compare Acts 13:33 and Psalm 2:7, where the resurrection is the day the King is declared.
  • "According to the flesh" / "according to the Spirit of holiness" most likely contrasts two stages or spheres of the one Son's existence: the sphere of his human descent, and the sphere of the Spirit in which he was vindicated. Some read "Spirit of holiness" as his divine nature; either way, the resurrection reveals rather than creates.
All three of this course's teachers hold eternal sonship, and Romans 8:3 ("God has done… by sending his own Son") settles it: the one sent was already the Son when he was sent.

What is "the obedience of faith"?

through whom we have received grace and apostleship to bring about the obedience of faith for the sake of his name among all the nations Romans 1:5, ESV

The phrase can be read three ways: the obedience that consists in faith, the obedience that flows from faith, or the obedience that faith is owed. Paul's usage in the letter suggests he would not choose between them. Believing the gospel is itself an act of submission to God's verdict about you (10:3 describes unbelief as a refusal to submit), and faith that leaves the life untouched is not the article Paul is describing (compare 6:17, "obedient from the heart").

Notice what this rules out at both ends. It rules out a faith that is merely intellectual assent with no bending of the will. And it rules out the idea that obedience is the price of standing with God, because the whole letter will insist that justification is by faith apart from works (3:28). The phrase brackets the letter (1:5 and 16:26) like two nails holding a picture straight. Whatever Paul says in between about faith alone, he never means faith by itself, unaccompanied and inert.

And note the scope: "among all the nations". Written to a church in the capital of an empire, by a man planning to reach Spain. The gospel is God's, and its intended audience is everyone.

Application

  • About God He kept a promise made through prophets across centuries. The gospel's antiquity is part of its credibility: God does not improvise, and he does not abandon what he begins. That is the same character chapters 9-11 will lean on.
  • About you Paul's first word about himself is slave. Before gifting, calling, or ministry, ownership. Ask which of your self-descriptions comes first in your own mind, and what that reveals.
  • Do Write out verses 1-7 by hand, marking the main clause ("Paul… to all those in Rome… grace to you and peace") in one colour and everything said about the gospel in another. Five minutes, and the shape of the sentence will stay with you.

Lesson 0048 · Unit 5: Chapters 12-16

Romans 15:1-13: Christ Did Not Please Himself

~30 min · Passage: Romans 15:1-13 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 15:1-13 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the obligation laid on the strong, the example in verse 3 and the psalm behind it, what Scripture is said to be for, the prayer of verses 5-6, the two purposes of Christ's servanthood in verses 8-9, and the four quotations that close the section.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "We who are strong have an obligation" (v. 1). Not an option or a kindness. The word implies a debt: the same category as 13:8 and 1:14.
  • "To bear with the failings of the weak, and not to please ourselves" (v. 1). "Bear" means to carry, not merely to tolerate.
  • The aim of pleasing your neighbour (v. 2). "For his good, to build him up." Not flattery: the goal is construction, as in 14:19.
  • The model (v. 3). "For Christ did not please himself." One sentence, and it is the whole argument of chapters 14-15.
  • The quotation (v. 3). Psalm 69:9: "The reproaches of those who reproached you fell on me." Christ absorbed insults aimed at God.
  • Why the Scriptures were written (v. 4). "For our instruction, that through endurance and through the encouragement of the Scriptures we might have hope." Compare 4:23-24.
  • Two titles for God in two verses (vv. 5, 13). "The God of endurance and encouragement" and "the God of hope". Paul names God by what he supplies.
  • The prayer's goal (vv. 5-6). Harmony "that together you may with one voice glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ." Unity is aimed at corporate worship.
  • The command that summarises the section (v. 7). "Welcome one another as Christ has welcomed you." The same verb as 14:1 and 14:3, now with Christ as the pattern.
  • Two purposes of the incarnation (vv. 8-9). "To show God's truthfulness, in order to confirm the promises given to the patriarchs, and in order that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy." Truth to the Jews, mercy to the Gentiles.
  • Four quotations, one theme (vv. 9-12). From the Law (Deut 32:43), the Prophets (Isa 11:10), and the Writings (Ps 18:49; 117:1), every section of the Hebrew canon, all about Gentiles praising God with his people.
  • The closing benediction (v. 13). Hope, joy, peace, believing, and the power of the Holy Spirit: the letter's vocabulary gathered into one sentence.

Interpretation

The argument's final ground: Christ

For Christ did not please himself, but as it is written, "The reproaches of those who reproached you fell on me." Romans 15:3, ESV

Two chapters of practical instruction about food and days come to rest on a single sentence about Jesus. Everything the strong are asked to do (bear with the weak, forgo liberties, welcome those who differ) is grounded not in a principle of tolerance but in the conduct of Christ, who "did not please himself".

The quotation deepens it. Psalm 69:9 describes the sufferer bearing insults that were actually directed at God. Christ did not merely decline his own preferences; he absorbed hostility that belonged to someone else. Which puts the inconvenience of forgoing meat in a very particular light. If he took reproaches that were not his, you can manage dinner.

Then verse 7 states the command in its final form: "welcome one another as Christ has welcomed you." Chapter 14 began by telling the strong to welcome the weak; here the command becomes mutual, and the standard becomes the welcome each of them has already received. That is the pattern of the whole letter's ethics: you extend what you were given, at the cost at which it was given.

Doctrine checkpoint: why the Old Testament was written down
Verse 4 is one of Scripture's clearest statements about its own purpose, and it repays attention.
  • "Whatever was written in former days was written for our instruction." The Old Testament was recorded with later readers in view: the same claim as 4:23-24 about Genesis 15:6. It is not a Jewish archive that Christians may consult; it was written for us.
  • Its stated products are endurance, encouragement, and hope. Not chiefly information. Paul has just quoted a psalm to settle a dispute about food, and he expects the Scriptures to produce staying power in ordinary Christians.
  • Hence the way this passage argues. Four Old Testament quotations in verses 9-12 to prove that Gentiles worship alongside Israel. Paul does not settle the Roman fracture by apostolic decree; he settles it from the Scriptures both parties already accepted.
  • And hence your own daily habit. This is why the course asks you to read the passage before the lesson. Endurance and hope come through the text itself, not through commentary about it.

Truth and mercy, Jew and Gentile

For I tell you that Christ became a servant to the circumcised to show God's truthfulness, in order to confirm the promises given to the patriarchs, and in order that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy. Romans 15:8-9, ESV

This is the letter's argument in one sentence, and the two halves are chosen with precision. Christ came to the circumcised to demonstrate God's truthfulness, because to Israel promises had been made, and keeping them is a matter of veracity. That is chapters 9-11 in a phrase. And he came so that Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy, because to the Gentiles no promises had been made, so their inclusion is sheer kindness.

Notice how neatly that settles the Roman quarrel. Neither group has grounds for superiority. The Jewish believer receives what was promised, which was itself a gift; the Gentile believer receives what was never promised, which is pure mercy. Truthfulness and mercy, one Saviour, one congregation.

Then the four quotations, and their shape is the point. Paul draws from the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings (the whole Hebrew canon) and every text has Gentiles praising God together with his people: "Rejoice, O Gentiles, with his people"; "let all the peoples extol him"; "in him will the Gentiles hope". The Gentile mission is not an innovation forced on a reluctant Israel; it was written into Israel's own songbook. That is the conclusion of an argument that began at 1:16 with "to the Jew first and also to the Greek", and it ends in a chorus.

The benediction

May the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing, so that by the power of the Holy Spirit you may abound in hope. Romans 15:13, ESV

The doctrinal body of the letter ends here, and it ends in prayer. Gather the vocabulary: hope (three appearances in one verse), joy, peace, believing, the power of the Holy Spirit. Every one of those has a history in this letter: peace at 5:1, joy and hope at 5:2-5, the Spirit throughout chapter 8, believing from 1:16 onward.

And notice the mechanism: joy and peace come "in believing", and hope abounds "by the power of the Holy Spirit". Not by effort, and not by favourable circumstances. Paul is writing to a fractured congregation in a hostile capital, on his way to a city where he expects danger (15:31), and his closing wish for them is overflow. If your reading of Romans has produced knowledge without hope, verse 13 names what is missing and where it comes from.

Application

  • About God He is the God of endurance, encouragement, and hope: named by what he gives. And he keeps promises to those who had them and shows mercy to those who did not.
  • About you Verse 1 calls it an obligation. Where have you treated bearing with a weaker believer as optional generosity rather than a debt?
  • Do Pray verse 13 for your own congregation by name today. Then read Psalm 69:1-12 and note what the sufferer of verse 9 was actually enduring.

Check yourself

Before next lesson
Read Romans 15:14-33 in your ESV, twice, and jot five observations. Watch: how Paul describes his own ministry in priestly terms, his ambition about where he preaches, the reason he has not yet come to Rome, his three-part travel plan, the collection and why he calls it a debt, and the three requests in his closing prayer.
Primary source
In your MacArthur Study Bible, read the notes on 15:1-3, 15:4 (the purpose of Scripture), and 15:8-9 (truthfulness and mercy).
Church prompt
Ask someone what they think the goal of church unity is. Verses 5-6 give an answer many people have not considered: that together you may with one voice glorify God. Unity is for worship, not for comfort.

Questions welcome, always: "how is bearing with the weak an obligation?", "why four quotations rather than one?", "what does 'in believing' add to joy and peace?" Ask your teacher.

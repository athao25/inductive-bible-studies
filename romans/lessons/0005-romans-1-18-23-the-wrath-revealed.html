Lesson 0005 · Unit 1: Chapters 1:1-3:20

Romans 1:18-23: The Wrath Revealed

~25 min · Passage: Romans 1:18-23 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 1:18-23 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: what is revealed and from where, what people do to the truth, what God has shown them and by what means, the two attributes named in verse 20, the verdict of "without excuse", and the two verbs of verses 21 and 23.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "For" (v. 18). The paragraph is grammatically attached to verse 17. The righteousness of God is revealed because the wrath of God is revealed. Good news follows bad news as its answer, not as its contradiction.
  • Two revelations, same verb. Righteousness "is revealed" (v. 17) and wrath "is revealed" (v. 18), both present tense. Not two eras; two disclosures happening now.
  • "From heaven" (v. 18). The source is God himself, not an impersonal moral order and not human consequences alone.
  • Two targets, one root (v. 18). Ungodliness and unrighteousness (vertical and horizontal) and both are held together by the participle that follows: those "who by their unrighteousness suppress the truth".
  • "Suppress" (v. 18). The verb means to hold down or restrain by force. This is not ignorance; it is effort. Truth is pushed under the surface and held there.
  • "What can be known about God is plain to them, because God has shown it" (v. 19). The knowledge is God's own doing. He is the active party even in the knowledge people are suppressing.
  • A deliberate paradox (v. 20). "His invisible attributes… have been clearly perceived… in the things that have been made." What cannot be seen is seen, in what can.
  • Two attributes named (v. 20). "Eternal power and divine nature." Creation testifies that God is, and that he is powerful and divine, not who Christ is or how to be saved. Note the limit.
  • "So they are without excuse" (v. 20). The verdict, stated as a purpose or result clause. This is the point of the whole paragraph.
  • Two failures (v. 21). "They did not honour him as God or give thanks." Idolatry begins as ingratitude before it becomes a statue.
  • A chain of darkening (vv. 21-22). Futile in thinking, darkened in heart, claiming to be wise, became fools. The intellect follows the will downhill.
  • "Exchanged" (v. 23). The first of three exchanges in this chapter (vv. 23, 25, 26). Glory for images, and the images descend in order: man, birds, animals, creeping things.

Interpretation

What kind of wrath is this?

For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who by their unrighteousness suppress the truth. Romans 1:18, ESV

Modern readers stumble here, usually because they imagine wrath as a loss of temper. Scripture's wrath is nothing of the kind: it is God's settled, holy, personal opposition to sin, as steady as his love and no more arbitrary. A judge who felt nothing about atrocity would not be gracious; he would be corrupt. Love that never burns at what destroys the beloved is not love.

Note the present tense. Paul is not speaking primarily of the coming day of judgment (that appears at 2:5, "storing up wrath for yourself on the day of wrath") but of something being revealed now. The next paragraph will tell us exactly what the present revelation looks like: three times, "God gave them up". Present wrath is God handing people over to what they insisted on having. It is a terrible mercy in reverse: not a lightning bolt but a released grip.

Doctrine checkpoint: how much can creation teach?
Verses 19-20 are the classic text for general revelation. Note precisely what Paul claims and does not claim.
  • It is real knowledge. "Clearly perceived", "plain to them", and God himself is the one who "has shown it". Not a hint or an inference available only to philosophers.
  • It has content: "eternal power and divine nature". That God exists, is powerful, is divine, and is therefore owed honour and thanks. Enough to make idolatry inexcusable.
  • It cannot save. There is no gospel in a sunset. Nothing here about the cross, the resurrection, or forgiveness. That is why 10:14 asks how they will believe in him of whom they have never heard, and why Paul is in debt to Greeks and barbarians.
  • Its function here is judicial. The purpose of the paragraph is the phrase "so they are without excuse". General revelation closes the mouth; it does not open the door.
So when someone asks, "what about the person who has never heard?", Romans 1 answers the question underneath it: nobody starts from neutral ignorance. Everyone has received light and everyone has suppressed it. That is why the answer to their situation is not information but mercy, and why missions is urgent rather than optional.

The anatomy of idolatry

For although they knew God, they did not honor him as God or give thanks to him, but they became futile in their thinking, and their foolish hearts were darkened. Romans 1:21, ESV

Watch the sequence, because Paul is describing a mechanism, not merely listing crimes. It begins with knowledge, "although they knew God". It proceeds by a refusal of two things owed: honour and thanks. Ingratitude is not a minor vice in this account; it is the hinge. To receive constantly from a giver you refuse to acknowledge requires a story in which the gifts came from somewhere else, and manufacturing that story is what verse 21 calls futile thinking.

Then the darkening. Note the order: the heart's rebellion darkens the mind, and the darkened mind congratulates itself, "claiming to be wise, they became fools". This is the biblical account of unbelief, and it is why argument alone rarely converts anyone. The problem is not primarily a shortage of evidence; verse 20 says the evidence is clearly perceived. The problem is a will that will not have this God, and an intellect obligingly supplying reasons.

Finally the exchange (v. 23): "they exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images resembling mortal man and birds and animals and creeping things." The words echo Psalm 106:20, where Israel "exchanged the glory of God for the image of an ox", and behind that, the golden calf. Paul is describing the Gentile world in language taken from Israel's worst day: a hint of what is coming in chapter 2. And notice the descent in the list: from man down through birds and beasts to things that crawl. Worship always shapes the worshipper, so an idol drags its maker downward. You will not rise above what you adore.

Application

  • About God He is not indifferent. The wrath of 1:18 and the righteousness of 1:17 come from the same character, and the cross (3:25-26) is where they meet. A God who could shrug at evil would have nothing to offer the wronged.
  • About you The mechanism in verse 21 is not foreign. Where are you receiving without thanking? Ingratitude is where idolatry starts, and it usually looks like ordinary distraction rather than dramatic rebellion.
  • Do Write down ten things you received this week that you did not earn. Thank God for each by name, out loud. Then read Psalm 106:19-23 and note the phrase Paul borrowed.

Before next lesson
Read Romans 1:24-32 in your ESV, twice, and jot five observations. Watch for the phrase that repeats three times, what follows each occurrence, the two further "exchanges", the shape of the vice list at the end, and the sting in the final verse. It is a hard passage; read it as a diagnosis rather than an insult.
Primary source
In your MacArthur Study Bible, read the notes on 1:18 (wrath), 1:19-20 (general revelation), and 1:21 (the darkening). Five minutes, after your own observations.
Church prompt
Ask someone how they would answer a friend who says, "a loving God would not be angry." Compare the answers against 1:18 and note whether anyone reaches for the cross. That is where the two revelations meet.

Questions welcome, always: "does this mean everyone secretly believes in God?", "how does 'without excuse' relate to those who die in infancy?", "is nature enough for anyone?" Ask your teacher; the third is answered plainly at 10:14-17.

Lesson 0017 · Unit 2: Chapters 3:21-5:21

Romans 5:1-5: Peace with God

~25 min · Passage: Romans 5:1-5 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 5:1-5 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the first word and what it reaches back to, the tense of "have been justified", the three things "we have" in verses 1-2, the two things we rejoice in, the four-link chain of verses 3-4, and the reason given in verse 5 why hope does not disappoint.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "Therefore" (v. 1). The first great hinge after the doctrine. Everything from 3:21 to 4:25 stands behind this word.
  • "Since we have been justified by faith" (v. 1). A completed action in the past. The verdict is not being decided; it has been given.
  • "We have peace with God" (v. 1). Not peace of mind but peace with God: the end of hostility, a change of relationship. The war is over because the enemy has been reconciled by another.
  • "Through our Lord Jesus Christ" (v. 1). The phrase recurs at verses 11 and 21, and at 6:23, 7:25, 8:39. Nothing in this section is had directly; everything is had through him.
  • "We have also obtained access… into this grace in which we stand" (v. 2). Access is a court word: being brought into a king's presence. And grace is not only a transaction but a place you stand.
  • "We rejoice in hope of the glory of God" (v. 2). The verb is kauchaomai, the same word translated "boast" in 3:27 and 4:2. Boasting excluded, then restored with a new object.
  • The glory we fell short of (3:23) is now our hope (5:2). The exact deficit named in the indictment becomes the promised inheritance.
  • "Not only that, but we rejoice in our sufferings" (v. 3). The same verb again, aimed at the least likely object.
  • A four-link chain (vv. 3-4). Suffering → endurance → character → hope. Each link "produces" the next; the Greek word for character means tested and approved, like metal assayed.
  • "Hope does not put us to shame" (v. 5). The reason is not the strength of our hoping but "God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit."
  • First mention of the Spirit in the letter's argument (v. 5). And first mention of God's love. Both arrive together, and both wait until after justification has been settled.

Interpretation

Peace with God is a status before it is a feeling

Therefore, since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ. Romans 5:1, ESV

The whole letter turns here from what God has done to what it means for those it was done for. And the first consequence Paul names is not a change in feeling but a change in relations between two parties. Chapter 1 established that we were under wrath; 5:10 will say plainly that we were "enemies". Peace, in this sentence, is the cessation of that hostility: a treaty, not a mood.

That order matters enormously for the Christian life. If peace with God were a feeling, it would fluctuate with sleep, health, and circumstance, and its absence on a bad morning would mean the relationship had lapsed. Because it is a status secured by a verdict already given, the bad morning is a bad morning and nothing more. Feelings follow facts here, and often at a distance.

Note the three things Paul says we have: peace (v. 1), access into grace (v. 2), and a place to stand. The picture behind "access" is being introduced into a throne room: a person of no standing brought in by someone who has it. And the standing is permanent: "this grace in which we stand", not into which we occasionally step.

Doctrine checkpoint: boasting, restored
Watch the vocabulary carefully across three chapters and you will see one of Paul's deliberate patterns.
  • 2:17, 23: boasting in God and in the law, condemned because it was self-referential.
  • 3:27; 4:2: boasting excluded entirely by the mechanism of justification.
  • 5:2, 3, 11: the same word, now translated "rejoice", aimed at the hope of glory, at sufferings, and finally "in God through our Lord Jesus Christ".
Paul does not treat the human impulse to exult in something as a defect to be extinguished. He treats it as a faculty pointed at the wrong object. Justification demolishes the false version and then hands the faculty back with an object that can carry it. That is why the letter's ethics later can be so demanding without being joyless: a Christian is not a person who has stopped boasting but a person whose boast has been relocated (compare Jer 9:23-24, which stands behind Paul's usage).

The chain that runs through suffering

Not only that, but we rejoice in our sufferings, knowing that suffering produces endurance, and endurance produces character, and character produces hope, and hope does not put us to shame. Romans 5:3-5, ESV

Three cautions will keep this from becoming a cruelty. First, Paul says we rejoice in our sufferings, not for them. Suffering is not called good; it is called productive under God's hand. Second, the rejoicing rests on knowledge ("knowing that") not on feeling. It is a conclusion drawn about a process, available to someone who is not enjoying any part of it. Third, this comes after 5:1-2, not before: only a person at peace with God can read their suffering this way, because for anyone else the suffering might be judgment.

The chain itself repays attention. Endurance is literally remaining-under: not gritted teeth but staying put rather than bolting. Character renders dokime, a word used of metal tested and found genuine: the quality of proven-ness that only shows up in someone who has been under load. And that proven-ness produces hope, which is the part most of us find surprising. We assume hope precedes endurance and gets used up by it. Paul says the opposite: having been carried through something you could not have carried yourself, you have evidence, and hope grows.

Then verse 5 anchors it: "hope does not put us to shame, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us." Hope in the Bible is not wishing; it is confident expectation, and here it is guaranteed by an experience: the love of God poured out, lavishly, by the Spirit. This is the first mention of the Spirit in the letter's argument, and it is not accidental that he appears at the point where doctrine has to be lived. The verb "poured" suggests flooding rather than dripping, and the tense marks a settled state. Whatever else the Christian life involves, Paul assumes that God's love is not merely believed but known inwardly, by the Spirit's work.

Application

  • About God He ended the hostility himself and then gave the Spirit so that his love would be felt as well as credited. He is not a reluctant reconciler.
  • About you Peace with God does not fluctuate with your performance today. Test the difference by asking whether your sense of standing before God this morning tracked your obedience yesterday. If so, you have been reading a feeling as a verdict.
  • Do Begin memory verse 4 today (5:1), keeping the "Therefore". Then take one current hardship and write out the four links of verses 3-4 against it, naming what endurance would actually look like this week.

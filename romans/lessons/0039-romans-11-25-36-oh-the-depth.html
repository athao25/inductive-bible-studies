Lesson 0039 · Unit 4: Chapters 9-11

Romans 11:25-36: Oh, the Depth

~30 min · Passage: Romans 11:25-36 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 11:25-36 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: what Paul calls a mystery and why he discloses it, the three qualifiers attached to the hardening, the disputed sentence in verse 26, the two quotations that follow, the striking claim of verse 29, the symmetry of verses 30-32, and the shape of the closing doxology.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "I do not want you to be unaware of this mystery" (v. 25). In Paul, a mystery is not a puzzle but something previously hidden and now revealed.
  • The stated purpose (v. 25). "Lest you be wise in your own sight": the same anti-arrogance aim as the olive tree.
  • Three qualifiers on the hardening (v. 25). It is partial ("a partial hardening"), it is on Israel, and it is temporary ("until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in").
  • The disputed sentence (v. 26). "And in this way all Israel will be saved." Note "in this way" (houtos), a word about manner, not simply "and then".
  • Two quotations (vv. 26-27). Isaiah 59:20-21 with Isaiah 27:9: "The Deliverer will come from Zion, he will banish ungodliness from Jacob… when I take away their sins."
  • Two statuses at once (v. 28). "As regards the gospel, they are enemies for your sake. But as regards election, they are beloved for the sake of their forefathers."
  • The anchor (v. 29). "For the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable." Literally, not to be repented of: God does not change his mind about them.
  • A symmetry in three steps (vv. 30-31). You were disobedient and received mercy through their disobedience; they are now disobedient so that they too may receive mercy.
  • The summary (v. 32). "For God has consigned all to disobedience, that he may have mercy on all."
  • The doxology (vv. 33-36). Three exclamations, two rhetorical questions from Isaiah 40:13 and Job 41:11, and a closing formula: "from him and through him and to him are all things."
  • Note where the argument ends. Not with a conclusion but with worship, and the last word of the section is "Amen".

Interpretation

Doctrine checkpoint: "all Israel will be saved" (11:26)
This is the live checkpoint of the unit, and serious believers differ. Three main readings, with their strongest evidence.
  • A future large-scale salvation of ethnic Israel. The hardening lasts "until" the full number of Gentiles comes in, implying a change afterwards; "all Israel" in verse 26 most naturally means the same Israel as verses 25 and 28; and verses 12 and 15 speak of their "full inclusion" and "acceptance". This is the majority Reformed reading and MacArthur's, and his dispensational framework leads him to tie it closely to a future national restoration.
  • The total number of elect Jews across history. "In this way" (not "and then") describes the manner just outlined: remnant by remnant, provoked to jealousy, saved through the gospel, so "all Israel" is the sum of the saved remnant through the age.
  • The whole people of God, Jew and Gentile together. Since 9:6 redefined Israel and 2:28-29 relocated Jewishness to the heart, "all Israel" could mean the complete olive tree. This reading is the least likely, because "Israel" in verses 25 and 28 clearly means ethnic Israel, and changing the referent mid-sentence needs stronger warrant than the context gives.
Where this course lands: some form of the first reading, held without dogmatism. The "until" of verse 25, the "how much more" of verses 12 and 15, and the "beloved for the sake of their forefathers" of verse 28 together point to a future in which many Jewish people believe. What the passage does not teach on any reading: that Israel is saved apart from Christ. The Deliverer banishes ungodliness and takes away sins (vv. 26-27), which is the gospel Paul has preached since 3:21. There is one way into the tree, and it is faith.

Irrevocable

For the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable. Romans 11:29, ESV

Six words that carry the weight of three chapters, and beyond them the weight of chapter 8. The Greek is ametameleta: not subject to a change of mind. God does not give and then reconsider.

In context this is about Israel: they are "beloved for the sake of their forefathers", and God's covenant commitment is not cancelled by their present unbelief. But notice how the principle reaches further. If God's calling could be revoked, then the calling of 8:30 could be revoked too, and the whole assurance of chapter 8 would rest on sand. The security of the individual believer and the faithfulness of God to Israel stand or fall together, which is precisely why Paul could not leave chapters 9-11 unwritten.

Verse 28 is worth holding beside it: "as regards the gospel, they are enemies for your sake. But as regards election, they are beloved." Two true descriptions of the same people at the same time, from two angles. That is a useful discipline for anyone tempted to a single flat verdict about a person or a group.

Mercy on all

For God has consigned all to disobedience, that he may have mercy on all. Romans 11:32, ESV

Paul's summary of everything since 1:18. He has argued that Gentiles are guilty (1:18-32), that Jews are guilty (2:1-3:8), and that the whole world is accountable (3:19). Now he says the shutting-up was purposeful: consigned to disobedience in order that mercy might be shown.

Does "all" here mean every individual without exception, so that everyone is finally saved? No, and the two "all"s must be read the same way. The verse is built on the two groups the chapters have been about, Jew and Gentile: both were shut up in disobedience, and mercy comes to both. Read the second "all" as every individual and you must read the first the same way, which would contradict 9:22 and 2:5-8. Paul is saying that mercy, not merit, is the only door for anybody in either group.

And the practical force is the same as the whole unit's: nobody has standing to look down on anybody. If God shut everyone up so that everyone could receive mercy, then a Gentile congratulating itself over Israel has missed the argument entirely.

Where doctrine is supposed to end

Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments and how inscrutable his ways! Romans 11:33, ESV

After eleven chapters of the tightest argument in Scripture, Paul stops reasoning and worships. That transition is itself a piece of teaching, and it is the point this course would most like you to take from the unit.

Notice what the doxology says. God's judgments are unsearchable and his ways inscrutable: Paul has just explained more of God's purposes than any writer in history and concludes that he has been standing at the edge of an ocean. Then two questions from Isaiah 40:13 and Job 41:11: who has known the mind of the Lord, and who has ever given God anything so as to put him in debt? Both are unanswerable, and both dismantle the posture that produced the objections of 9:14 and 9:19.

Then the closing line, one of the great summaries of the Bible: "For from him and through him and to him are all things. To him be glory forever. Amen." Source, means, and goal: everything begins in God, exists through him, and returns to him. If that is true, then the doctrine of election is not a problem to be managed but a facet of the fact that God is God. And if your study of these chapters has produced arguments but not this, the chapters have not finished with you.

Application

  • About God His purposes are larger than our grasp, and knowing more of them should make us more, not less, aware of how much is beyond us.
  • About you Test your theology by where it ends. If your understanding of sovereignty makes you argumentative rather than worshipful, verse 33 is the missing step.
  • Do Begin memory verse 11 today (11:33-36). Then read the whole doxology aloud, slowly, as a prayer: it was written to be said, not analysed.

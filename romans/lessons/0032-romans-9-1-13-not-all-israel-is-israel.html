Lesson 0032 · Unit 4: Chapters 9-11

Romans 9:1-13: Not All Israel Is Israel

~30 min · Passage: Romans 9:1-13 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 9:1-13 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: how Paul establishes his sincerity, what he says he could wish for himself, the eight privileges of verses 4-5, the thesis stated in verse 6, and the two family examples used to prove it. Note especially what the second example rules out.

Recall first

Observation check

  • A triple oath (v. 1). "I am speaking the truth in Christ; I am not lying; my conscience bears me witness in the Holy Spirit." Three assurances before he says anything, because what follows will be hard to believe.
  • "Great sorrow and unceasing anguish" (v. 2). Placed immediately after 8:38-39. The man who cannot be separated from God's love is in continual grief.
  • "I could wish that I myself were accursed" (v. 3). Anathema (cut off from Christ) for the sake of his kinsmen. Compare Moses in Exodus 32:32.
  • The list finally completed (vv. 4-5). Adoption, glory, covenants, the giving of the law, worship, promises, patriarchs, and the Christ. Paul began this list at 3:2 with "to begin with" and finishes it here.
  • A doxology to Christ (v. 5). "Christ, who is God over all, blessed forever. Amen." One of the clearest statements of Christ's deity in Paul.
  • The thesis (v. 6). "But it is not as though the word of God has failed. For not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel." Everything in chapters 9-11 defends that sentence.
  • Example one: Isaac and Ishmael (vv. 7-9). Both are Abraham's sons; only one is the child of promise. Descent from Abraham was never sufficient.
  • "Children of the promise are counted as offspring" (v. 8). The counting verb again: logizomai, from chapter 4.
  • Example two: Jacob and Esau (vv. 10-13). Same father, same mother, same conception, and the choice announced "though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad".
  • The stated purpose (v. 11). "In order that God's purpose of election might continue, not because of works but because of him who calls."
  • Two quotations (vv. 12-13). Genesis 25:23 ("the older will serve the younger") and Malachi 1:2-3 ("Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated").
  • Note the timing of each. The first was spoken before birth; the second was written roughly fifteen centuries later, looking back over both nations.

Interpretation

The question that had to be asked

But it is not as though the word of God has failed. For not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel. Romans 9:6, ESV

Chapter 8 ended with an unbreakable promise. Chapter 9 opens with the fact that threatens it: God made promises to Israel, and Israel by and large has not believed. If the recipients of the clearest promises in history can end up outside, what is chapter 8 worth? Paul cannot leave this alone, not because he is defending a theory but because the assurance of every believer depends on the reliability of God's word.

His answer is stated in verse 6 and demonstrated for the rest of the chapter: the promise never attached to physical descent in the first place. "Not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel." There has always been an Israel within Israel, and God's word has done precisely what it said it would do.

Notice how the argument runs on the reader's own Scriptures. Abraham had two sons; the promise went to one, and the choice was announced before Isaac existed. Isaac had twin sons by one mother, conceived in one act, and the choice was announced before either had done anything. If Paul had used Isaac and Ishmael alone, a reader could say the difference lay in the mothers or in Ishmael's conduct. The twins remove every such explanation, which is exactly why Paul reaches for them.

Doctrine checkpoint: is this about nations or individuals?
The most common objection to the Reformed reading of Romans 9 is that Paul is discussing corporate election (nations chosen for historical roles) rather than individuals chosen for salvation. Take the objection seriously; then weigh the text.
  • The corporate element is real. Genesis 25:23 is explicitly about "two nations", and Malachi 1:2-3 looks back on Israel and Edom as peoples. Nobody should deny this.
  • But the context is individual and salvific. Paul's grief in verses 1-3 is over persons ("my kinsmen according to the flesh") and what he wishes he could suffer for them is being accursed and cut off from Christ. That is not a discussion about national vocation.
  • The chapter continues in individual terms. "It depends not on human will or exertion, but on God, who has mercy" (v. 16); "he has mercy on whomever he wills, and he hardens whomever he wills" (v. 18); the vessels of wrath and mercy of verses 22-23 are people prepared for glory.
  • The objection Paul anticipates only makes sense on the individual reading. If he were merely assigning national roles, no one would protest "Is there injustice on God's part?" (v. 14) or "Why does he still find fault?" (v. 19). Those questions arise precisely because eternal destinies are in view.
  • And a nation is composed of individuals. The corporate and individual are not rival options; God's dealings with peoples run through his dealings with persons, and the salvation of the remnant in 11:5 is by definition a matter of individuals "chosen by grace".
MacArthur, White and Durbin agree here, and White's written and spoken debates on Romans 9 are the most thorough defence of this reading available. The corporate framing is part of Paul's argument; it does not replace what he says about mercy, hardening, and calling.

"Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated"

though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad (in order that God's purpose of election might continue, not because of works but because of him who calls) Romans 9:11, ESV

Two things about this hard sentence. First, on the word "hated": in Hebrew idiom, love and hate are frequently comparative. Jacob is told he "hated" Leah when the text has just said he loved Rachel more (Gen 29:30-31), and Jesus says a disciple must "hate" father and mother (Luke 14:26). So the pairing means at minimum "chosen and not chosen", and the Malachi context is God's covenant faithfulness to one people and his judgment on another. That said, do not soften it into indifference. Malachi is speaking of real judgment on Edom, and Romans 9 goes on to speak of vessels of wrath.

Second, and more important, is Paul's stated purpose clause. The reason God announced the choice before birth and before any deed was "in order that God's purpose of election might continue, not because of works but because of him who calls." God deliberately arranged the timing so that no one could locate the ground of the choice in the person chosen. That is the same logic as 4:16, where the promise rests on faith so it may be guaranteed by grace. Election is not God responding to what he saw; it is God acting so that the outcome depends on him.

Which brings us back to why Paul is telling us any of this. The argument is not offered as a puzzle for the curious. It is the ground of assurance: if God's choice depended on anything in us, then the failure of Israel would prove the promises breakable, and so would our own failures. Because it rests on "him who calls", neither does.

The cost of holding it

Before you leave this passage, sit with verses 1-3 for a moment. Paul has just taught, and will teach again, that God chooses. He is not a man for whom the doctrine dissolves urgency or grief: he says he would accept being cut off from Christ himself if it would save his kinsmen. Whatever you conclude about election, if the doctrine leaves you cool toward the lost, you have not learned it from this chapter. Paul's version comes with anguish and prayers (10:1) attached.

Application

  • About God His word has not failed, and it never rested on human descent. What looks like a failed promise is usually a misread promise.
  • About you Every advantage of verses 4-5 was real, and none of it saved. Name the equivalent advantages you hold, and ask what they are actually for.
  • Do Read Genesis 25:19-34 and Malachi 1:1-5 today with this passage open. Then pray for one unbelieving person you love, in Paul's spirit: sorrow and intercession, not resignation.

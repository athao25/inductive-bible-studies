Lesson 0024 · Unit 3: Chapters 6-8

Romans 7:7-13: Is the Law Sin?

~25 min · Passage: Romans 7:7-13 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 7:7-13 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the question that opens the paragraph, which commandment Paul singles out and why that one is significant, how sin is described as an agent, the meaning of "apart from the law sin lies dead", the three adjectives applied to the law in verse 12, and what verse 13 says the law's exposure of sin accomplishes.

Recall first

Observation check

  • The question, and the strongest possible denial (v. 7). "Is the law sin? By no means!" Paul has said the law arouses passions (7:5) and increases trespass (5:20), and he now protects the law's honour.
  • The shift to "I" (v. 7). From here to the end of the chapter Paul writes in the first person. Whose "I" this is becomes the chapter's central interpretive question.
  • The tenses change too. Verses 7-13 are past tense; verses 14-25 are present. That shift is a major clue for reading the second half.
  • The commandment chosen (v. 7). "You shall not covet": the tenth, and the only one that is entirely internal. No court can prosecute it, and no outward performance can satisfy it.
  • Sin personified (vv. 8, 11). Sin "seized an opportunity", "produced in me", "deceived me", "killed me". The word for opportunity is a military term: a base of operations, a bridgehead.
  • "Apart from the law, sin lies dead" (v. 8). Dormant rather than absent, like a sleeping animal, not a nonexistent one.
  • "I was once alive apart from the law" (v. 9). A description of unthreatened self-confidence before the commandment struck home, not of literal innocence.
  • "The very commandment that promised life proved to be death to me" (v. 10). An echo of Leviticus 18:5: "if a person does them, he shall live by them."
  • "Deceived me" (v. 11). The verb used of the serpent with Eve (Gen 3:13; 2 Cor 11:3). Paul is telling every person's story in the shape of Eden's.
  • Three verdicts on the law (v. 12). Holy, righteous, good. And the commandment itself is called holy: the very one that occasioned the death.
  • The purpose (v. 13). "In order that sin might be shown to be sin… that sin might become sinful beyond measure." The law's exposure is not a malfunction; it is the design.

Interpretation

How a good law produces a bad result

But sin, seizing an opportunity through the commandment, produced in me all kinds of covetousness. For apart from the law, sin lies dead. Romans 7:8, ESV

Paul's defence of the law is also his most penetrating analysis of sin. The commandment is holy; the fault lies in what it lands on. Sin uses the command as a bridgehead: the Greek aphorme is the base from which a military operation is launched. The prohibition does not create the desire; it gives the desire a target and reveals its character. This is why the tenth commandment is the perfect test case: "you shall not covet" cannot be satisfied by any outward performance, so it exposes what a person actually is rather than what they can manage to do.

Notice the two things the law accomplishes here, both of them good. It informs: "if it had not been for the law, I would not have known sin" (v. 7). And it provokes: sin, meeting the command, springs to life and reveals its own nature. Verse 13 names the purpose of the second effect: "in order that sin might be shown to be sin, and through the commandment might become sinful beyond measure." A disease that hides is more dangerous than one that produces visible symptoms. The law forces the symptoms.

And there is an Eden shape underneath the whole paragraph. A command given. A tempter seizing the opportunity in the command. Deception. Death. Paul says "sin deceived me and through it killed me", using the word Eve uses in Genesis 3:13. Every person relives Adam's story in miniature, and the law is the point at which the story becomes conscious.

Doctrine checkpoint: who is the "I" of Romans 7?
This is the chapter's great disputed question. It matters most for verses 14-25, which you meet tomorrow, but it starts here. Three serious readings:
  • Paul before conversion, autobiographically. The Pharisee who thought himself "blameless as to righteousness under the law" (Phil 3:6) until the tenth commandment exposed him. This fits "I was once alive apart from the law" (v. 9) well.
  • Paul as a believer, describing present Christian experience. The tense shift to the present at verse 14 supports it, and the anguish of verses 15-24 matches what mature Christians report about themselves.
  • Paul speaking representatively: Adam, and Israel under the law. "I was once alive apart from the law, but when the commandment came, sin came alive and I died" fits Adam exactly and Israel at Sinai closely. The "I" is a rhetorical device for the human story.
For verses 7-13, the past tenses and the Eden vocabulary favour the third reading with the first folded into it: Paul is telling the human story through his own. Tomorrow's lesson takes up the harder question for verses 14-25, where the present tense changes the terms. What all readings agree on: the law is not the villain, sin is; and law alone cannot rescue anyone, whoever the "I" is.

The commandment that promised life

The very commandment that promised life proved to be death to me. Romans 7:10, ESV

Behind this stands Leviticus 18:5, quoted again at Romans 10:5: "if a person does them, he shall live by them." The offer was genuine. Perfect obedience would indeed yield life; nothing in Romans denies it. The catastrophe is not in the terms but in the party attempting them. To a sinner, an offer of life on condition of perfect obedience is a sentence of death, because the condition cannot be met.

That is worth sitting with, because it explains why so much sincere religion produces despair or self-deception and nothing in between. Face the law honestly and it kills; evade it and you must lower it to something you can manage, which is exactly what the Pharisee did with the tenth commandment by treating covetousness as an internal matter beneath legal notice. Paul had been that man, and the commandment found him.

So the chapter's answer to "is the law sin?" is a resounding no, followed by something more useful than a defence. The law is holy, righteous and good, and it is working perfectly when it drives you to despair of yourself. A mirror that flatters is broken. This one does not, and that is why 8:1-4 is such a relief: what the law "could not do, weakened by the flesh", God did by sending his own Son.

Application

  • About God He gave a law that would expose rather than a law that could be managed. That is severe kindness: only a fully honest diagnosis makes a real cure findable.
  • About you Which commandment finds you the way the tenth found Paul? Not the one you keep well but the one that reaches inside. That is the one doing its job.
  • Do Read Exodus 20:17 and Philippians 3:4-9 together today. Note what Paul once counted as blameless and what he counted it as afterwards, and write one sentence on what changed.

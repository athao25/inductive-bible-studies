Lesson 0018 · Unit 2: Chapters 3:21-5:21

Romans 5:6-11: While We Were Still Sinners

~25 min · Passage: Romans 5:6-11 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 5:6-11 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the four descriptions of our condition, the timing marker repeated in verses 6 and 8, the comparison Paul makes with human self-sacrifice, the two "much more" arguments and what each infers, and the note of rejoicing that closes the paragraph.

Recall first

Observation check

  • Four descriptions of us (vv. 6, 8, 10). Weak, ungodly, sinners, enemies. Each is worse than the last, and the paragraph gets darker as it gets warmer.
  • "At the right time" (v. 6). The cross was scheduled. Compare Galatians 4:4, "when the fullness of time had come."
  • Human analogy, carefully graded (v. 7). Scarcely for a righteous person; perhaps for a good person someone would dare to die. Paul concedes the best that human love achieves, and then goes past it.
  • "But God shows his love for us" (v. 8). Present tense: the cross is a permanent demonstration, not a past event that has faded.
  • "While we were still sinners" (v. 8). The timing clause that rules out every version of "God loved us because he foresaw what we would become."
  • Two "much more" arguments (vv. 9-10). An argument from the greater to the lesser: if God did the hard thing for enemies, the easier thing for friends is certain.
  • "Saved by him from the wrath of God" (v. 9). Salvation is defined here by what we are saved from, and it points back to 1:18.
  • "Reconciled… by the death of his Son… saved by his life" (v. 10). Two verbs, two stages: reconciliation accomplished by his death, final salvation secured by his living intercession.
  • Note the tense shift in verse 9-10. Justified and reconciled are past; "shall be saved" is future. Salvation in Romans has a completed past, a present, and a future.
  • "We also rejoice in God" (v. 11). The boasting word again, third time in this chapter, and now with the highest possible object, not in his gifts but in him.

Interpretation

The proof of love is dated, not felt

but God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us. Romans 5:8, ESV

Paul has just said God's love has been poured into our hearts (5:5). Now he anchors that experience in something outside the heart, because feelings need evidence and a bad week can erase them. The demonstration is an event with a location and a date: Christ died. Not "God says he loves us", which anyone could say, but a costly act performed at the precise moment we were least deserving of it.

Watch the argument of verse 7. Paul concedes the summit of human love: occasionally someone will die for a genuinely good person, a child, a comrade. That is real and rare. Then verse 8 goes past it in the only direction available: God's love was displayed not for the good but for the hostile. Human love at its noblest is drawn out by worth in the beloved; this love creates worth where there was none.

Which is why the timing clause matters so much. "While we were still sinners" removes every version of the idea that God's love is a response to something in us, including the subtle version most Christians actually live by, in which God's affection rises and falls with our week. If his love began while we were enemies, nothing in our subsequent behaviour is the reason for it, and nothing in our subsequent behaviour is its ground.

Doctrine checkpoint: the "much more" arguments (5:9-10)
Paul uses a standard Jewish form of reasoning, from the harder case to the easier: qal wahomer, "light and heavy". Both arguments run the same way, and they are the strongest assurance texts in the letter so far.
  • Argument one (v. 9). If God justified us at the cost of his Son's blood while we were guilty, much more will he save us from the coming wrath now that we are justified. The hardest part is already done.
  • Argument two (v. 10). If we were reconciled while enemies by his death, much more shall we be saved by his life. Enemies were reconciled by a dead Christ; friends will certainly be kept by a living one. Hebrews 7:25 spells out the mechanism: he "always lives to make intercession for them".
  • What this does to assurance. The logic makes final salvation a matter of God's consistency rather than our reliability. Anyone who fears that God, having saved them, might grow tired and let them go is being asked here to explain why the God who paid for enemies would abandon children. Chapter 8:31-39 will restate the same argument at full volume.

Reconciliation, and who moved

For if while we were enemies we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son, much more, now that we are reconciled, shall we be saved by his life. Romans 5:10, ESV

Reconciliation is the fourth great picture of salvation in this letter, alongside justification (the courtroom), redemption (the slave market), and propitiation (the temple). This one comes from personal relationships: two parties at enmity are made friends. And the passive voice is doing careful work: "we were reconciled", not "we reconciled ourselves". The offended party took the initiative and bore the cost.

Notice the word "enemies". It is stronger than most of us are comfortable with, and it cuts in both directions: we were hostile to God (8:7 will say the mind of the flesh is hostility toward God), and we were objects of his righteous wrath (1:18). Sentimental accounts of the atonement usually keep the first half and drop the second, leaving a God who was never opposed to us and therefore never had anything to overcome. Romans keeps both, which is what makes reconciliation an achievement rather than a misunderstanding cleared up.

And then verse 11: "we also rejoice in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received reconciliation." The paragraph does not end with our safety. It ends with God himself as the object of joy. That is the final answer to the boasting question raised at 3:27, and it is worth noticing how far Paul has travelled since chapter 1; from wrath revealed against people who would not give thanks, to people who exult in the God they once hated.

Application

  • About God He loved his enemies before they stopped being enemies, and he proved it at a cost he could not recover. That is the pattern Jesus commands in Matthew 5:44, and it began with us.
  • About you If you cannot feel God's love this week, verse 8 tells you where to look instead: not inward but backward, to an event. Feelings follow facts, sometimes slowly.
  • Do Begin memory verse 5 today (5:8), keeping "But God" attached. Then apply verse 10's logic to whatever you most fear about your future with God, in writing, in one sentence.

Church prompt
Ask someone how they would answer a believer who says, "I know God saved me, but I'm not sure he'll keep me." Romans 5:9-10 is the answer, and it is an argument rather than a reassurance.

Questions welcome, always: "what is the difference between a righteous man and a good man in verse 7?", "were we really God's enemies, or only he ours?", "if we are already justified, what are we still to be saved from?" Ask your teacher; verse 9 answers the third with one word: wrath.

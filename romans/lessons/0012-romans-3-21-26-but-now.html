Lesson 0012 · Unit 2: Chapters 3:21-5:21

Romans 3:21-26: But Now

~30 min · Passage: Romans 3:21-26 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 3:21-26 three times in your ESV, stop and do that now. Six verses, and the densest paragraph in Scripture. Write at least five observations: the two words that open it, what is manifested and apart from what, who bears witness to it, the great nouns of verses 24-25, and the two purposes introduced by "so that" and "so that" in verses 25-26.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "But now" (v. 21). The hinge of the letter. "But" reverses everything since 1:18; "now" marks a moment in history: this happened, at a date, in public.
  • "Apart from the law… although the Law and the Prophets bear witness to it" (v. 21). Both halves matter. Not produced by law-keeping, yet not a novelty: the Old Testament testified to it, which chapter 4 will demonstrate from Abraham and David.
  • "For all who believe" and "there is no distinction" (v. 22). The scope clause, aimed straight at the Roman fracture.
  • "All have sinned and fall short" (v. 23). The verb tenses differ: sinned (a completed fact) and fall short (a present, continuing state). Not only did we sin; we are short.
  • "Justified by his grace as a gift, through the redemption" (v. 24). Three of the letter's key terms in one clause: justified, grace, redemption. Note "as a gift": the Greek adverb means gratis, without cause in the recipient.
  • "Whom God put forward" (v. 25). God is the subject. The cross is not humanity placating God; it is God providing the means.
  • "A propitiation by his blood, to be received by faith" (v. 25). Propitiation is hilasterion, the word the Greek Old Testament uses for the mercy seat.
  • Two purpose clauses (vv. 25-26). "To show his righteousness, because in his divine forbearance he had passed over former sins" and "so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus." Both are about vindicating God, not merely about saving us.
  • "At the present time" (v. 26). Paired with "but now" in verse 21, framing the paragraph as a historical announcement.
  • Faith is mentioned four times in six verses. And works are mentioned once, to exclude them (v. 20 just before, v. 27 just after).

Interpretation

Propitiation: the word the paragraph turns on

whom God put forward as a propitiation by his blood, to be received by faith. This was to show God's righteousness, because in his divine forbearance he had passed over former sins. Romans 3:25, ESV

Some translations render hilasterion as "sacrifice of atonement" or "expiation". The distinction is not pedantry. Expiation means the removal of sin's stain: something done to sin. Propitiation means the turning away of wrath: something done to God's just anger. Romans has spent 1:18-3:20 establishing that there is real wrath to be dealt with, so the fuller word is the one the argument requires. C. H. Dodd's mid-century attempt to reduce the term to expiation was answered decisively by Leon Morris and Roger Nicole, and the ESV's "propitiation" reflects that outcome.

There is a second layer. In the Greek Old Testament, hilasterion is the standard word for the kapporeth, the golden lid of the ark: the mercy seat, where on the Day of Atonement the high priest sprinkled blood, once a year, in the dark, alone (Lev 16:14-15). Above that lid God was enthroned between the cherubim; beneath it lay the tablets of the broken law. Blood came between the two. Paul says God has now "put forward" (publicly displayed) the true mercy seat, not hidden behind a curtain but exhibited in daylight on a Roman cross. Read Leviticus 16 this week with this verse open; the paragraph will double in weight.

Doctrine checkpoint: why did God need to demonstrate his righteousness?
Verse 25 gives a reason for the cross that most of us never think of: "because in his divine forbearance he had passed over former sins." The problem it solves is this, for centuries God had forgiven sinners. David committed adultery and murder and was told "the LORD also has put away your sin" (2 Sam 12:13). On what basis? The blood of bulls and goats cannot take away sins (Heb 10:4). So had God simply overlooked evil?
  • That charge is what the cross answers. The demonstration in view is not chiefly a display of love (that is 5:8) but a vindication of justice. God had been running a moral deficit in the eyes of the universe, forgiving without payment. At the cross the account is settled: retrospectively for every sin passed over, and prospectively for every sin forgiven since.
  • Verse 26 is the summit. "So that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus." Anyone can be just by condemning the guilty. Anyone can be a justifier by ignoring the crime. Only at the cross is God both at once, and the word joining them, and, is the most expensive word in Scripture.
  • This is why the atonement must be substitutionary. If Christ's death did not actually bear the penalty owed, then God is still forgiving without justice, and verse 26 is false. Every alternative theory of the atonement that removes penal substitution leaves this sentence with nothing to say.

What justification is, and what it is not

and are justified by his grace as a gift, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus Romans 3:24, ESV

To justify is a courtroom verb, not a workshop one. It does not mean to make someone righteous by degrees; it means to declare someone righteous, as a judge does. That is why Scripture can use the word of God himself: "that you may be justified in your words" (3:4), where no change of character can possibly be in view. Vindicated, declared in the right.

Three qualifiers in verse 24 tell you everything about how it comes. By his grace: the source is God's free favour. As a gift: the cost to the recipient is nothing. Through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus: the cost to God was everything, because redemption means release secured by payment of a price. Free is not the same as cheap. It was free to us because it was not free to him.

And the location of the righteousness matters. It is God's righteousness, revealed and given, "through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe" (v. 22). Faith is not a work that earns the verdict; it is the empty hand that receives it. Luther's word for the resulting status was iustitia aliena: an alien righteousness, one belonging to another and credited to us. Chapter 4 will prove that this is exactly what happened to Abraham, and chapter 5 will explain how one man's obedience can count for many.

Application

  • About God He would not save at the expense of his justice. A God who forgave by lowering the standard would be no comfort to anyone who has been wronged. This one absorbed the cost himself.
  • About you "There is no distinction" (v. 22) cuts two ways. It denies you any advantage over anyone else, and it removes every disqualification you thought you had.
  • Do Read Leviticus 16:11-19 today with 3:25 open. Then say verse 26 aloud slowly, pausing at "and": "that he might be just (and) the justifier." Sit with that word for a minute; it cost the death of the Son.

Church prompt
Ask someone at church how God can forgive sin and still be just. If the answer is "because he's loving", press gently: Romans 3:26 says something more costly and more specific.

Questions welcome, always: "what is the difference between propitiation and expiation?", "were Old Testament believers saved on credit?", "does 'faith in Jesus Christ' mean our faith or his faithfulness?" Ask your teacher; the last is a genuine scholarly debate, and the letter's argument works either way because 3:22 immediately adds "for all who believe".

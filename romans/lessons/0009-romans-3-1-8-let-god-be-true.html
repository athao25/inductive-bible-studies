Lesson 0009 · Unit 1: Chapters 1:1-3:20

Romans 3:1-8: Let God Be True

~25 min · Passage: Romans 3:1-8 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 3:1-8 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the question that opens the chapter and why it arises from chapter 2, what Paul says the Jews were entrusted with, the three objections raised, the quotation in verse 4, and the accusation Paul reports in verse 8. Mark clearly which lines are Paul's and which belong to his objector.

Recall first

Observation check

  • The question follows directly from 2:28-29 (v. 1). If a real Jew is one inwardly, then what advantage is there in being a Jew outwardly? Paul is aware of what his own argument has just cost.
  • "Much in every way" (v. 2). He does not retreat. The privileges of chapter 2 were real and the answer here is emphatic.
  • "To begin with" (v. 2). Paul starts a list, and never finishes it here. He picks it up at 9:4-5, where the full catalogue arrives: adoption, glory, covenants, law, worship, promises, patriarchs, and the Christ.
  • "The oracles of God" (v. 2). The first advantage is the Scriptures. Israel was the custodian of God's own speech.
  • Objection one (v. 3). "What if some were unfaithful? Does their faithlessness nullify the faithfulness of God?" Answer: "By no means!", and then me genoito, the strongest negation available, and the first of ten in this letter.
  • "Let God be true though every one were a liar" (v. 4). A rule of interpretation as much as a doctrine: when God's word and universal human opinion conflict, the arithmetic is settled in advance.
  • The quotation (v. 4). Psalm 51:4, David's confession after Bathsheba. Chosen carefully: the psalm where a covenant member admits his sin and vindicates God's verdict.
  • Objection two (v. 5). If our unrighteousness shows God's righteousness, is God unjust to inflict wrath? Paul answers with an argument from the last judgment: "How could God judge the world?"
  • "I speak in a human way" (v. 5). An apology for even framing the question. Paul is uncomfortable putting the words on paper.
  • Objection three, and a real slander (vv. 7-8). "Why not do evil that good may come?, as some people slanderously charge us with saying." His gospel was already being caricatured as a licence to sin, and he will answer that charge properly at 6:1.
  • "Their condemnation is just" (v. 8). The shortest verdict in the chapter, and Paul does not soften it.

Interpretation

Why the digression is not a digression

At first glance Paul interrupts his march toward 3:20 to fend off three quibbles. In fact he is protecting the foundation of everything he will build later. If human unfaithfulness could cancel God's promises, then the gospel he is about to announce would rest on nothing, since it too is a promise. The question "does God keep his word to a people who broke theirs?" is not a side issue. It is the question chapters 9-11 exist to answer, and here Paul plants the flag early: me genoito.

What if some were unfaithful? Does their faithlessness nullify the faithfulness of God? By no means! Let God be true though every one were a liar. Romans 3:3-4, ESV

Notice the asymmetry Paul is defending. God's faithfulness is not contingent on ours; it is the fixed point around which everything else moves. That is why the letter can promise at 8:38-39 that nothing separates us from his love, and why it can insist at 11:29 that "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable". Cut this verse and both of those promises lose their guarantee.

And the second half of verse 4 gives you a working rule for the whole Christian life: when your experience, your feelings, or the consensus of everyone you know contradicts what God has said, the correct conclusion is not that God has failed. "Let God be true though every one were a liar" is not anti-intellectualism; it is knowing which claim in a contradiction has the better track record.

Doctrine checkpoint: the objection Paul refuses to answer yet
Verse 8 records that Paul was being accused of teaching "let us do evil that good may come". That charge is the standard reaction to grace, and it is worth noticing three things about it.
  • It is evidence that Paul really taught free grace. Nobody ever accused a moralist of encouraging sin. If your gospel has never once been misheard this way, it may not be Paul's gospel. As one preacher put it, the doctrine of justification by faith alone has not been preached properly until someone objects that it lets people sin.
  • Paul does not answer it here. He simply says the slander is condemned, and moves on. The real answer takes two chapters and arrives at 6:1-14, where it turns out that the reason a Christian will not go on sinning is not fear of penalty but union with a crucified and risen Christ.
  • The charge is a caricature, not a summary. Grace does not merely forgive; it changes the person forgiven. "Sin will have no dominion over you, since you are not under law but under grace" (6:14). Note which way that sentence runs.

Advantage, honestly assessed

Hold verses 1-2 and 3:9 together, because they look contradictory until you see the two questions being asked. "What advantage has the Jew? Much in every way" (vv. 1-2). "Are we Jews any better off? No, not at all" (v. 9). The first question is about privilege: were the Scriptures, the covenants, and the promises a real gift? Yes, immeasurably. The second is about standing before the judge: does that gift produce an acquittal? Not at all.

The distinction is worth carrying into your own life. Advantages are real: a Christian home, sound teaching, decades of good books, a church that preaches the text. Paul would never call them nothing; he calls them "much in every way". But they are stewardship, not status. They raise accountability rather than lowering the bar. The person who has heard the gospel four thousand times and never bent the knee is in a worse position than the one who has heard it once, not a better one.

Application

  • About God His faithfulness does not float on ours. That is the only reason any promise in this letter is worth anything, and the reason chapter 8 can end where it does.
  • About you When Scripture and your experience disagree, which do you instinctively adjust? Verse 4 tells you which one is more likely to be wrong, and the answer is not flattering.
  • Do Read Psalm 51 in full today, slowly. It is where verse 4 comes from, and it shows you what it looks like when a guilty covenant member stops defending himself and takes God's side against himself.

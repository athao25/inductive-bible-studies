Lesson 0042 · Unit 5: Chapters 12-16

Romans 12:3-8: One Body, Many Members

~25 min · Passage: Romans 12:3-8 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 12:3-8 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the first application of the renewed mind, what "sober judgment" is measured by, the body image and what it rules out, the seven gifts listed, and the qualifier attached to each. Ask why this comes immediately after verses 1-2.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "By the grace given to me" (v. 3). Paul grounds his own authority in grace before telling anyone how to think of themselves. The instruction is modelled before it is given.
  • The first application of the renewed mind is self-estimate (v. 3). "Not to think of himself more highly than he ought to think, but to think with sober judgment."
  • A wordplay on thinking. Verse 3 uses four related Greek words for thinking and estimating in a single sentence: the renewed mind of verse 2 immediately at work.
  • The measure (v. 3). "Each according to the measure of faith that God has assigned." The yardstick is what God gave, not what others have.
  • The body image (vv. 4-5). "Many members… individually members one of another." Note the last phrase: not just members of the body but of each other.
  • Gifts differ "according to the grace given to us" (v. 6). Charismata from charis: gifts from grace, which settles the question of whether they can be boasted in.
  • Seven gifts (vv. 6-8). Prophecy, service, teaching, exhortation, giving, leading, mercy. A mixed list of speaking and doing, public and quiet.
  • Each gift gets a manner (vv. 6-8). In proportion to faith; in serving; in teaching; in exhortation; with generosity; with zeal; with cheerfulness. The how matters as much as the what.
  • The list is not ranked and not exhaustive. Compare 1 Corinthians 12 and 1 Peter 4:10-11, which name different gifts. No two New Testament lists match.
  • Ordinary gifts sit beside spectacular ones. Prophecy is first; giving, leading and showing mercy are on the same list, without apology.

Interpretation

Why self-estimate is the first ethical topic

For by the grace given to me I say to everyone among you not to think of himself more highly than he ought to think, but to think with sober judgment, each according to the measure of faith that God has assigned. Romans 12:3, ESV

Given twelve chapters to work with, Paul makes his first concrete application of the renewed mind a matter of how you assess yourself. That is not an accident, and its placement makes most sense against the background of the letter. The congregation is divided; chapter 11 had to warn Gentiles, "do not become proud, but fear"; the whole argument of chapters 9-11 was aimed at humility. So the first thing a renewed mind produces is an accurate self-estimate.

Note that Paul does not say to think lowly of yourself. He says not to think of yourself more highly than you ought, and to use "sober judgment". False modesty is not the goal; accuracy is. And the measure supplied is "the measure of faith that God has assigned": that is, what you actually received. Comparison with other people is quietly removed as a yardstick, which cuts off inflation and deflation at once. You cannot boast about a gift you were given, and you cannot despise a gift the Giver chose to give you.

Members of one another

so we, though many, are one body in Christ, and individually members one of another. Romans 12:5, ESV

The body image does two things simultaneously, and both were needed in Rome. It establishes diversity: many members, different functions, no expectation that everyone is the same. And it establishes dependence: a hand has no independent existence, and a foot cannot resign.

Then Paul adds a phrase that goes beyond the standard version of the metaphor. Ancient writers used the body image for the state (Livy tells the fable of the belly and the limbs) usually to persuade lower classes to accept their place. Paul's version says "members one of another": belonging horizontally, to each other, not merely each to the head. Roman readers would have heard the familiar image and then the unfamiliar turn.

The practical implication is one modern Christians resist. On this account you cannot be a Christian in isolation, not because church attendance is a rule but because you are a body part. A severed hand is not an independent hand; it is a dying one. And notice the ordering: your gift exists for the body's use, not for your fulfilment. That reframes the question people usually ask about gifts: "what is mine?" becomes "what does this body need that God has equipped me to supply?"

Doctrine checkpoint: reading the gift lists
Four observations that keep this passage from generating anxiety or pride.
  • No list in the New Testament is complete. Romans 12 names seven; 1 Corinthians 12 names others; 1 Peter 4:10-11 reduces them to two broad categories, speaking and serving. Different lists for different purposes means these are illustrations rather than a taxonomy.
  • The manner is commanded as firmly as the gift. Give "with generosity", lead "with zeal", show mercy "with cheerfulness". A gift exercised sourly is disobedience in the same breath as it is obedience, and note how much of the list is quiet, practical work rather than platform ministry.
  • Gifts are grace, so they measure nothing about you. Charismata from charis. Verse 6 says they differ "according to the grace given to us", which is the same reasoning that excluded boasting at 3:27.
  • Live gifts before you label them. The passage does not tell anyone to take an inventory and discover their gift before serving; it tells people who are already serving to do it in proportion to faith and with the right spirit. Most people discover what they have been given by doing what needs doing.
On the disputed question of whether gifts like prophecy continue today, this course notes that Romans 12 does not address it. MacArthur argues from elsewhere that the revelatory gifts have ceased; others disagree. What Romans 12 requires of every reader, on any view, is that they use what they have been given, in the manner prescribed, for the body's good.

Application

  • About God He distributes deliberately and unequally, and the inequality is not injustice: it is design for a body that needs different parts.
  • About you Take the sober judgment test in both directions. Where do you overestimate yourself, and where do you use "I'm not gifted" as a way of avoiding work the body needs?
  • Do Name one thing your congregation needs that you could supply, and one gift in the verses 6-8 list that you have been exercising in the wrong manner: sourly, grudgingly, or for recognition. Fix the second this week.

Check yourself

Before next lesson
Read Romans 12:9-21 in your ESV, twice, and jot five observations. Watch: how many commands appear and how short they are, the first one and why it governs the rest, the treatment of enemies, the two quotations from the Old Testament, and the final command of verse 21. It reads like a list; look for the structure inside it.
Primary source
In your MacArthur Study Bible, read the notes on 12:3 (sober judgment, measure of faith) and 12:6-8 (the gifts). Then compare 1 Corinthians 12:4-11 and 1 Peter 4:10-11 with this list.
Church prompt
Ask someone in your congregation what gift they see in you. Most people are poor judges of their own, which is part of why verse 5 says we are members of one another.

Questions welcome, always: "what is 'the measure of faith'?", "do all the gifts continue today?", "how do I find mine?" Ask your teacher; on the last, the passage suggests you find it by serving, not by testing.

Lesson 0014 · Unit 2: Chapters 3:21-5:21

Romans 4:1-8: Abraham Believed God

~25 min · Passage: Romans 4:1-8 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 4:1-8 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: which Old Testament figures Paul calls as witnesses, how many times a form of "count" or "credit" appears, the contrast between wages and gift in verses 4-5, the phrase that should shock you in verse 5, and what David's psalm contributes that Genesis did not say.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "What then shall we say was gained by Abraham?" (v. 1). Paul reaches for the strongest possible test case. If justification by faith cannot be proved from Abraham, it cannot be proved at all.
  • "Our forefather according to the flesh" (v. 1). Paul writes as a Jew to a mixed church, and by verse 11 Abraham will have become the father of believing Gentiles too.
  • The hypothetical is granted and then closed (v. 2). "If Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about", and then, decisively, "but not before God."
  • The proof text (v. 3). Genesis 15:6: "Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness." Everything in this chapter is exegesis of that one sentence.
  • "Counted" is the key word. Greek logizomai, a bookkeeping term: to reckon to an account. It appears eleven times in chapter 4, and four times in these eight verses alone.
  • Wages versus gift (v. 4). "To the one who works, his wages are not counted as a gift but as his due." If you earn it, calling it grace is a category error.
  • "To the one who does not work but believes" (v. 5). The contrast is not between kinds of work but between working and believing.
  • "Him who justifies the ungodly" (v. 5). The most startling phrase in the chapter. Exodus 23:7 says God "will not acquit the wicked"; Proverbs 17:15 calls justifying the wicked an abomination. Paul says God does exactly that, which is why 3:25-26 had to come first.
  • David is called as second witness (vv. 6-8). Psalm 32:1-2, on the blessing of the man whose sins are covered and against whom the Lord will not count sin.
  • The two psalm lines are joined by the same verb. "Counted righteous apart from works" (v. 6) and "will not count his sin" (v. 8). Positive and negative sides of one transaction.
  • David's own case is loaded. Psalm 32 belongs to the aftermath of adultery and murder. Paul's example of a man forgiven apart from works is a man whose works were indefensible.

Interpretation

Credited, not earned

Now to the one who works, his wages are not counted as a gift but as his due. And to the one who does not work but believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is counted as righteousness. Romans 4:4-5, ESV

Paul's argument here is essentially about accounting. There are two ways an amount can appear in your account: you can earn it, in which case it is a debt owed to you, or it can be credited by someone else, in which case it is a gift. These are mutually exclusive categories, and Paul will state the exclusivity flatly at 11:6: "if it is by grace, it is no longer on the basis of works; otherwise grace would no longer be grace."

So what exactly is credited? Not faith as though faith itself were the righteousness: that would make believing a very small work that earns a very large wage, which is precisely the structure verse 4 rules out. Faith is the instrument, the empty hand. What is credited is a righteousness that belongs to another: 5:19 will say that "by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous", and 2 Corinthians 5:21 puts both directions of the transaction in one line: "he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God."

This is the doctrine of imputation, and it is the reason Christian assurance is possible. If the righteousness credited to you were produced in you, your confidence would rise and fall with your performance. Because it is credited from outside, the ground of your standing today is exactly what it was on your worst day and your best one.

Doctrine checkpoint: "him who justifies the ungodly"
Take the force of verse 5 before you soften it. Under the law, a judge who acquitted a guilty man was corrupt: "Do not acquit the guilty" (Ex 23:7); "He who justifies the wicked and he who condemns the righteous are both alike an abomination to the LORD" (Prov 17:15). Paul now describes God doing the very thing his law calls an abomination.
  • The paradox is intentional. Paul is not being careless; he has just spent 3:21-26 explaining how the acquittal can be righteous. The mercy seat is what makes verse 5 sayable without blasphemy.
  • The person justified is described as ungodly, not as improving. Not "the sincere", not "the seeking", not "the reformed". Whatever qualification you were tempted to import, this phrase excludes it.
  • Abraham himself was such a man. Joshua 24:2 says that Abraham's family "served other gods" beyond the River. God's call came to an idolater, and Genesis 15:6 came before circumcision, before Moriah, before the law existed.
Any doctrine of justification that requires the candidate to be less than ungodly at the moment of justification has left Romans 4 behind.

Why David is the second witness

"Blessed are those whose lawless deeds are forgiven, and whose sins are covered; blessed is the man against whom the Lord will not count his sin." Romans 4:7-8, ESV, quoting Psalm 32:1-2

Jewish interpretive practice paired texts by shared vocabulary, and Paul does it here: Genesis 15:6 says God counts righteousness to a man; Psalm 32:2 says God does not count sin against a man. The same verb links them, and the two halves make one transaction: sin not reckoned against you, righteousness reckoned to you. Forgiveness alone would leave you at zero; justification puts something in the account.

The choice of witnesses is also strategic. Abraham is the beginning of the nation and David is its ideal king: the two people a first-century Jewish reader would think of first, and the two hardest cases to explain away. And Paul takes David not from a psalm of triumph but from the aftermath of the worst thing he ever did. If the archetypal patriarch was justified before he had done anything, and the archetypal king was blessed after he had done something unforgivable, then the door 3:21-26 opened is wide enough for anyone in Rome, Jew or Gentile, respectable or ruined.

Application

  • About God He keeps books, and he closes accounts. "Will not count his sin" is not divine forgetfulness; it is a deliberate act, made possible at cost.
  • About you If you are trying to be less ungodly before you come, verse 5 has already ruled that out. The category God justifies is the one you are in.
  • Do Read Genesis 15 and Psalm 32 today, in that order, ten minutes total. Write one sentence on each: what was Abraham asked to believe, and what did David say it cost him to stay silent about his sin?

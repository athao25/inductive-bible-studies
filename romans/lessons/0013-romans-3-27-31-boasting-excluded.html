Lesson 0013 · Unit 2: Chapters 3:21-5:21

Romans 3:27-31: Boasting Excluded

~25 min · Passage: Romans 3:27-31 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 3:27-31 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: what is excluded and by what "law", the summary in verse 28, the confession Paul quotes in verse 30, the argument he draws from it, and the objection and answer of verse 31. Mark which lines belong to the objector.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "Then what becomes of our boasting?" (v. 27). Paul asks the question himself. After 3:21-26, boasting is not merely wrong; it has become impossible, and the verb he uses means excluded, shut out.
  • "By what kind of law? By a law of works? No, but by the law of faith" (v. 27). Nomos here means principle or system. Paul is playing with the word deliberately, and the play continues into verse 31.
  • "For we hold" (v. 28). A formula of settled conviction, introducing the letter's most compressed doctrinal statement: "one is justified by faith apart from works of the law."
  • The argument turns monotheistic (vv. 29-30). "Or is God the God of Jews only? Is he not the God of Gentiles also?" The proof for justification by faith is the oneness of God.
  • The Shema stands underneath (v. 30). "Since God is one" echoes Deuteronomy 6:4, the confession every Jewish reader recited daily.
  • Two prepositions, deliberately varied (v. 30). He justifies the circumcised by faith and the uncircumcised through faith. Different wording, identical route: a stylistic refusal to let anyone find two doors.
  • The final objection (v. 31). "Do we then overthrow the law by this faith?" The expected charge, since Paul has just said justification is apart from law.
  • "By no means! On the contrary, we uphold the law" (v. 31). The seventh me genoito so far, and a claim Paul does not explain here. Chapter 4 is the explanation.

Interpretation

Why boasting is not merely discouraged but excluded

Then what becomes of our boasting? It is excluded. By what kind of law? By a law of works? No, but by the law of faith. Romans 3:27, ESV

Paul does not say boasting is unattractive or immature. He says the door has been shut on it by the structure of the gospel itself. Any system in which you contribute leaves you something to mention; a system in which the verdict is a gift received by empty hands leaves nothing. Notice that this is an argument about mechanism, not about manners. Humility here is not a virtue you cultivate but a fact you concede.

This matters because boasting has been a running thread since 2:17 ("you… boast in God") and 2:23 ("you who boast in the law"). Paul has watched a congregation split along lines of ethnic and religious pride, and his solution is not a plea for better attitudes. It is a doctrine that makes the pride unintelligible. If you were justified freely as a gift while ungodly, then the person across the aisle who was justified in exactly the same way is not below you. The theology dismantles the sociology.

And note where boasting goes rather than being merely destroyed. In 5:2-3 and 5:11 the same word group returns: we rejoice (literally boast) in hope of the glory of God, in our sufferings, and in God himself through our Lord Jesus Christ. Boasting is not abolished; it is redirected to an object that can bear the weight.

Doctrine checkpoint: "faith apart from works of the law" and James 2:24
Verse 28 is the sentence Luther famously translated with the word "alone" added ("allein durch den Glauben") and was attacked for it. Meanwhile James writes, "a person is justified by works and not by faith alone" (Jas 2:24). Two apostles, opposite sentences. How do they fit?
  • They are answering different questions. Paul asks how a guilty sinner gets a right standing before God; his opponent is the man trusting his own obedience. James asks how you tell a living faith from a dead one; his opponent is the man claiming faith with no life attached (Jas 2:14-17).
  • They use "justify" differently. Paul uses it of God's declaration; James uses it in the sense of demonstration or vindication: Abraham's obedience at Moriah "fulfilled" the verdict pronounced in Genesis 15 decades earlier (Jas 2:23).
  • They quote the same verse and agree about it. Both cite Genesis 15:6. Paul stresses that the counting happened before Abraham did anything; James stresses that the man so counted later climbed a mountain with his son. Neither would recognise a faith that never obeys, and neither would allow obedience to be the ground of acceptance.
  • Luther's "alone" is defensible as translation of sense. The Greek says "apart from works of the law", which is what "faith alone" means, and Paul's own argument in 4:4-5 makes it explicit. Luther was right about the doctrine even where the added word made him vulnerable.
The classical summary, from the Reformers, is exact: we are justified by faith alone, but the faith that justifies is never alone.

Monotheism as an argument for the Gentile mission

Or is God the God of Jews only? Is he not the God of Gentiles also? Yes, of Gentiles also, since God is one, who will justify the circumcised by faith and the uncircumcised through faith. Romans 3:29-30, ESV

This is one of Paul's most elegant moves, and it is easy to read past. The Jewish reader confesses daily that "the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deut 6:4). Paul takes that confession (the single most distinctive claim of Israel's faith) and draws from it a conclusion the reader had not drawn: if there is only one God, he is the God of everyone, and there cannot be two different ways of being right with him. A God of Jews by law and of Gentiles by faith would be two gods with two systems.

So the unity of the church is not a modern sentiment about inclusion. It is an implication of monotheism. Break the Roman congregation into a law-keeping tier and a faith tier and you have implicitly denied the Shema. That is a considerably heavier argument than "let us all get along", and it is why chapters 14-15 can treat table fellowship as a gospel issue rather than a matter of taste.

"We uphold the law"

Verse 31 sounds like a reversal, and Paul leaves it hanging: how does justification by faith establish the law? He gives no answer here, because the answer is chapter 4, and it comes in three parts, which you should watch for as you read.

  • The law's own witness. Genesis and the Psalms teach justification by faith; so reading Paul's way honours the Old Testament rather than discarding it (4:3, 6-8).
  • The law's own verdict. Only in the gospel is the law's demand actually satisfied rather than lowered: at the cross, and then by the Spirit in believers (8:3-4).
  • The law's proper use. Restored to its true function, the law stops being a ladder and becomes a mirror and then a guide (3:20; 13:8-10).

Application

  • About God He is one, and therefore there is one way to him for everyone. That is exclusive in a way modern ears dislike, and inclusive in a way ancient ears found scandalous.
  • About you Boasting has not disappeared from you; it has moved. Notice where yours goes this week (competence, family, theology, service) and practise redirecting it to the object of 5:11.
  • Do Begin memory verse 3 today (3:28). Then read James 2:14-26 in one sitting with 3:28 open beside it, and write one sentence explaining how both are true.

Lesson 0035 · Unit 4: Chapters 9-11

Romans 10:1-13: The Word Is Near You

~30 min · Passage: Romans 10:1-13 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 10:1-13 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: what Paul prays for and for whom, the diagnosis of verses 2-3, the claim of verse 4 and how you would translate the key word, the two righteousnesses contrasted, the Old Testament passage reapplied in verses 6-8, and the two elements of verses 9-10 with the promise of verse 13.

Recall first

Observation check

  • A prayer, not a verdict (v. 1). "My heart's desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved." Immediately after chapter 9's sovereignty, Paul prays. He sees no tension.
  • A generous diagnosis (v. 2). "They have a zeal for God" (he grants their sincerity) "but not according to knowledge."
  • The specific failure (v. 3). "Being ignorant of the righteousness of God, and seeking to establish their own, they did not submit to God's righteousness." Note "submit": refusing a gift is an act of insubordination, not merely a mistake.
  • "Christ is the end of the law for righteousness" (v. 4). Telos, which can mean termination, goal, or both.
  • Two righteousnesses quoted (vv. 5-8). Leviticus 18:5 for the law's way ("the person who does the commandments shall live by them"); Deuteronomy 30:12-14 for faith's way.
  • The Deuteronomy passage is reapplied boldly (vv. 6-8). "Who will ascend into heaven?" becomes "to bring Christ down", and "who will descend into the abyss?" becomes "to bring Christ up from the dead".
  • "The word is near you, in your mouth and in your heart" (v. 8). And Paul identifies it: "that is, the word of faith that we proclaim."
  • Mouth and heart, in that order (v. 9), then reversed (v. 10). Verse 9 follows the order of the Deuteronomy quotation; verse 10 puts the heart first, which is the logical order.
  • The two elements (v. 9). Confess "Jesus is Lord" with the mouth; believe in the heart that God raised him from the dead.
  • "No distinction between Jew and Greek" (v. 12). The third time in the letter (compare 3:22-23).
  • "Bestowing his riches on all who call on him" (v. 12). The verb pictures wealth being lavished, not rationed.
  • Joel 2:32 quoted (v. 13). "Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved." In Joel, "the LORD" is Yahweh; Paul applies it to Jesus, having just said "Jesus is Lord".

Interpretation

Zeal without knowledge

For, being ignorant of the righteousness of God, and seeking to establish their own, they did not submit to God's righteousness. Romans 10:3, ESV

Paul's diagnosis is precise and it is not unkind. He grants zeal: real, costly, sincere devotion to God. Then he identifies what is wrong with it: ignorance of the righteousness God gives, and an attempt to build a substitute. The result is a refusal to submit.

That word is the sharpest in the sentence. We usually think of unbelief as intellectual: the person is not persuaded. Paul treats the refusal of God's righteousness as insubordination, because accepting it requires admitting that you have nothing to offer. Establishing your own righteousness feels like devotion and functions as rebellion, since it insists on terms other than God's. This is why moral, religious, serious people can be further from the kingdom than the obviously lost: they have more to surrender.

Paul knew this from the inside. He had been the zealous man, "as to righteousness under the law, blameless" (Phil 3:6), and he counted it loss "in order that I may gain Christ and be found in him, not having a righteousness of my own that comes from the law, but that which comes through faith in Christ" (Phil 3:8-9). Romans 10:3 is autobiography before it is diagnosis, which is why verse 1 is a prayer and not a complaint.

Doctrine checkpoint: "Christ is the end of the law" (10:4)
The Greek telos can mean termination (the end of a road), goal (the point of the whole thing), or fulfilment. Which does Paul mean?
  • Termination. The law's era as a covenant administration has closed, and its function as a way of pursuing righteousness is finished, which fits verses 5-8 exactly, where the law's way and faith's way are set against each other.
  • Goal. The law always pointed forward to Christ; he is what it was aiming at. This fits 3:21 ("the Law and the Prophets bear witness to it") and Galatians 3:24.
  • Both, and the qualifier decides the emphasis. Note the full phrase: "the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes." Whatever else is meant, the law has ended as a route to righteous standing. Most careful commentators, MacArthur among them, take both senses as present with termination-for-righteousness in the foreground.
What it does not mean: that God's moral will has evaporated. Chapter 13 will quote the commandments as binding, and 8:4 said the law's righteous requirement is fulfilled in those who walk by the Spirit. The road to standing is closed; the description of what pleases God is not.

A word that has come near

But the righteousness based on faith says, "Do not say in your heart, 'Who will ascend into heaven?'" (that is, to bring Christ down) "or 'Who will descend into the abyss?'" (that is, to bring Christ up from the dead). Romans 10:6-7, ESV

Paul's use of Deuteronomy 30 startles some readers, but look at the original. Moses is telling Israel that the commandment he gives "is not too hard for you, neither is it far off. It is not in heaven, that you should say, 'Who will ascend to heaven for us and bring it to us?'… But the word is very near you." Moses' point is accessibility: God has already spoken; nobody needs to fetch the message from a distance.

Paul takes that principle and shows where it has landed. Nobody needs to ascend to heaven, because Christ has already come down. Nobody needs to descend to the abyss, because Christ has already been raised. Both journeys have been made, by God, in the opposite direction. What remains is a word near enough to be in your mouth and heart: the gospel, preached.

This is why the gospel is called good news rather than good advice. Advice tells you what journey to make; news reports a journey already completed. The two impossible errands of verses 6-7 are exactly what religion asks of people, and both have been cancelled by events.

Confess and believe

because, if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. Romans 10:9, ESV

Three things are worth noting in the clearest "how to be saved" verse in Scripture.

The order. Verse 9 says mouth then heart, following the order of the Deuteronomy quotation in verse 8; verse 10 immediately restates it as heart then mouth, which is the logical sequence. Paul is not making confession the cause of faith. He is making the point that real faith speaks.

The content. "Jesus is Lord": kyrios, the word the Greek Old Testament uses for Yahweh, and the confession the empire demanded for Caesar. To say it in first-century Rome was to make a claim about deity and to refuse one about the emperor. And "God raised him from the dead" is a historical claim: Christianity's minimum content includes an event that either happened or did not.

The scope. Verses 12-13 close the paragraph where the letter began: no distinction, the same Lord, riches for all who call. And Joel's "everyone who calls on the name of the LORD" (a promise about Yahweh) is applied without hesitation to Jesus. It is one of the quiet, decisive statements of Christ's deity in the New Testament, and it arrives as an argument for Gentile inclusion rather than as a formal doctrinal claim.

Application

  • About God He came near. Both errands that would have been required of us have been performed by him, in the opposite direction.
  • About you Sincerity is not the same as truth (v. 2), and effort is not the same as submission (v. 3). Where are you offering God zeal instead of surrender?
  • Do Begin memory verse 10 today (10:9-10). Then read Deuteronomy 30:11-14 with verses 6-8 open, and note exactly what Moses was claiming and what Paul does with it.

In your MacArthur Study Bible, read the notes on 10:3, 10:4 (end of the law), and 10:9-10 (confess and believe). Then read Philippians 3:4-11 alongside 10:2-3.
Church prompt
Ask someone how they would explain the gospel to a sincere adherent of another religion. Romans 10:2-3 gives you a way to honour their zeal and still name the problem, which is a rare combination.

Questions welcome, always: "does 10:9 mean saying the words saves?", "what does 'end of the law' mean?", "how can Paul use Deuteronomy 30 that way?" Ask your teacher; on the first, note that verse 10 puts heart-belief first and that the confession is of lordship, not a formula.

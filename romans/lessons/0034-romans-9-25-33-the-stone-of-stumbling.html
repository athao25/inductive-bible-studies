Lesson 0034 · Unit 4: Chapters 9-11

Romans 9:25-33: The Stone of Stumbling

~25 min · Passage: Romans 9:25-33 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 9:25-33 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the two prophets quoted and what each contributes, how the word "remnant" is used, the paradox summarised in verses 30-31, the reason given in verse 32 for Israel's failure, and the two Isaiah texts combined into the quotation of verse 33.

Recall first

Observation check

  • Hosea first (vv. 25-26). "Those who were not my people I will call 'my people'." In Hosea the words applied to unfaithful Israel; Paul applies them to Gentiles. He reads the principle rather than the original referent only.
  • Isaiah second (vv. 27-29). Two quotations, both about a remnant: "though the number of the sons of Israel be as the sand of the sea, only a remnant of them will be saved."
  • Two directions of surprise. Hosea says outsiders come in; Isaiah says insiders are reduced to a few. Together they explain what Paul's contemporaries were seeing.
  • "If the Lord of hosts had not left us offspring" (v. 29). Isaiah 1:9. Without a preserved remnant, Israel would have been Sodom: a reminder that even the remnant is a mercy, not an achievement.
  • The paradox stated (vv. 30-31). Gentiles "who did not pursue righteousness have attained it"; Israel "who pursued a law that would lead to righteousness did not succeed in reaching that law."
  • The verb is athletic. "Pursue" is the runner's word. Israel ran hard and finished nowhere; the Gentiles were not even in the race and crossed the line.
  • The reason (v. 32). "Because they did not pursue it by faith, but as if it were based on works." The failure was not laziness but method.
  • "They have stumbled over the stumbling stone" (v. 32). A person, not a principle.
  • Two Isaiah texts fused (v. 33). Isaiah 28:16 (a precious cornerstone in Zion; whoever believes will not be put to shame) and Isaiah 8:14 (a stone of stumbling and a rock of offence). The same stone does both.
  • The chapter ends with faith (v. 33). "Whoever believes in him will not be put to shame." After all the sovereignty, the closing word is belief.

Interpretation

Both halves of the surprise

What shall we say, then? That Gentiles who did not pursue righteousness have attained it, that is, a righteousness that is by faith; but that Israel who pursued a law that would lead to righteousness did not succeed in reaching that law. Romans 9:30-31, ESV

This is one of the most concise and most devastating summaries in Scripture. Two groups, two trajectories, and both outcomes are the opposite of what anyone would predict. Gentiles were not looking, and found. Israel ran with everything they had, and did not arrive.

Verse 32 gives the reason, and it is worth stating carefully: the problem was not that Israel pursued righteousness. Pursuing righteousness is good. The problem was how: "not by faith, but as if it were based on works." They took a road that does not reach the destination, and effort on the wrong road never becomes progress. 10:2 will say it kindly and devastatingly: "they have a zeal for God, but not according to knowledge."

Notice how this paragraph balances the previous one. Chapter 9 has just insisted on God's sovereign mercy; now it insists with equal force on human responsibility. Israel is not excused because of hardening, and the reason for their exclusion is stated in terms of what they did: they sought righteousness by works and stumbled over Christ. Romans holds both without apology and without a formula for reconciling them. Any reading of chapter 9 that makes verses 30-33 unnecessary has gone wrong, and so has any reading of verses 30-33 that makes verses 14-24 embarrassing.

Doctrine checkpoint: how Paul uses the Old Testament here
Hosea's "not my people" originally described unfaithful northern Israel, whom God would restore. Paul applies it to Gentiles. Is that legitimate?
  • He is reading the principle, not swapping the referent. The pattern in Hosea is that God creates a people out of those who were not a people. If God does that for apostate Israel, he can do it for Gentiles; the underlying divine action is the same.
  • Peter does the same thing. 1 Peter 2:10 applies the identical Hosea text to a largely Gentile readership, which suggests a shared apostolic understanding rather than a private manoeuvre by Paul.
  • Isaiah's remnant works the same way. Written about the Assyrian crisis, it describes a permanent pattern of God's dealing: judgment falls, a remnant is preserved by grace, and the promise continues through them. Paul applies the pattern to his own day, and chapter 11 will apply it again to Elijah's.
  • The controlling conviction: the Old Testament records how God acts, and God does not change. This is not allegory or a loose proof-text; it is reading the Scriptures as the record of a consistent character.

One stone, two effects

as it is written, "Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone of stumbling, and a rock of offense; and whoever believes in him will not be put to shame." Romans 9:33, ESV

Paul fuses two Isaiah passages into one sentence, and the fusion is the point. Isaiah 28:16 promises a tested cornerstone laid in Zion, a sure foundation for whoever believes. Isaiah 8:14 warns that the LORD himself will become "a stone of offence and a rock of stumbling to both houses of Israel". The same stone is foundation and obstacle. What it is to you depends on what you do when you meet it.

And notice that the stone is a person: "whoever believes in him". The obstacle over which Israel stumbled was not a doctrine of justification but Jesus Christ himself: crucified, and therefore under the law's curse in the eyes of anyone reading Deuteronomy 21:23 without the gospel. Paul says elsewhere that Christ crucified is "a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Cor 1:23). Nothing has changed. The offence of Christianity has never chiefly been its ethics; it is the cross, and the fact that it leaves nothing for us to contribute.

Which is exactly why a works-based pursuit stumbles over him. If you are building your own righteousness, a Saviour who insists on supplying yours is not good news but an insult. The stone is only precious to people who have given up carrying their own stones. That is the sense in which the two halves of Romans 9 belong together: sovereign mercy explains why anyone believes, and the stumbling stone explains why those who do not are without excuse.

Application

  • About God He calls people who were not his people and preserves a remnant when everything looks lost. Both are mercies, and neither is owed.
  • About you Israel's error was method, not effort. Where are you working hard at something God never asked for, and calling the exhaustion faithfulness?
  • Do Read Isaiah 8:11-15, Isaiah 28:14-16, and Hosea 1:10-2:1 today. Then ask honestly which of the two effects of the stone describes your instinct when you meet the claim that you contribute nothing.

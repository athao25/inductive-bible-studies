Lesson 0025 · Unit 3: Chapters 6-8

Romans 7:14-25: Wretched Man That I Am

~30 min · Passage: Romans 7:14-25 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 7:14-25 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the change of tense from the previous paragraph, how often "I" appears, the repeated pattern of wanting and not doing, what Paul says dwells in him, the two "laws" at war in verses 22-23, the cry of verse 24, and the two halves of verse 25.

Recall first

Observation check

  • The tenses turn present (v. 14). Everything in 7:7-13 was past; from here it is "I am", "I do", "I find". The shift is the single most important observation in the paragraph.
  • "The law is spiritual, but I am of the flesh" (v. 14). The contrast is not law versus grace but law versus me. The problem has been relocated from the command to the person.
  • "Sold under sin" (v. 14). Slave-market language, and startling, since 6:18 said "having been set free from sin, you have become slaves of righteousness."
  • The pattern repeats three times (vv. 15-20). I do not do what I want; I do the very thing I hate. Stated, restated, and concluded twice with "it is no longer I who do it, but sin that dwells within me."
  • "Nothing good dwells in me, that is, in my flesh" (v. 18). Note the correction Paul inserts. He does not say nothing good dwells in him full stop; he specifies the flesh.
  • "I delight in the law of God, in my inner being" (v. 22). A remarkable claim. Compare 8:7, where the mind set on the flesh "does not submit to God's law; indeed, it cannot."
  • Four uses of "law" in verses 21-23. Principle, God's law, the law of my mind, the law of sin. Paul is using the word in different senses within three verses.
  • War imagery (v. 23). "Waging war against the law of my mind and making me captive."
  • The cry (v. 24). "Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death?" A question asking for a person, not a technique.
  • The answer comes instantly (v. 25a). "Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!" Not "here is what to do" but "here is who".
  • Then a summary that returns to the tension (v. 25b). "So then, I myself serve the law of God with my mind, but with my flesh I serve the law of sin." The chapter ends unresolved, and 8:1 supplies the resolution.

Interpretation

Doctrine checkpoint: who is speaking in 7:14-25?
This is the most argued paragraph in Romans, and honest readers land in different places. Take the three main readings seriously before choosing.
  • A believer, in normal Christian experience. Evidence: the present tenses; "I delight in the law of God in my inner being" (v. 22), which 8:7 says the unregenerate cannot do; "I want to do right" (v. 21); and the fact that only a Christian is grieved this way by sin. Weakness: "sold under sin" (v. 14) and "captive to the law of sin" (v. 23) sit awkwardly against 6:17-18 and 8:2.
  • An unbeliever, or Paul before conversion, described dramatically in the present tense. Evidence: the fleshly, sold, captive vocabulary; the total absence of the Spirit from the paragraph, against twenty mentions in chapter 8. Weakness: the delight in God's law and the will to good are hard to attribute to the mind that "cannot" submit (8:7).
  • A person under the law and outside of Christ's power: often read as Israel's experience, or as anyone trying to be holy by law-keeping. Evidence: chapter 7 is about the law throughout; the paragraph shows precisely what a good law does to a person without the Spirit; and the absence of the Spirit is then not an oversight but the point. Weakness: the "I" still reads personally.
Where this course lands. The paragraph describes the Christian who is attempting sanctification by law rather than by the Spirit, which is why the Spirit is missing from it and floods the next chapter. That reading honours the present tense, explains the delight in God's law, and keeps the contrast Paul is actually drawing (7:6 announced "the new way of the Spirit and not the old way of the written code", and chapter 7 shows the old way from the inside). MacArthur reads it as normal Christian experience; many Reformed writers read it as the believer under law; others take the pre-conversion view. On one point all agree, and it is the point that matters: no one, believer or not, is delivered from the power of sin by the law. The cry of verse 24 is answered by a person, and the mechanism arrives in chapter 8.

The anatomy of a divided man

For I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate. Romans 7:15, ESV

Whatever your view of the speaker, notice what the paragraph is not saying. It is not describing a man who loves sin and cannot get enough of it. It describes someone who wants what is good, agrees that the law is good, delights in it inwardly, and finds himself doing the opposite anyway. This is a person defeated in the act of trying.

That distinction is pastorally decisive. A person untroubled by their sin is not in Romans 7; they are in Romans 1. The wretchedness of verse 24 is itself evidence of something at work, because dead men do not groan. But the paragraph is equally clear that the groaning is not the cure. Effort, sincerity, delight in the law, and hatred of one's own failure are all present here, and together they achieve nothing. What is absent is the Spirit, and that absence is the paragraph's loudest silence.

Verse 18 rewards close reading: "I know that nothing good dwells in me, that is, in my flesh." Paul begins a sweeping statement and then narrows it with a corrective clause. He is not denying that God has done anything in him; he is locating the theatre of the problem. Flesh here does not mean the body as such but fallen human nature operating apart from the Spirit's power. The body is not the enemy: 6:13 tells you to present its members to God, and 12:1 will ask for the whole of it.

The question, and why it asks for a "who"

Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death? Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord! Romans 7:24-25, ESV

Read the question carefully: not "what will deliver me" but "who". Everything in the chapter to this point has been an attempt to solve the problem with resources: knowledge of the law, agreement with it, desire, effort, self-examination. The one thing not yet tried is another person. And the answer arrives before the sentence has cooled: "Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord."

"This body of death" may be an allusion to a punishment reported in antiquity, in which a corpse was strapped to a living criminal until the decay killed him. Whether or not Paul had that in mind, the image is exact: something dead attached to you that you cannot detach by any effort of your own.

And then verse 25b, which surprises everyone: after the thanksgiving, Paul restates the tension rather than resolving it. "So then, I myself serve the law of God with my mind, but with my flesh I serve the law of sin." That is deliberate. The deliverance is real and it is announced, but the full explanation of how (the Spirit of life, the condemnation of sin in the flesh, the righteous requirement fulfilled in us) is one verse away. Chapter 7 ends with a man; chapter 8 begins with a verdict. Do not stop reading tonight at verse 25.

Application

  • About God He does not answer the cry of verse 24 with a programme. He answers it with his Son, and then with his Spirit.
  • About you If your Christian life feels like this paragraph, notice what is missing from it. Chapter 7 is full of "I" (about forty times) and empty of the Spirit. That ratio is diagnostic.
  • Do Read Romans 8:1-4 tonight even though it is tomorrow's lesson. Chapter 7 was never meant to be left standing on its own overnight.

Church prompt
Ask two mature believers who they think is speaking in Romans 7:14-25, and why. You will likely get two different answers with good reasons, which is a useful lesson in itself: some texts are genuinely hard, and charity between readers is part of exegesis.

Questions welcome, always: "if this is a Christian, why 'sold under sin'?", "if it is not, how can he delight in God's law?", "why does the chapter end back in the tension?" Ask your teacher; the third has a simple answer, because 8:1 is the next sentence.

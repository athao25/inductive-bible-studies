Lesson 0021 · Unit 3: Chapters 6-8

Romans 6:1-14: Dead to Sin

~30 min · Passage: Romans 6:1-14 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 6:1-14 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the question that opens the chapter and which earlier verse provokes it, how often "died", "death", "buried" and "raised" appear, the logic of the "if… then" statements in verses 5-8, the three commands in verses 11-13, and the promise attached to verse 14.

Recall first

Observation check

  • The objection comes from 5:20 (v. 1). "Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound?" If grace super-abounds where sin increases, the arithmetic seems to favour more sin. Paul raises it himself.
  • "By no means!" (v. 2). And then, crucially, he answers with a fact rather than a rule: "How can we who died to sin still live in it?"
  • "Do you not know?" (v. 3). Paul assumes this is basic Christian instruction, not advanced teaching. The Romans should already have known it.
  • Baptism language throughout (vv. 3-4). Baptized into Christ Jesus, baptized into his death, buried with him by baptism into death.
  • The purpose (v. 4). "In order that, just as Christ was raised… we too might walk in newness of life." The resurrection half is aimed at daily conduct.
  • "United with him" (v. 5). The word suggests being grown together, like a graft. Not imitation but organic union.
  • "Our old self was crucified with him" (v. 6). Past tense, accomplished. The purpose: "that the body of sin might be brought to nothing, so that we would no longer be enslaved to sin."
  • "One who has died has been set free from sin" (v. 7). Literally "has been justified from sin": a legal image: death discharges the claim.
  • Christ's death and life described (vv. 9-10). "Death no longer has dominion over him… the life he lives he lives to God." What is true of him becomes the template for us.
  • The first imperative in the letter's ethics (v. 11). "Consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God." Consider is the bookkeeping word again: logizomai, reckon. Count as true what is true.
  • Two more imperatives (vv. 12-13). "Let not sin reign"; "present yourselves to God". Note "present", the same verb as 12:1.
  • The promise, not the threat (v. 14). "For sin will have no dominion over you, since you are not under law but under grace." The reason obedience is possible is grace, not law.

Interpretation

The answer is a fact, not an exhortation

What shall we say then? Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound? By no means! How can we who died to sin still live in it? Romans 6:1-2, ESV

This is the paragraph the letter has owed us since 3:8, and the shape of Paul's answer is as important as its content. He does not say "you must not" or "God would be displeased" or "think of the consequences". He says something has happened to you that makes the proposal incoherent. The objection assumes the Christian is the same person as before, now with a more generous forgiveness policy. Paul's reply is that the person has died.

Which death? Not a mystical experience and not a resolution you made. Paul points to union with Christ: because you are in him, his death counts as yours, exactly as Adam's sin counted as yours in 5:12-21. The federal logic of the last chapter is now applied. In Adam you sinned and died; in Christ you died and rose. Those are not metaphors for how you feel; they are statements about which humanity you belong to.

Note carefully what "died to sin" does not mean. It does not mean sin is no longer attractive: verse 12 assumes it will make demands, and verse 13 assumes you can still hand your body over to it. It means sin's legal claim and ruling authority are broken. A slave who dies is no longer subject to the master; the master may still shout, but the relationship of ownership is finished.

Doctrine checkpoint: what is baptism doing here?
Verses 3-4 speak of being "baptized into Christ Jesus" and "buried with him by baptism into death". Careful believers read the reference differently, and it is worth knowing the options.
  • Spirit baptism. Paul is describing the Spirit's act of placing the believer into Christ (1 Cor 12:13), with water baptism as its outward sign. On this reading the union is the substance and the rite the picture.
  • Water baptism as the shorthand for conversion. In the New Testament, conversion, faith, confession and baptism form a single package, so naming one names the whole. Paul appeals to their baptism as the moment they publicly enacted the reality.
  • What both agree on. The passage teaches union with Christ in death and resurrection, not that water produces the union. The argument requires that everyone Paul addresses died with Christ, and he draws the conclusion for all his readers without qualification.
Note in passing how well the imagery fits immersion (down into the water as burial, up out of it as resurrection) and how little the argument depends on that observation. Paul's point stands whatever your church's practice: what you enacted then is a fact you now reckon on.

Reckon, then refuse, then present

So you also must consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus. Romans 6:11, ESV

Verse 11 is the first command in the letter's ethical teaching, and it is not "try harder". It is "count it true". The verb is the accounting word from chapter 4: the same one used of righteousness credited to Abraham. Reckoning does not create the fact; it aligns you with it. You do not make yourself dead to sin by considering it, any more than a bank clerk creates a balance by reading it. But a man who does not know what is in his account will live as though he were poor.

Then two commands follow from it. "Let not sin reign in your mortal body" (v. 12): a defensive move, refusing the old ruler's orders. And "present yourselves to God as those who have been brought from death to life" (v. 13): an offensive move, handing over your members as weapons or instruments to the new one. Note that both assume real agency and real struggle. Paul is not describing a passive experience in which sin evaporates; he is describing a war fought on the basis of a victory already won.

The order is everything. Indicative, then imperative: because you died and rose, therefore reckon, refuse, and present. Reverse them and you have moralism: the attempt to become dead to sin by behaving as though you were. Every Christian ethic that produces exhaustion has reversed Romans 6.

"Not under law but under grace"

Verse 14 says something startling: "sin will have no dominion over you, since you are not under law but under grace." Most religious instinct says the opposite, that law restrains and grace loosens. Paul says law is precisely what cannot break sin's dominion (that was 3:20 and 5:20), and grace is what can.

Why? Because law tells you what to do without changing who you are, and it aggravates the problem by turning sin into transgression. Grace, by contrast, has already killed the old self and given a new life to live from. A rule imposed on an unchanged heart provokes; a new life produces. Chapter 7 will make the mechanism painfully clear, and chapter 8 will name the power. For now, take verse 14 as a promise rather than a demand: dominion is broken, and the tense is future-certain.

Application

  • About God He did not answer the problem of ongoing sin with tighter rules. He answered it by putting us into his Son's death and resurrection.
  • About you Which of the three commands do you neglect? Most of us attempt verse 12 (refuse) without verse 11 (reckon), which is why it feels like white-knuckling. Start where Paul starts.
  • Do Name one specific sin you have treated as inevitable. Write out verse 11 with that sin in the sentence, then verse 13's "present" as a concrete action for tomorrow morning.

Church prompt
Ask someone what they think "died to sin" means. Common answers include "sin no longer tempts me", which the text contradicts in verse 12. The right answer is about dominion, not attraction.

Questions welcome, always: "is 6:3 about water baptism?", "if my old self was crucified, why do I still sin?", "how can grace restrain sin better than law?" Ask your teacher; the last one is the whole of chapters 7-8 in a question.

Lesson 0044 · Unit 5: Chapters 12-16

Romans 13:1-7: The Governing Authorities

~30 min · Passage: Romans 13:1-7 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 13:1-7 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the command and the reason given for it, how the authorities are described three times, the two functions of the one who bears the sword, the two motives for submission named in verse 5, and the list of what is owed in verse 7. Then find out who was emperor in AD 57.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "Let every person be subject" (v. 1). The command is universal in scope. And the word is subject (take your appointed place under) rather than obey without qualification.
  • The reason, stated twice (v. 1). "There is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God." Authority is derivative, which is the key to the whole passage.
  • Resistance described (v. 2). "Whoever resists the authorities resists what God has appointed." The seriousness follows from the derivation.
  • "Rulers are not a terror to good conduct, but to bad" (v. 3). Paul states the general norm of government, not a claim that every ruler behaves.
  • "God's servant": twice (vv. 4, 6). The magistrate is diakonos and then leitourgos, both words otherwise used of religious service. A pagan official is called God's minister.
  • "He does not bear the sword in vain" (v. 4). Lethal force acknowledged, and given a purpose.
  • "An avenger who carries out God's wrath" (v. 4). The same root as 12:19, where personal vengeance was forbidden. Vengeance is God's, and this is one channel of it.
  • Two motives (v. 5). "Not only to avoid God's wrath but also for the sake of conscience." Fear of penalty and inward conviction.
  • Four obligations (v. 7). Taxes, revenue, respect, honour. Two financial, two attitudinal.
  • The historical setting matters. Written around AD 57, under Nero: early in his reign, but the same emperor who would burn Christians within a decade. Paul himself would be executed by Roman authority.
  • Where the paragraph sits. Between "overcome evil with good" (12:21) and "owe no one anything except to love" (13:8). The frame is love, not politics.

Interpretation

Derivative authority

For there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God. Romans 13:1, ESV

The foundation of the whole paragraph is that no human authority is original. Every one of them holds power on loan. That single fact cuts in two directions at once, and reading only one direction produces the two standard errors about this passage.

It grounds real submission: the police officer, the tax office, and the magistrate are not merely convenient social arrangements but instruments God has appointed, so defying them casually is defying him. Government is God's ordinance, not a necessary evil.

And it grounds real limits: a derivative authority cannot outrank its source. If the state commands what God forbids, the same principle that requires submission requires refusal. Scripture is consistent on this: the Hebrew midwives disobeyed Pharaoh (Ex 1:17), Daniel's friends refused the statue (Dan 3:16-18), Daniel kept praying against the decree (Dan 6:10), and the apostles told the Sanhedrin, "we must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). None of them was rebelling against God's ordinance; each was honouring it above its delegate.

It also matters that Paul was writing under Nero, and that Nero would eventually kill him. Whatever this passage means, it cannot mean that Christians must approve of their rulers or that unjust regimes forfeit all claim to order. Paul is not describing a Christian state; he is telling believers in a pagan empire how to live as good citizens under a government they did not choose and would not have chosen.

Doctrine checkpoint: the limits of submission
This is the live checkpoint of the chapter, and Christians who agree on everything else disagree here. Four points that most careful readers accept:
  • Submission is the norm, not the exception. The default posture is subjection, taxes paid, honour given. Any framework that turns civil disobedience into an ordinary tool of Christian living has inverted the passage.
  • Obedience stops where sin begins. When a government commands what God forbids or forbids what God commands, refusal is obedience to the higher authority. Acts 5:29 is the apostolic precedent, spoken to a lawful court.
  • Refusal in Scripture is accompanied by willingness to bear the penalty. Daniel went to the lions; the apostles took the flogging; Paul appealed to Roman law and then submitted to Roman custody. Biblical civil disobedience is open and takes the consequences, which distinguishes it from evasion.
  • Beyond that, believers differ. Whether unjust taxation, tyrannical regimes, or gross injustice against others may be actively resisted (and by whom) is disputed. The Reformed tradition developed the idea of "lesser magistrates" resisting greater ones; others read 13:1-7 more absolutely. This course notes the difference rather than settling it, and observes that the passage's own emphasis falls on submission, since that was what the Roman Christians needed to hear.
One practical note: verse 7 says "pay to all what is owed to them… respect to whom respect is owed, honour to whom honour is owed." Contempt for rulers is not a Christian liberty, however poor the ruler. That is a harder command than it looks in almost any political climate.

The sword, and the two vengeances

for he is the servant of God, an avenger who carries out God's wrath on the wrongdoer. Romans 13:4, ESV

Read this verse immediately after 12:19, since the two are meant to be held together, and the same word family appears in both. "Never avenge yourselves… for it is written, 'Vengeance is mine'" (12:19). Then: the magistrate is "an avenger who carries out God's wrath". So vengeance is God's, and one of the channels through which he exercises it in the present age is lawful public justice.

That resolves what looks like a contradiction. The Christian is forbidden private retaliation and is not thereby committed to indifference about wrongdoing. Reporting a crime is not revenge; it is referring the matter to the institution God appointed for it. And the passage's language about the sword (lethal force, borne "not in vain") is why the Christian tradition has generally held that the state may lawfully use coercion, including in the punishment of crime and the defence of the innocent, in a way that individuals may not.

Finally, notice the frame. This paragraph sits between "overcome evil with good" and "owe no one anything except to love each other". Paul does not treat the state as a separate sphere with its own morality; he treats good citizenship as one department of loving your neighbour. Taxes appear in Romans as an application of love, which is an unusual and clarifying way to think about a tax return.

Application

  • About God He governs through structures, including ones that do not know him. Nero was carrying delegated authority he neither recognised nor deserved.
  • About you Verse 7 names respect and honour as debts. Test your speech about political leaders you dislike against that word, "owed".
  • Do Pray for your actual rulers by name today, as 1 Timothy 2:1-2 commands. If that feels strange, note how much easier it is to complain about them than to intercede for them, and let the difficulty be instructive.

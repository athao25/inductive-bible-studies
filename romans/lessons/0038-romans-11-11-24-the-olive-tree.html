Lesson 0038 · Unit 4: Chapters 9-11

Romans 11:11-24: The Olive Tree

~30 min · Passage: Romans 11:11-24 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 11:11-24 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the purpose given for Israel's stumbling, the two "how much more" arguments, what Paul says about magnifying his own ministry and why, the parts of the olive tree and who is which, the warning issued to the grafted branches, and the reason for hope given in verses 23-24.

Recall first

Observation check

  • The question and its answer (v. 11). "Did they stumble in order that they might fall? By no means!" Stumbling is not the same as final ruin.
  • A purpose in the stumbling (v. 11). "Through their trespass salvation has come to the Gentiles, so as to make Israel jealous." Picking up Deuteronomy 32:21 from 10:19.
  • The first "how much more" (v. 12). If their failure means riches for the world, "how much more will their full inclusion mean!"
  • Paul's strategy stated openly (vv. 13-14). "I magnify my ministry in order somehow to make my fellow Jews jealous, and thus save some of them." His Gentile mission has a Jewish aim as well.
  • The second "how much more" (v. 15). If their rejection is the reconciliation of the world, their acceptance will be "life from the dead".
  • Two images of holiness (v. 16). Firstfruits and lump; root and branches. Both say the same thing: what is consecrated first sanctifies what follows.
  • The olive tree (vv. 17-24). Natural branches broken off, a wild olive shoot grafted in, all supported by a root.
  • "You do not support the root, but the root supports you" (v. 18). The stated purpose of the whole image: to prevent Gentile arrogance.
  • Faith and unbelief are the mechanism (v. 20). "They were broken off because of their unbelief, but you stand fast through faith."
  • "So do not become proud, but fear" (v. 20). A command, not a suggestion.
  • Kindness and severity (v. 22). "Note then the kindness and the severity of God." Both are his, and the Gentile reader is told to look at both.
  • Grafting against nature (v. 24). Paul notes his own image is horticulturally backwards (nobody grafts wild shoots into a cultivated tree) and says the natural branches will be even easier to graft back.

Interpretation

A stumble that is not a fall

So I ask, did they stumble in order that they might fall? By no means! Rather, through their trespass salvation has come to the Gentiles, so as to make Israel jealous. Romans 11:11, ESV

Paul is drawing a distinction the previous paragraph might have obscured. Israel stumbled over the stone (9:32-33) and much of the nation is hardened (11:7). Does that mean they are finished? No, and the reason is that God has been running a strategy through the stumbling rather than merely permitting a disaster.

The strategy is jealousy, and it is drawn from Deuteronomy 32:21, quoted at 10:19: God provokes an unfaithful people by blessing those who were "not a nation". So Gentile salvation is not merely a consolation after Israel's failure; it is designed in part to provoke Israel to want what they see. Paul says frankly that this shapes his own work: he magnifies his Gentile ministry partly in order to save "some of my fellow Jews" (vv. 13-14). That is a startling piece of missionary strategy, and it tells you something about how God writes history, even the detours are load-bearing.

Then the two "how much more" arguments. If Israel's failure enriched the world, their fullness will do more; if their rejection meant reconciliation for the world, their acceptance will be "life from the dead". Paul is arguing from the lesser to the greater again, and the direction of the argument is unmistakably hopeful.

The tree, and the warning it exists to deliver

do not be arrogant toward the branches. If you are, remember it is not you who support the root, but the root that supports you. Romans 11:18, ESV

Read the image carefully. There is one tree, not two. The root is the patriarchal promise: Abraham and the covenant God made with him. The natural branches are Israelites. Some were broken off through unbelief. The wild shoot is the Gentile believer, grafted in "contrary to nature" (v. 24), and now sharing "the nourishing root of the olive tree" (v. 17).

Notice what the picture rules out. It rules out the idea that God started a new tree: Gentile Christians are grafted into Israel's tree, drawing life from a Jewish root, sharing promises made to Abraham. And it rules out any sense of superiority in the grafted branch, which is precisely why Paul draws the picture. This is the passage where the doctrine of chapters 9-11 finally lands on the Roman congregation's actual problem: Gentile believers looking down on Jewish ones.

Verse 20 supplies the mechanism and the response in one line: "They were broken off because of their unbelief, but you stand fast through faith. So do not become proud, but fear." Faith is not an achievement to boast in; by definition it is the posture of receiving. A branch that boasts about being grafted has misunderstood grafting.

Doctrine checkpoint: does 11:21-22 threaten a believer's security?
"For if God did not spare the natural branches, neither will he spare you… provided you continue in his kindness. Otherwise you too will be cut off." Set against 8:38-39, this looks like a contradiction. Four observations resolve it.
  • The address is corporate. Paul is speaking to "you Gentiles" (v. 13) as a group about their place in redemptive history, not to an individual about the security of their soul. Branches here are categories of people as well as persons.
  • Warnings are one of the means God uses to keep his people. Romans has warnings (8:13) and unbreakable promises (8:30) in the same letter, from the same pen, a chapter apart. A warning heeded is part of how perseverance happens; the chain of 8:29-30 does not run around our attention but through it.
  • Those cut off are cut off for unbelief (v. 20), which is exactly what the elect are kept from. The warning describes a real danger for anyone whose connection to the tree is nominal.
  • The tone is the point. Paul's aim is not to make believers anxious but to make them humble: "do not become proud, but fear." Fear here is reverent seriousness, the opposite of complacency, not the dread that 8:15 abolished.
Read verse 22 as a whole: "note then the kindness and the severity of God". A Christianity that has only kindness produces presumption; one that has only severity produces despair. Paul insists you look at both, and both are aimed at humility.

"Contrary to nature", and how much easier the other way

For if you were cut from what is by nature a wild olive tree, and grafted, contrary to nature, into a cultivated olive tree, how much more will these, the natural branches, be grafted back into their own olive tree. Romans 11:24, ESV

Paul knows his horticulture is upside down. Ancient growers grafted cultivated shoots into hardy wild stock, not the reverse; grafting a wild shoot into a cultivated tree would be pointless agriculture. He does it deliberately and flags it ("contrary to nature") because that is exactly what Gentile inclusion is. It is not the normal way things work; it is a miracle of grace.

And then the argument: if God has already done the unnatural thing, the natural thing is hardly a difficulty. Reattaching branches to their own tree is easier than grafting in foreign ones. Verse 23 gives the only condition: "if they do not continue in their unbelief", and adds the assurance, "for God has the power to graft them in again." The hardening of 11:7 is not a sentence beyond reversal, because the God who does it also has the power to undo it.

That leaves the chapter poised for its climax. Israel has stumbled but not fallen; the stumbling has a purpose; the purpose ends in mercy; and God retains the power to restore. Tomorrow Paul names the mystery and then stops arguing altogether and worships.

Application

  • About God He works through detours. What looked like the failure of his plan turned out to be a stage in it, aimed at mercy for more people, not fewer.
  • About you Test yourself with verse 18. Is there any group whose unbelief you regard with satisfaction rather than grief? That is the arrogance this passage exists to kill.
  • Do Write down one way you could actively bless or serve Jewish people or another group you have quietly written off. Then pray verse 14's prayer: that something in your life might make somebody want what you have.

Church prompt
Ask someone what the olive tree image teaches about the relationship between the church and Israel. The one-tree structure surprises most people, and it is the strongest argument in Scripture against Christian anti-Semitism.

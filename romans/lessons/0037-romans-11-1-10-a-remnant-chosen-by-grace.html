Lesson 0037 · Unit 4: Chapters 9-11

Romans 11:1-10: A Remnant Chosen by Grace

~25 min · Passage: Romans 11:1-10 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 11:1-10 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the question that opens the chapter and the two answers Paul gives, what Elijah said and what God answered, how "remnant" is defined in verse 5, the exclusive statement of verse 6, and the two quotations about hardening that close the paragraph.

Recall first

Observation check

  • The question (v. 1). "Has God rejected his people?" It follows directly from 10:21's disobedient and contrary people.
  • Two answers. First "By no means!", and then a piece of evidence: "For I myself am an Israelite, a descendant of Abraham, a member of the tribe of Benjamin."
  • Paul is exhibit A. If God had rejected Israel, the apostle to the Gentiles would not exist.
  • "Whom he foreknew" (v. 2). The same verb as 8:29, and used here of a people God set his love on beforehand.
  • Elijah's complaint (vv. 2-3). From 1 Kings 19:10, 14: "they have killed your prophets, they have demolished your altars, and I alone am left."
  • God's reply (v. 4). "I have kept for myself seven thousand men who have not bowed the knee to Baal." Note "I have kept": God's preserving, not their persevering.
  • The parallel drawn (v. 5). "So too at the present time there is a remnant, chosen by grace." Elijah's situation is a pattern, not a one-off.
  • The exclusive principle (v. 6). "But if it is by grace, it is no longer on the basis of works; otherwise grace would no longer be grace." Grace and works are treated as mutually exclusive categories, as in 4:4-5.
  • Three groups (v. 7). Israel as a whole failed to obtain; "the elect obtained it"; "the rest were hardened."
  • Two quotations on hardening (vv. 8-10). Deuteronomy 29:4 with Isaiah 29:10 ("a spirit of stupor, eyes that would not see"), and Psalm 69:22-23 ("let their table become a snare").
  • "Down to this very day" (v. 8). Paul reads the hardening as a long-running condition, not a sudden event.

Interpretation

The evidence is a man

I ask, then, has God rejected his people? By no means! For I myself am an Israelite, a descendant of Abraham, a member of the tribe of Benjamin. Romans 11:1, ESV

Paul's first argument is autobiographical, and it is stronger than it looks. If God had written Israel off, then the fiercest opponent the church ever had (an Israelite of the tribe of Benjamin, a Pharisee) would not be writing this letter. His own existence proves that Jewish people are still being saved, and that God's mercy toward Israel has not been withdrawn.

Then "whom he foreknew" (v. 2), the same word as 8:29. As there, it does not mean God saw in advance what Israel would do; it means he set his love on them beforehand. And the argument runs the same way as chapter 8's: a love that originated in God's choice cannot be cancelled by the failures of those loved. What secured the individual believer in 8:38-39 secures the people here.

Elijah, and the danger of counting

But what is God's reply to him? "I have kept for myself seven thousand men who have not bowed the knee to Baal." Romans 11:4, ESV

The Elijah story is chosen with care. Standing at Horeb after Carmel, Elijah tells God that the covenant is finished and he alone is left. He is a serious man, he has just seen fire fall from heaven, and he is completely wrong about the numbers. God's answer corrects him: there are seven thousand.

Two lessons follow, and Paul intends both. First, the visible situation is not the whole situation. Elijah could see the altars torn down and could not see the seven thousand; a prophet's despair was based on incomplete data. Anyone who concludes from the state of the church, or a nation, or a family that God has finished is standing where Elijah stood.

Second, and more importantly, notice the grammar of God's answer: "I have kept for myself." The seven thousand did not hold out by strength of character; they were kept. That is why verse 5 can call the present remnant "chosen by grace": the remnant is not the group who managed to remain faithful but the group God preserved. This is the same doctrine as the golden chain, applied to a nation's history.

Doctrine checkpoint: grace and works cannot be mixed (11:6)
"But if it is by grace, it is no longer on the basis of works; otherwise grace would no longer be grace." This is the sharpest statement of the principle in the New Testament, and it functions as a rule for reading everything else.
  • The categories are mutually exclusive by definition, not by decree. Paul does not say God has decided not to mix them. He says mixing them destroys the meaning of the word. A wage that is partly a gift is not a gift; it is a discounted wage.
  • It rules out every "faith plus" scheme. Faith plus circumcision, faith plus sacraments, faith plus sufficient perseverance as a ground of acceptance. Any addition converts the whole into a works arrangement.
  • It does not rule out works as fruit. Romans has just spent chapters 6-8 on the obedience that grace produces. The question is always what your standing rests on, not whether obedience follows.
  • Apply it to the remnant. The seven thousand are not a meritorious minority. They are the demonstration that when anyone stands, God kept them.

Elect, hardened, and the shape of Israel's present

What then? Israel failed to obtain what it was seeking. The elect obtained it, but the rest were hardened. Romans 11:7, ESV

Verse 7 gives the anatomy of the situation in one sentence. Israel as a whole did not obtain; a subset ("the elect") did; the remainder were hardened. Note the passive voice on "hardened", and remember the mechanism from 9:18 and 1:24-28: God hardens by withdrawing restraint from people already set on their own way. The quotations that follow reinforce it. Deuteronomy 29:4 says God had not given them a heart to understand "to this day", spoken to a generation that had seen the exodus. Isaiah 29:10 speaks of a spirit of stupor. Psalm 69 asks that the table (the very blessings) become a snare, which is a bleak thought: gifts turning into traps when they are received without their giver.

But do not read verse 7 as the end of the story, because Paul does not. The whole chapter is structured to prevent that. Verse 11 will ask, "did they stumble in order that they might fall? By no means!" The hardening is real, partial (v. 25), and purposeful, and the next two paragraphs will show that it serves a design that ends in mercy. Chapter 11 is where the darkest statements in the unit are placed inside the largest hope in the unit.

One pastoral note before you finish. It would be easy to read this paragraph as a Gentile Christian with satisfaction: the elect obtained, the rest were hardened, and we are on the right side of the sentence. Paul is about to spend fourteen verses making sure nobody does that: "do not become proud, but fear" (11:20). Keep that coming warning in view while you read verse 7.

Application

  • About God He keeps a remnant for himself in every generation, including the ones that look emptiest. The seven thousand were there while Elijah was counting to one.
  • About you Where are you drawing conclusions about God's work from what you can see? Elijah's data was good and his conclusion was wrong.
  • Do Read 1 Kings 19:1-18 today, the whole scene. Note what God gives Elijah before he corrects him (food, sleep, and his presence) and let that shape how you speak to someone who has concluded that everything is lost.

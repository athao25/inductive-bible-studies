Lesson 0007 · Unit 1: Chapters 1:1-3:20

Romans 2:1-11: You Have No Excuse

~25 min · Passage: Romans 2:1-11 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 2:1-11 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: what the first word connects to, who is addressed and how, the charge laid against the judge, the four descriptions of God's kindness and what it is meant to do, and the two destinies laid out in verses 7-10 with the principle stated in verse 11.

Recall first

Observation check

  • "Therefore" (v. 1). Same conclusion as 1:20, same words: "you have no excuse". Chapter 1 ended with the pagan world condemned; chapter 2 opens by applying the identical verdict to the reader who was enjoying it.
  • The address changes to the singular "you" (v. 1). After a chapter of "they", Paul turns and points. "O man, every one of you who judges." The diatribe partner has arrived.
  • The charge is not judging but self-exemption (vv. 1, 3). "You who judge practise the very same things." Paul does not say moral discernment is wrong; he says using it as a shield is fatal.
  • "We know" (v. 2). Common ground: God's judgment "rightly falls", literally, is according to truth. Paul establishes agreement before he presses the knife.
  • Four gifts, one purpose (v. 4). Kindness, forbearance, patience, riches, and "God's kindness is meant to lead you to repentance." Delay is not approval.
  • "Storing up wrath" (v. 5). A treasury image, and a grim inversion of "lay up treasures in heaven". Every unrepentant day makes a deposit.
  • "On the day of wrath" (v. 5). Here the future judgment appears, distinct from the present handing-over of 1:24-28. Two revelations of wrath: now, and then.
  • "He will render to each one according to his works" (v. 6). A quotation of Psalm 62:12 and Proverbs 24:12. Judgment is by works, universally and without exception.
  • A chiasm (vv. 7-10). Good → evil → evil → good, with "the Jew first and also the Greek" attached to both the wrath (v. 9) and the glory (v. 10), matching 1:16.
  • The principle (v. 11). "God shows no partiality", literally, does not receive the face. The judge is not impressed by anybody's credentials.

Interpretation

The trap, and why Paul set it

Therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges. For in passing judgment on another you condemn yourself, because you, the judge, practice the very same things. Romans 2:1, ESV

Chapter 1 was bait. Every religious reader in Rome would have followed the catalogue of pagan depravity with rising agreement: this is exactly the argument Jewish moralists of the period made about the Gentile world, and Paul knew it. Then the sentence turns and the reader is in the dock, condemned by the very standard they had been applying.

The logic is precise. To judge someone is to invoke a standard. To invoke a standard is to accept it for yourself. Therefore every act of moral condemnation is an act of self-condemnation for anyone who has ever crossed the same line, and verse 1 says the judge does "the very same things". Not identical acts, necessarily, but the same catalogue: the list of 1:29-31 included envy, gossip, boasting, and heartlessness precisely so that this sentence would be true of everyone reading it.

Note what Paul does not say. He does not say "who are you to judge?" in the modern sense; chapters 12-16 will be full of moral instruction, and 1 Corinthians 5 tells a church to exercise judgment. The problem is not discernment but the assumption of immunity: the belief that seeing sin clearly in another places you outside its jurisdiction.

Doctrine checkpoint: judgment "according to works" (2:6-10)
Paul is about to prove that no one is justified by works of the law (3:20) and that justification is by faith apart from works (3:28). Here he says God renders to each according to his works, and promises eternal life to "those who by patience in well-doing seek for glory". Is this a contradiction?
  • It is not, because works are evidence rather than currency. Judgment according to works is the consistent teaching of Scripture (Matt 16:27; 2 Cor 5:10; Rev 20:12) and it is not the same as justification by works. The verdict is grounded in Christ and received by faith; the evidence produced at the assize is a life.
  • The standard here is perfection, and that is the point. Paul is establishing the principle by which anyone could be justified by law, so that 3:20 can shut the door on it. The offer of verse 7 is genuine and the qualification is unattainable, which 3:9-20 will demonstrate.
  • A live checkpoint. Careful readers differ on whether verses 7 and 10 describe a real class of people (believers, whose faith works) or a hypothetical standard nobody meets. MacArthur reads it primarily as the divine standard exposing everyone; others, noting 2:26-29 and its language of the Spirit-changed heart, hear a description of the transformed believer whose works testify. Both readings agree on the conclusion Paul draws in 3:20, and both refuse to make works the ground of acceptance.
Whichever way you land, keep the sequence Paul keeps: faith is the root, works are the fruit, and the fruit is what a court can see.

What kindness is for

Or do you presume on the riches of his kindness and forbearance and patience, not knowing that God's kindness is meant to lead you to repentance? Romans 2:4, ESV

Verse 4 exposes a specific misreading of experience. The presumptuous reader looks at a comfortable life and concludes that God is either indifferent or approving. Paul says the comfort has a purpose and the reader has misread it: kindness is designed to lead (the verb is one used of leading an animal by a halter) toward repentance. The blessing was a summons, not a verdict.

This is the counterpart to 1:24-28. There, God's judgment took the form of withdrawal; here, his mercy takes the form of delay. Both can be misread, and both are being misread by somebody in Rome. The unbeliever reads freedom into abandonment; the moralist reads approval into patience. The letter is systematically removing every place to stand except one.

And then verse 5, with its treasury: "you are storing up wrath for yourself". The image is deliberately economic, and it is the exact opposite of the one Jesus uses in Matthew 6:20. Something is accumulating either way. That is a sobering way to read an uneventful decade.

Application

  • About God He shows no partiality (v. 11). No family, denomination, doctrinal precision, or service record buys standing. That is terrifying if you were counting on any of them, and liberating if you were not.
  • About you Take the last thing you were indignant about (the news story, the driver, the person at church) and ask what standard you invoked and whether you keep it. Verse 1 is not asking you to stop discerning. It is asking you to stop exempting yourself.
  • Do Name one specific way God has been kind to you in a season when you were not seeking him. Then answer verse 4 in the way the verse intends: repent of one thing today, concretely, rather than generally.

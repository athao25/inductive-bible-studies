Lesson 0028 · Unit 3: Chapters 6-8

Romans 8:18-25: Creation Groans

~25 min · Passage: Romans 8:18-25 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 8:18-25 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: the comparison Paul makes in verse 18, who is waiting and for what, what creation was subjected to and by whose decision, how many groanings appear in the paragraph, what "firstfruits" means, and the definition of hope given in verses 24-25.

Recall first

Observation check

  • An accountant's verdict (v. 18). "I consider" is logizomai again: the reckoning verb. Paul has done the arithmetic: present sufferings are "not worth comparing" with coming glory.
  • Creation personified (vv. 19-22). It waits with eager longing, was subjected, will be set free, groans and suffers together. Four verbs with the non-human world as subject.
  • "Eager longing" (v. 19). The Greek pictures a head craned forward, watching for something to appear over the horizon.
  • "Subjected… not willingly, but because of him who subjected it" (v. 20). The subjection was God's judicial act at the fall (Gen 3:17-18), not an accident or a defect in the design.
  • "In hope" (v. 20). Even the curse was laid down with an end in view. The subjection was never final.
  • Creation's freedom is tied to ours (v. 21). "The creation itself will be set free… and obtain the freedom of the glory of the children of God." When the children are revealed, the world is repaired.
  • Childbirth, not death throes (v. 22). "Groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now." The image implies something is being born, not merely something ending.
  • Three groans in the chapter. Creation groans (v. 22), we groan (v. 23), the Spirit groans (v. 26).
  • "The firstfruits of the Spirit" (v. 23). The first portion of a harvest, pledging the rest. Not a sample of something different but the beginning of the same thing.
  • Adoption, still future (v. 23). Verse 15 said we have received adoption; verse 23 says we wait for it: "the redemption of our bodies". Already and not yet in two paragraphs.
  • Hope defined (vv. 24-25). "Hope that is seen is not hope." Therefore hope necessarily involves waiting, and waiting involves patience.

Interpretation

A world that is not what it was made to be

For the creation was subjected to futility, not willingly, but because of him who subjected it, in hope that the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to corruption and obtain the freedom of the glory of the children of God. Romans 8:20-21, ESV

Paul does something here that few religious systems attempt: he accounts for the state of the physical world without blaming matter and without denying the damage. Creation is not evil, and it is not fine. It was subjected to futility (the word carries emptiness, frustration, purposelessness) by a deliberate act of God, when Adam's sin brought the ground under curse (Gen 3:17-18). The world is under a sentence passed on account of humanity.

Which means two errors are excluded at once. The world is not a prison to escape, as Greek thought held and as much popular Christianity quietly assumes when it speaks of "going to heaven" as the end of the story. And the world is not fine as it stands, requiring only better management. It is a good thing under a curse, awaiting release.

Notice the linkage in verse 21: creation obtains "the freedom of the glory of the children of God". The fate of the physical world is bound to the fate of God's people. Humanity was made to govern the earth (Gen 1:28); when humanity fell, the domain fell with it; when the children of God are revealed in glory, the domain is restored. That is why the Christian hope is resurrection and new creation, not disembodied escape, and why verse 23 defines our adoption's completion as "the redemption of our bodies", not release from them.

Doctrine checkpoint: already and not yet
Compare 8:15 with 8:23 and you have the tension that structures the whole Christian life.
AlreadyNot yet
Justified (5:1)Saved from wrath at the last day (5:9)
Adopted, crying Abba (8:15)Waiting for adoption, the redemption of the body (8:23)
Dead to sin (6:2)Putting sin to death daily (8:13)
Have the firstfruits of the Spirit (8:23)Await the full harvest (8:23)
Glorified, in God's purpose (8:30)Awaiting the glory to be revealed (8:18)
Get this wrong in either direction and the Christian life distorts. Over-realise it (expect the "not yet" now) and you will conclude that sickness, struggle and grief mean something has gone wrong with your faith. Under-realise it (treat everything as future) and you will live as though nothing has changed. Romans 8 holds both: we have the firstfruits, and we groan.

Groaning is not unbelief

And not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies. Romans 8:23, ESV

Read that sentence slowly, because it demolishes a common piece of bad teaching. Who groans? Not the weak, the immature, or the faithless. Those "who have the firstfruits of the Spirit": the more of the Spirit you have, the more you feel the gap between what is and what will be. Groaning is a symptom of possessing the down payment, not of lacking it.

And the chapter puts believers in remarkable company: creation groans, we groan, and the Spirit himself groans on our behalf (v. 26). Whatever lament is, it is not a failure of faith. It is the sound the whole redeemed order makes while waiting.

Then hope is defined with a logic so simple it is easy to miss: "hope that is seen is not hope. For who hopes for what he sees?" (v. 24). By definition, hope has an invisible object; therefore the Christian life necessarily involves waiting, and waiting necessarily requires patience (v. 25). Anyone who demands to see now what has been promised for later has not understood the category. And the "eager longing" of verse 19 shows what that patience looks like, not resignation, but a head craned toward the horizon.

Finally, hold verse 18 over the whole paragraph. Paul reckons the sufferings of this present time "not worth comparing" with the coming glory. He wrote that having been beaten, stoned, shipwrecked and imprisoned (2 Cor 11:23-28), so it is not the verdict of a comfortable man. It is not a denial that suffering is heavy; it is a statement about the weight on the other side of the scale.

Application

  • About God He subjected creation in hope. Even his judgments are aimed at a restoration, which is why the groan of the world is a labour pain rather than a death rattle.
  • About you If you feel the wrongness of the world more than you used to, that may be the Spirit rather than a failure of joy. Groaning belongs to the people with firstfruits.
  • Do Write down the two or three things you most groan over. Beside each, write what verse 21 promises will finally happen to it. Then pray them as laments, out loud: Scripture permits the form, and this paragraph practically prescribes it.

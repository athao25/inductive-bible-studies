Lesson 0001 · Orientation

How to Study Scripture

~25 min · No passage prerequisite · The one skill every other lesson builds on

Before Romans, the tool. This course uses inductive study: you draw meaning out of the text instead of reading your assumptions into it. Today you learn the three steps and practice them on two verses of Romans. If you have come from the Matthew, John, or Isaiah courses, treat this as your sharpening; the method is the same, but Romans will stretch it in a direction narrative never did. Here you are not tracking a story. You are following an argument.

Why method matters

Scripture is God speaking (2 Tim 3:16). If God has spoken, then the meaning of a passage is not up to us; our job is to hear what he actually said. That conviction, common to MacArthur, White, and Durbin and to the whole Reformed tradition, is why they all preach expositionally: verse by verse, letting the text set the agenda. You are about to do the same thing at a personal scale, 20 minutes at a time, for 16 chapters.

Romans raises the stakes on method. It is the most quoted and the most misquoted book in the New Testament, because its sentences are so memorable that they detach easily from the argument that produced them. "All have sinned" without verse 24. "All things work together for good" without the golden chain that guarantees it. "God gave them up" as a slogan rather than a diagnosis. Method is how you keep the sentences attached to their reasons.

The three steps

StepQuestionDiscipline
1. ObservationWhat does it say?Look long. List facts: who, what, repeated words, contrasts, and above all the connectors.
2. InterpretationWhat does it mean?What did Paul intend the Roman church to understand? Context first; Scripture interprets Scripture.
3. ApplicationWhat must change?What does this reveal about God? What in me must be confessed, believed, obeyed?

The order is not decoration. Skip observation and your interpretation floats free of the text. Skip interpretation and your application applies something the passage never said. The full cheat sheet lives at reference: inductive method; it is your daily template from tomorrow on.

The Romans-specific skill: read the connectors

In a Gospel, the units are scenes. In Romans, the units are inferences. Paul builds by linking claims with small words, and if you skim them you lose the argument entirely. Train your eye on these five:

WordWhat it signalsExample
For (gar)Here is the reason for what I just said1:16 "for it is the power of God"; 1:18 "For the wrath of God is revealed"
ThereforeHere is what follows from everything above5:1; 8:1; 12:1, three of the great hinges of the letter
But / but nowA contrast, often a reversal3:21 "But now the righteousness of God has been manifested"
So that / in order thatPurpose: why God did it3:26; 8:29
Much moreAn argument from the lesser to the greater5:9-10, 15, 17, five times in one paragraph
The paragraph sentence
From tomorrow, add one line to your observation notes: write the paragraph as a single sentence with its connector intact. "Because every mouth is stopped by the law, therefore no one is justified by works." If you cannot write that sentence, you have not yet found the argument, and no amount of commentary reading will substitute for finding it yourself.

The second Romans skill: who is talking?

Paul writes in a style ancient teachers called diatribe: he stages an argument with an imagined objector, voices the objection, and then demolishes it. Watch for the pattern "What shall we say then?" followed by a question, followed by me genoito, "By no means!" (6:1-2; 6:15; 7:7; 9:14). The objection is not Paul's view. It is the view he expects you to raise, and answering it is how he advances.

Miss this and the letter becomes incoherent: you will have Paul recommending sin at 6:1 and denying God's justice at 9:14. Mark the speakers in your notes, even with something as crude as brackets, and half of the letter's supposed contradictions vanish.

One rule for your helps
You own the MacArthur Study Bible in Olive Tree. Excellent tool, wrong first move. Always make your own observations before opening any note or commentary. Helps confirm and correct your reading; they must never replace it. This matters more in Romans than anywhere else, because the commentary tradition here is so strong that it is easy to spend a year reading about Romans without ever reading Romans.

Practice: Romans 1:16-17

Two verses, and not random ones: this is Paul stating the theme of the letter you are about to study. Read them slowly, twice, in your ESV. Then, before scrolling on, write down at least five observations on paper.

For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith, as it is written, "The righteous shall live by faith." Romans 1:16-17, ESV

Observations you might have made

  • It begins with "For". This is not the start of a thought; it is the reason for verse 15, where Paul says he is eager to preach in Rome. The theme verse of Romans is grammatically a footnote to Paul's travel plans, which tells you how completely the gospel governs his life.
  • Three "for"s in two verses. Eager to preach, for I am not ashamed, for it is God's power, for in it God's righteousness is revealed. A chain of reasons, each going deeper.
  • "Not ashamed" is understatement. A crucified provincial as the world's Saviour was, in Roman ears, absurd. Paul answers the sneer of the capital before he arrives in it.
  • "Power" is dynamis. The gospel does not merely describe salvation; it effects it. The message is the instrument.
  • "To everyone who believes" limits and opens at once. Everyone: no ethnic wall. Who believes: no other entrance.
  • "To the Jew first and also to the Greek." A phrase Paul will repeat at 2:9-10. Priority in sequence, not in privilege: the whole of chapters 2-3 and 9-11 will work this out.
  • "The righteousness of God is revealed." Revealed, not achieved by us. Present tense: it happens whenever the gospel is preached.
  • A quotation closes it. Habakkuk 2:4, "as it is written". The theme of the letter is stated in Paul's words and then proved from Scripture, which is exactly how the rest of the book will proceed.

Interpretation: one phrase, one revolution

"The righteousness of God" can mean God's own righteous character, or the righteous standing he gives. Martin Luther read it for years in the first sense only, and hated it: a gospel that revealed God's righteousness sounded like more bad news for a man who had none. When he saw that the phrase also names the righteousness God gives to those who believe, he wrote that he felt himself "altogether born again and to have entered paradise itself through open gates." Read the glossary entry now; the phrase will return at 3:21-26, where Paul shows both senses meeting at the cross, and at 10:3, where he explains what Israel missed.

Notice how much of that interpretation came from observation. The word order, the tense, the quotation, the little "for": the doctrine was in the details, and the details were available to anyone willing to look twice.

Application

  • About God He does not merely require righteousness; he reveals and supplies one. That is the difference between religion and the gospel, and it is the reason this letter exists.
  • About you Where are you tempted to be ashamed of this message? Name the specific room or conversation. Paul's antidote is not nerve but content: he is unashamed because of what the gospel is.
  • Do Set the daily rhythm today. Choose the time, the place, the paper. Tomorrow you meet the whole letter at once; the day after, you begin at 1:1 and do not stop until the doxology.

Lesson 0022 · Unit 3: Chapters 6-8

Romans 6:15-23: Slaves of Righteousness

~25 min · Passage: Romans 6:15-23 (ESV, read first!)

Did you read it?
If you have not yet read Romans 6:15-23 twice in your ESV, stop and do that now. Write at least five observations: how this objection differs from the one in verse 1, the governing image of the paragraph, what verse 17 thanks God for, the phrase Paul apologises for using and why, the fruit each slavery yields, and the careful difference between the two halves of verse 23.

Recall first

Observation check

  • A second, subtler objection (v. 15). Verse 1 asked about continuing in sin as a policy; verse 15 asks about the occasional sin: "are we to sin because we are not under law but under grace?" Same answer: "By no means!"
  • The governing image is slavery (vv. 16-22). The word appears in some form more than a dozen times. Everyone in the passage is a slave; the only question is whose.
  • "You are slaves of the one whom you obey" (v. 16). Obedience reveals ownership. Not a call to choose a master so much as a diagnosis of which one you already have.
  • Thanksgiving in the middle of an argument (v. 17). "But thanks be to God, that you who were once slaves of sin have become obedient from the heart."
  • "The standard of teaching to which you were committed" (v. 17). Literally, a pattern or mould into which you were handed over. The teaching shapes the disciple, not the reverse.
  • "I am speaking in human terms, because of your natural limitations" (v. 19). Paul apologises for the slavery metaphor. He knows it is inadequate for a relationship that is also sonship (8:15).
  • Two trajectories (v. 19). Members presented to impurity and lawlessness "leading to more lawlessness"; members presented to righteousness "leading to sanctification". Both directions accelerate.
  • The freedom that was not freedom (v. 20). "When you were slaves of sin, you were free in regard to righteousness." A bitter irony: the only freedom sin grants is freedom from doing good.
  • The shame test (v. 21). "What fruit were you getting at that time from the things of which you are now ashamed?" The question expects the answer: none.
  • Wages versus free gift (v. 23). Opsonia is a soldier's pay: earned, owed, delivered on time. Set against charisma, a gift. Note the asymmetry: sin pays, God gives.
  • "In Christ Jesus our Lord" (v. 23). The chapter ends where every paragraph in this section ends: not with a principle but with a person.

Interpretation

There is no unowned life

Do you not know that if you present yourselves to anyone as obedient slaves, you are slaves of the one whom you obey, either of sin, which leads to death, or of obedience, which leads to righteousness? Romans 6:16, ESV

The modern assumption is that freedom means autonomy: belonging to no one, subject to nothing. Paul's anthropology has no such category. Every human being is a servant of something, and the evidence is behaviour: "you are slaves of the one whom you obey." What you actually do, habitually, reveals whose you are. That is a considerably more searching test than what you believe about yourself.

This reframes the question of verse 15 entirely. The person asking "may I sin occasionally, since I am under grace?" is treating sin as an activity one may dip into. Paul treats it as a master one serves. There is no such thing as a small submission to a rival lord; each act of obedience deepens the ownership, which is why verse 19 describes both directions as leading somewhere: lawlessness to more lawlessness, righteousness to sanctification. Nothing stays where you put it.

And notice the honest sting of verse 20-21. "When you were slaves of sin, you were free in regard to righteousness": the only liberty on offer was liberty from goodness. Then the question: what did you actually get? Paul does not argue that sin is unpleasant; he asks his readers to remember, and lets the memory do the work. The fruit was shame, and the end was death.

Doctrine checkpoint: sanctification, and how it differs from justification
Verses 19 and 22 introduce a word this letter has not used before: sanctification. Keeping it distinct from justification is one of the most practically important distinctions in theology.
JustificationSanctification
What it isA declaration of righteous standingA work making a person actually holy
Where it happensIn God's court, outside usIn the believer, over time
How it comesReceived by faith alone, complete at onceWorked by the Spirit, with our active participation
DegreesNone: no one is more justified than anotherMany: believers differ, and each grows
Basis of assuranceChrist's work, unchangingEvidence, which fluctuates
Confuse them and one of two errors follows. Fold sanctification into justification and you make your standing depend on your progress, which destroys assurance. Sever them entirely and you get the fiction Paul is refuting here: a justification that leaves the person unchanged. Romans holds them together and distinct, never the same thing, never separable.

Wages and gift

For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord. Romans 6:23, ESV

The most quoted verse in the chapter, and its power is in the asymmetry that most quotations flatten. Read the two halves as strict parallels and you would expect: "the wages of sin is death, but the wages of righteousness is life." Paul refuses. Death is earned; life is given. Nobody receives eternal life as compensation for services rendered, which is the whole letter in one sentence.

Opsonia was the soldier's ration money, paid regularly and reliably. Sin is a scrupulous employer: it always pays, it pays in full, and it pays on time. The word for the other half is charisma, from the same root as grace: a gift, unearned by definition. And the gift is not merely a commodity but a location: "in Christ Jesus our Lord". Eternal life is not a substance handed over at a door; it is life in union with a person.

One more observation, because it will matter in chapter 8. The wages of sin is death, and Christ died. If death is what sin earns, then his death (for those in him) is the wage already paid. That is why 8:1 can say there is now no condemnation: the account has been settled by the one who owed nothing.

Application

  • About God He does not pay wages to his children. Every part of your salvation, from the verdict to the final glory, is in the category of gift.
  • About you Apply verse 16's test honestly: over the past month, what did your habits say you belong to? Not your convictions: your habits.
  • Do Begin memory verse 6 today (6:23), keeping "For" and the whole second half. Then answer verse 21 in writing: what fruit did you actually get from the thing you are now ashamed of?

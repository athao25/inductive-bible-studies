Paul places these side by side and never offers a mechanism for combining them. Notice what he does instead: he prays (10:1), he preaches (10:14-15), he warns (11:20-22), and he worships (11:33-36). Those four responses are the unit's real conclusion, and any doctrine of election that produces fewer than four of them has been abstracted from its text.

What this unit is for

It is easy to treat Romans 9-11 as a doctrinal battleground and miss what Paul is doing with it. He wrote these chapters for a divided congregation, and every hard sentence in them is aimed at humility. If salvation is by sovereign mercy, then the Gentile majority in Rome has nothing to boast of and no grounds for looking down on Jewish believers or on unbelieving Israel. And if God's promises are irrevocable, then the anxious believer (Jew or Gentile) can rest.

Which is why the unit ends by handing you straight into chapter 12. "I appeal to you therefore, brothers, by the mercies of God": the mercies of chapters 9-11, and of everything before them. The next four chapters are about ordinary life: bodies, gifts, enemies, taxes, food, days, and people you find difficult. Paul thinks eleven chapters of doctrine was the necessary preparation for that. The largest "therefore" in the Bible is about to arrive.

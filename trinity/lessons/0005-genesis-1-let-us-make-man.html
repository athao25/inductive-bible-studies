Genesis 1: Let Us Make Man

~25 min · Passage: Genesis 1:1-3, 26-28 (ESV, read first!)

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on these verses include:

  • One Creator, no rivals (v. 1). "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth." No battle with other gods, no pre-existing matter he must negotiate with. The chapter is monotheism in narrative form.
  • The Spirit of God is there (v. 2). "The Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters." Present and active at creation, before anything is formed.
  • Creation happens by speech (v. 3). "And God said, 'Let there be light.'" God's word goes out and carries his creative power. Psalm 33:6 summarizes the chapter: "By the word of the LORD the heavens were made, and by the breath of his mouth all their host." Word and breath: file that.
  • "Let us make man in our image" (v. 26). Plural. After a chapter of "let there be", the climactic creature is introduced with deliberation, and the deliberation is "us" and "our".
  • The plural becomes singular immediately (v. 27). "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him." Us, our; then he, his, three times. Whatever verse 26 means, the author saw no conflict with one God creating.
  • Image and likeness ground dominion and blessing (vv. 26-28). Mankind, male and female together, images God and is blessed and commissioned by him.

Interpretation

What does "let us" mean, honestly?

Then God said, "Let us make man in our image, after our likeness." Genesis 1:26, ESV

Weigh the options like a Berean, because this verse is often claimed too quickly. A plural of majesty? Kings speak that way in English, but Hebrew has no clear parallel of a speaker using "we" this way of himself. God addressing the angels? The next verse refuses it: man is made in God's image, not in an angelic committee's, and Scripture never makes angels co-creators; Isaiah 44:24 says God "alone stretched out the heavens... by myself". Self-deliberation, God speaking to his own fullness, comes closest, and notice what a strange thing it is for strict solitary oneness: within the one God there is speech, counsel, an "us". The honest conclusion is the one lesson 3 practiced: this verse does not teach the Trinity, and a careful reader will not claim it does. But it is exactly the kind of thing you would expect on the first page if the God of the Shema were eternally more than solitary, and the full revelation shows why the seed was planted. Moses could not have unpacked it; John could: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. All things were made through him" (John 1:1-3). The New Testament reads Genesis 1 and finds the Word who was spoken and the Spirit who hovered, both there, both God, both creating.

The pattern of the seed

This is the Old Testament's consistent way with the persons, and it is worth naming before the unit closes. The Spirit of God appears throughout, empowering, filling, carrying the prophets along, yet is never called a mere force. The word of the LORD comes to people, creates, heals, accomplishes, and in places acts with a strange independence (Ps 107:20; Isa 55:11). The Angel of the LORD speaks as God, receives worship, bears the name (Gen 22, Ex 3, Judg 13), yet is also distinguished from the LORD. Wisdom is beside God at creation "like a master workman" (Prov 8:30). None of these, alone, proves three persons; together they are a page full of loose threads that strict solitary oneness cannot tie off. Progressive revelation means the Old Testament is not silent about the persons; it is pregnant with them. The unit's conclusion stands untouched: one God. The question the seed plants is what that oneness contains, and the New Testament, beginning at a Jordan riverbank and a Galilean wedding, answers it.

Application

  • About God The God who said "let us make man" was not lonely; creation flows from fullness, not need. You exist because triune love overflowed, not because God lacked company.
  • About you You bear the image of this God, made for fellowship, made male and female in community, because the God you image has never been solitary.

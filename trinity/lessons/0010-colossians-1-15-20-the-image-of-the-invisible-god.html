Colossians 1:15-20: The Image of the Invisible God

Observation check

Compare your list. Strong observations on this passage include:

  • "The image of the invisible God" (v. 15). Not a picture of God but the visible expression of him: to see the Son is to see what God is (cf. John 14:9; Heb 1:3).
  • "Firstborn of all creation" (v. 15). Hold this phrase; the interpretation section weighs it, because everything turns on whether "firstborn" means first-created or preeminent heir.
  • "For" (v. 16). The reason he is firstborn: "for by him all things were created". Verse 16 is the argument for verse 15, and it is an argument from creating, not from being created.
  • The categories are total (v. 16). Heaven and earth, visible and invisible, thrones, dominions, rulers, authorities. Paul is writing to a church tempted by angel-veneration (2:18), so he sweeps every angelic rank into the "created by him" column.
  • Three prepositions (v. 16). "By him... through him and for him." Creation's origin, means, and goal. "For him" is startling: in Romans 11:36 the same "from him and through him and to him" belongs to God.
  • "He is before all things" (v. 17). Present tense "is", echoing the timeless being-language you met in John.
  • "In him all things hold together" (v. 17). Not a watchmaker's world: the universe's coherence, this second, is a person's continuing work.
  • Head, beginning, firstborn from the dead (v. 18). The hymn's second stanza moves from cosmos to church: "that in everything he might be preeminent". Paul's own commentary on what "firstborn" is for.
  • "All the fullness of God was pleased to dwell" in him (v. 19). Made explicit at 2:9: "in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily". Fullness, not a portion; bodily, not temporarily.

Interpretation

Does "firstborn of all creation" make the Son a creature?

He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation. For by him all things were created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible... Colossians 1:15-16, ESV

This is history's most contested verse in this debate; the Arians built on it, and Jehovah's Witnesses still do, reading "firstborn" as "first creature made". Three observations settle it, all from the text rather than from a lexicon. First, "firstborn" in Scripture is a rank, not a birth-order report: Israel is God's "firstborn son" (Ex 4:22) without being the oldest nation, and David, the youngest of eight, is appointed "the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth" (Ps 89:27). The firstborn is the heir, the preeminent one, which is exactly how verse 18 glosses it: "that in everything he might be preeminent". Second, the logic of "for": Paul does not say the Son is firstborn because he was created first, but because by him all things were created. Creators are not members of the category they create. Third, if Paul had meant "created first", Greek had a word for it, protoktistos, "first-created"; he chose prototokos, "firstborn", and then spent two verses placing the Son on the Creator's side of the Creator-creature line that Unit 1 drew. The Son is the firstborn over all creation as its maker and heir, not the firstborn member of it.

The fullness, and why it mattered in Colossae

The Colossian temptation was not atheism but supplementation: Christ plus ascetic rules, plus visionary experiences, plus the veneration of angelic powers (2:16-23). Paul's answer is not to scold but to enlarge: the one who redeemed you (1:13-14) is the one in whom the entire fullness of deity dwells bodily, who made every power you are tempted to appease, sustains the atoms of the universe at this moment, and reconciled you "by the blood of his cross" (1:20). Notice how the hymn welds creation and redemption into one figure: the same "him" who made the thrones and dominions is the head of the church and the firstborn from the dead. That weld is the doctrine of the Trinity doing pastoral work: if the Son were anything less than fully God, then somewhere above him would stand a power worth adding to him, and assurance would leak out through the gap. Because he is the fullness, "you have been filled in him" (2:10), and there is nothing left over to seek elsewhere. Sufficiency is a corollary of deity.

Application

  • About God The invisible God has an image, and the image has a face. What God is like is no longer a speculation; it is a person you can read four Gospels about.
  • About you "In him all things hold together" includes the things you are anxious about this week. The hands sustaining the cosmos are the hands pierced for your reconciliation (1:20).
  • Do Write out the Creator-creature line from Unit 1 and place Colossians 1:16-17 on it. Then name your own Colossian temptation: what are you tempted to add to Christ as though he were not enough? Review Philippians 2:10-11 aloud.
